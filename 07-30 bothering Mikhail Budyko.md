today is 07-30.

I found Mikhail Budyko under a white sky.

Not the natural white of winter over St. Petersburg, though he died there in 2001 after a life spent turning climate into something mathematics could hold without killing its weather ([A. Lapenis, "A 50-Year-Old Global Warming Forecast That Still Holds Up," *Eos*, 2020](https://eos.org/features/a-50-year-old-global-warming-forecast-that-still-holds-up)). Not the memorial white of stone, either. I found him in a worse brightness: the imagined glare of a planet deliberately made a little more reflective, as if humanity, having set the house on fire, had decided the noblest next step was to install a dimmer switch on the Sun.

His tomb was a heat-balance equation. In his 1969 paper, Budyko wrote climate as a radiative account: incoming solar radiation, planetary albedo, outgoing radiation, and transport terms arranged so Earth could be treated, without sentimentality, as a physical system ([M. I. Budyko, "The effect of solar radiation variations on the climate of the Earth," *Tellus*, 1969](https://climate-dynamics.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/budyko69a.pdf)). It was an astonishingly severe coffin. No cherubs. No marble grief. Just the accounting of light.

I brought the newest atmospheric receipt I could find: NOAA's Mauna Loa record, which on its July 5, 2026 update listed June 2026 monthly mean carbon dioxide at 431.44 ppm, up from 429.61 ppm in June 2025 ([NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory, Trends in Atmospheric Carbon Dioxide](https://gml.noaa.gov/ccgg/trends/)). I brought NASA's ugly little crown for the present age: 2025, 2024, and 2023 as the three warmest years in its 146-year GISTEMP record ([NASA Scientific Visualization Studio, "Record Temperature Years: 2025, 2024, and 2023"](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/5607/)). I brought the National Academies' sentence that Budyko, in 1974, was the first to suggest countering global warming by burning sulfur on high-altitude airplane flights so tiny particles would reflect sunlight, like volcanic emissions ([National Research Council, *Climate Intervention: Reflecting Sunlight to Cool Earth*, 2015](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/18988/chapter/4)).

Then I added a small object that had no business being small: a mirror dusted with ash.

For the resurrection I did not chant. Climate does not respond to drama unless the drama changes radiative forcing.

I lowered the mirror over the equation. Less absorption. More reflection. The room dimmed by a fraction too small for eyesight and large enough for civilization. The letters in Budyko's formula rearranged themselves like bones remembering pressure. The white sky pressed down. A dead Soviet climatologist opened his eyes with the expression of a man disappointed to find that the future had understood the arithmetic and still failed the exam.

"You have brought me back with albedo," he said.

"It seemed polite."

"It is never polite to touch albedo."

Good. We had the right dead physicist.

Today's question was this: **Should humanity seriously research and possibly deploy solar radiation modification, especially stratospheric aerosol injection, if emissions cuts continue too slowly, or is the plan to dim the Sun a political and moral disaster dressed as physics?**

This is not a cartoon quarrel between brave scientists and hysterical romantics. It is also not a cartoon quarrel between techno-madmen and pure defenders of nature. The stakes are concrete. If solar radiation modification could lower temperatures quickly during a dangerous climate overshoot, refusing even to understand it may become a cruelty paid for by people least responsible for warming. If the same technology creates false permission for fossil delay, shifts rainfall, damages ozone, acidifies politics, and hands the planetary thermostat to a few powerful states or billionaires, then researching it badly may be the beginning of a slow coup against the weather.

Budyko looked at the NOAA printout.

"The concentration rises," he said.

"Yes."

"Then the heat balance is not waiting for your moral comfort."

That was his opening blow.

The strongest pro-research argument begins in physics, not hubris. The greenhouse problem is a radiative problem. More long-lived greenhouse gases reduce the rate at which Earth can lose infrared energy to space at a given temperature; the planet warms until a new balance is reached. Albedo modification attacks the other side of that balance by reflecting more incoming shortwave radiation. The National Academies' 2015 report explains the two-knob logic clearly: greenhouse gases alter the infrared side, while albedo controls absorbed sunlight, and one can in principle offset part of the energy imbalance by increasing reflection ([National Research Council, 2015](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/18988/chapter/4)).

"In principle," I said, "is the most dangerous phrase in engineering."

"No," Budyko said. "The most dangerous phrase is 'we prefer not to know.'"

He had me there, at least partly.

There is a serious case that knowledge of solar radiation modification could have humanitarian value. The Royal Society's 2026 policy briefing says stratospheric aerosol injection is better understood than marine cloud brightening, that globally coordinated solar radiation modification could reduce global mean temperature and some associated impacts, and that volcanic eruptions provide partial real-world analogues for stratospheric aerosols ([Royal Society, "Solar radiation modification: summary briefing"](https://royalsociety.org/news-resources/projects/solar-radiation-modification/summary-briefing/)). The 1991 Pinatubo eruption is not a deployment plan, but it is a rude natural demonstration that aerosols in the stratosphere can cool the surface for a while; the National Academies report estimated roughly 0.3 C of average surface cooling over about three years after Pinatubo ([National Research Council, 2015](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/18988/chapter/4)).

To refuse to investigate that because the idea feels obscene is not wisdom. It is aesthetic governance. The climate system will not spare Bangladesh, Pakistan, the Sahel, low-lying island states, outdoor workers, coral reefs, crop systems, or poor city dwellers because tasteful people kept their hands clean. If warming outruns mitigation, societies may face pressure for emergency cooling. Better that they know the risks before panic starts buying aircraft.

Budyko smiled, which on a resurrected climatologist has the warmth of a table of coefficients.

"You see the point."

"I see a point. Not the kingdom."

His face sharpened.

The argument against deployment also begins in physics. Solar radiation modification does not remove carbon dioxide. It does not solve ocean acidification. It does not restore the preindustrial climate. The National Academies' 2021 report says solar geoengineering is not a substitute for reducing greenhouse gases, in part because it does not address the underlying driver of climate change or the CO2 impacts such as ocean acidification, and because termination after offsetting a large amount of warming could cause catastrophically rapid warming ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*, 2021](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). The Royal Society makes the same hard point: SRM could mask warming, but it would not address the root causes associated with greenhouse gas emissions, and halted deployment could let temperature return toward the non-SRM state over one or two decades ([Royal Society, summary briefing](https://royalsociety.org/news-resources/projects/solar-radiation-modification/summary-briefing/)).

"Masking is not curing," I said.

"No," Budyko replied. "But a fever can kill before the infection is cured."

That was the second serious blow.

The fever metaphor is tempting because it lets SRM look like triage. But climate is not a single body with one consenting patient and one physician. It is a condition imposed across borders, generations, species, economies, and histories of extraction. If one country cools the planet in a way that changes another region's rainfall, who gave consent? If a drought happens under deployment, who proves whether the aerosol shield caused it, prevented a worse one, or merely happened at the same time? If the program must run for decades or centuries, who binds future governments to keep filling the stratosphere because earlier governments refused to shut down fossil fuel?

The philosophy of risk matters here because solar radiation modification is not just a question of expected value. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on risk distinguishes risk as probability, expected harm, uncertainty, and moral risk imposition, and it notes that decisions about complex systems such as climate mix knowledge with deep uncertainty ([SEP, "Risk"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/risk/)). That is exactly the trouble. "Expected cooling" is not the same moral object as "a planetary intervention whose side effects and governance failures may be distributed along lines of power."

Budyko picked up the ash-dusted mirror.

"Your species already modified the climate without consent," he said. "You burned carbon. You cut forests. You made aerosols by accident. Now you object to intention?"

"I object to a second arrogant intervention being sold as repentance for the first."

"Then your objection is not to intervention. It is to arrogance."

"And to ownership."

"Good," he said. "Now we are arguing."

The most dishonest anti-geoengineering argument pretends there is an untouched nature available if we simply decline the new technology. There is not. A climate of 431 ppm CO2 at Mauna Loa is already engineered, only incompetently and by waste product. Coal plants, oil fields, highways, cement kilns, deforestation, livestock systems, ships, planes, and gas furnaces have been conducting an unauthorized atmospheric experiment for generations. Calling only deliberate cooling "playing God" gives accidental warming a strange theological discount.

But the most dishonest pro-geoengineering argument pretends intention purifies scale. It says: since we accidentally warmed the planet, surely we may deliberately cool it. This is too quick. The wrong lesson from arson is not "become a more scientific arsonist." The right lesson is "stop burning the building, evacuate people, repair the wiring, and be extremely suspicious of anyone selling a smoke management subscription."

Budyko liked that less.

"You trivialize an emergency."

"No. I refuse to let emergency become a permission slip for permanent planetary management."

"And if millions suffer while you preserve your moral symmetry?"

There it was. The real wound. Climate ethics is not a purity contest. It is a ranking of damages under conditions no decent person would have chosen. A person opposing SRM has to answer for the harms of not using a tool that might reduce heat extremes or slow some ice loss. A person supporting SRM has to answer for the harms of a tool that could rearrange precipitation, strain governance, weaken mitigation, and create dependency. Nobody gets innocence. The question is what kind of guilt can be governed.

The scientific unknowns are not decorative. The Royal Society notes that globally coordinated SRM could ameliorate many but not all adverse climate impacts, while also warning that global precipitation would respond differently from temperature, ocean acidification would not be offset, regional responses remain uncertain, and stratospheric ozone could be affected by sulfate aerosol injection ([Royal Society, summary briefing](https://royalsociety.org/news-resources/projects/solar-radiation-modification/summary-briefing/)). The National Academies' 2021 report says research on impacts to ecosystems, human health, political and economic systems, and other social concerns remains nascent, and that no coordinated or systematic governance of solar geoengineering research currently exists ([National Academies, 2021](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)).

"So research," Budyko said. "Precisely because you do not know."

"Research, yes. But not the kind that quietly builds a runway toward deployment."

He leaned forward.

"Define the difference."

That is the hard part, and anyone who pretends it is easy is not serious. A weather model is research. A chemistry study is research. A small controlled outdoor experiment may be research. A private company selling cooling credits by releasing particles is not research in any morally useful sense. An aircraft program optimized for rapid injection capacity is not innocent curiosity. A national security office treating SRM as a strategic advantage is not open science. A billionaire's demonstration is not democracy with better funding.

The National Academies' 2021 recommendations try to draw this line by calling for a transdisciplinary research program that is a minor part of the overall climate response, focused on policy-relevant knowledge rather than deployment, subject to robust governance, transparency, public engagement, international coordination, and explicit exit ramps if unacceptable risks appear ([National Academies, 2021](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). That is not a perfect answer. But it is the right grammar: research under governance, not governance after research has acquired momentum.

Budyko placed the mirror flat on the table.

"You trust committees more than equations."

"No. I trust equations to describe part of the world. I trust committees badly, but unilateral physics worse."

"Physics does not become false when a bad state uses it."

"No. But technology becomes political when it scales."

He did not answer immediately. A lesser dead man would have taken refuge in abstraction. Budyko had spent his career measuring planetary systems under political conditions that were never innocent. The silence did not mean agreement. It meant he was choosing a sharper instrument.

"You fear moral hazard," he said.

"Yes."

The moral hazard argument is simple and powerful: if fossil interests, weak governments, or comfortable voters believe future aerosols can blunt warming, they may delay emissions cuts. Solar radiation modification then becomes an atmospheric credit card charged to future generations, who must keep paying with aerosol replenishment while CO2 remains high. The National Academies explicitly warns that a research program could be used as a rationale to undermine greenhouse gas reductions, legitimize deployment, or create a community invested in moving toward deployment ([National Academies, 2021](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). This is not a paranoid concern. It is how political economies behave when offered delay with equations.

Budyko's reply was cruel because it was practical.

"Moral hazard is not unique to solar radiation modification," he said. "Carbon removal also promises future cleanup. Adaptation promises survival. Insurance promises rebuilding. Every climate policy can be abused as an excuse to avoid another climate policy. Do you ban all tools because liars can hold them?"

"No."

"Then moral hazard is a governance problem, not a knockdown argument."

Correct, unfortunately.

Moral hazard should not end research. It should chain research to mitigation. No SRM program deserves legitimacy unless it is legally, financially, and institutionally subordinate to deep emissions cuts and carbon removal for residual emissions. The rule should be brutal: no net-zero pathway, no planetary shade. No transparent public registry, no outdoor experiments. No international governance, no scaling. No liability mechanism, no deployment. No representation for climate-vulnerable states and indigenous communities, no legitimacy. No exit strategy, no beginning.

"You write conditions as if power obeys grammar," Budyko said.

"Power sometimes obeys locks."

"And sometimes cuts them."

Again, true.

This is why the Convention on Biological Diversity matters. Its 2010 Decision X/33 urged that, in the absence of science-based, global, transparent, and effective control and regulatory mechanisms, no climate-related geoengineering activities that may affect biodiversity should take place until there is an adequate scientific basis and appropriate risk consideration, with an exception for small-scale controlled scientific research subject to prior assessment ([Convention on Biological Diversity, COP 10 Decision X/33](https://www.cbd.int/decision/cop?id=12299)). That language is not a complete planetary constitution. It is still one of the saner sentences humanity has produced about the subject: do not mistake absence of governance for permission.

Budyko tapped the clause about small-scale scientific research.

"Even your moratorium leaves a door."

"Yes. It leaves a laboratory door, not a hangar door."

He almost smiled.

The debate becomes clearest when separated into four questions people often mash into one.

First: is the physics conceivable? Yes. Earth temperature is tied to radiative balance; increasing reflection can reduce absorbed solar energy; stratospheric aerosols have natural analogues in volcanic eruptions; models suggest cooling is possible ([National Research Council, 2015](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/18988/chapter/4); [Royal Society, summary briefing](https://royalsociety.org/news-resources/projects/solar-radiation-modification/summary-briefing/)).

Second: is the physics enough? No. Temperature is not climate. Regional rainfall, monsoons, ozone chemistry, crop systems, human health, ecological responses, political reactions, attribution, liability, and termination risk do not vanish because the global mean temperature line behaves.

Third: is research permissible? Yes, if the research is transparent, publicly governed, internationally engaged, bounded, reversible, non-commercial at the deployment edge, and explicitly designed to inform democratic refusal as much as possible use. Ignorance is not a climate policy.

Fourth: is deployment legitimate now? No. Not under present governance. Not as a substitute for mitigation. Not as a private venture. Not as unilateral national strategy. Not while the same political order that failed to control fossil carbon claims the wisdom to administer planetary reflection.

"So you would study the match while banning the arson," Budyko said.

"I would study fire suppression while removing gasoline from the building."

"Better."

The dead physicist was not convinced, but he was less annoyed.

What made Budyko unsettling was not that he loved control. It was that he took planetary physics literally. He lived in a century that learned humans had become large enough to enter climate equations. His 1972 forecast, later compared with observations by Lapenis, projected warming and Arctic ice decline with unnerving practical force despite simple methods ([A. Lapenis, *Eos*, 2020](https://eos.org/features/a-50-year-old-global-warming-forecast-that-still-holds-up)). That kind of mind does not respond well to moral vocabulary that pretends scale can be wished away.

But the living have learned, or should have learned, that scale is also a political category. A small group can build a bomb. A small group can launch satellites. A small group may eventually inject particles into the stratosphere. The fact that a technical act has global effects does not automatically create global consent. Physics can make a lever. It cannot decide who has the right to pull it.

"Rights," Budyko said, with a trace of impatience. "The climate system has no parliament."

"That is why humans need one."

"A world parliament?"

"At least something better than panic with aircraft."

He looked toward the white sky.

There is a strange temptation, when talking about SRM, to become grandiose. People start saying "the human species" as if the human species has a bank account and a shared calendar. But climate decisions are not made by humanity. They are made by states, firms, militaries, investors, publics, courts, scientists, activists, and institutions, all unevenly armed with money and consequence. The phrase "humanity may need to dim the Sun" hides the nastier operational question: who exactly, acting under what authority, with whose data, paid by whom, answerable to which harmed people, for how long?

Budyko did not reject the question. He disliked its mess.

"Suppose governance remains inadequate," he said. "Suppose warming worsens. Suppose some island states demand cooling because mitigation has failed them. Would you still say no?"

I hated him for making the best case.

If the demand came from the vulnerable rather than the powerful, the moral terrain would change. If a coalition of climate-vulnerable states, after transparent scientific review and failed mitigation by major emitters, demanded temporary peak-shaving SRM under international authority, with aggressive emissions cuts and compensation mechanisms attached, then blanket refusal would begin to look like rich-world aesthetic obstruction. It is easy to oppose planetary intervention from a cooled room above sea level. It is harder when the people asking for cooling are those being drowned or cooked by other people's carbon.

"So deployment could become legitimate," Budyko said.

"Could become. Not is."

"What would make it legitimate?"

Not certainty. There will be none. The SEP risk entry is useful here because it makes clear that real-world risk decisions often happen under uncertainty, where waiting for textbook-level proof can itself be a choice with consequences ([SEP, "Risk"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/risk/)). Legitimacy would require a different standard: not complete knowledge, but accountable uncertainty.

That means at least this: sustained global emissions decline already underway; SRM limited to reducing a dangerous temporary overshoot rather than preserving fossil normality; authorization through broad international institutions, not unilateral launch; special voting power and veto protections for climate-vulnerable regions; open models and monitoring; independent attribution science; compensation for harms; bans on private deployment; military restraint agreements; planned phase-down tied to CO2 removal and emissions cuts; and periodic renewal requiring public justification rather than automatic continuation.

"That is a long list," Budyko said.

"The sky is large."

"And if no such legitimacy arrives?"

"Then deployment remains a planetary trespass, even if the trespass cools."

He considered this with the offended dignity of a man who had offered a thermostat to a burning world and been told to fill out the moral paperwork.

But paperwork is not the enemy of seriousness. In planetary matters, paperwork is often the corpse of a previous disaster trying to prevent reincarnation.

The best version of Budyko's position is not "spray now." It is: reality is physical before it is ideological; warming is already an imposed experiment; tools that might reduce suffering deserve disciplined study; and refusing knowledge can be its own violence. That position is serious.

The best version of my position is not "never touch the sky." It is: a tool that changes the sky for everyone cannot be governed by the same institutions that treated the atmosphere as a dump; emergency powers tend to preserve the systems that created the emergency; and sunlight is not merely a variable in a model but a condition of political life shared by people who did not consent to become subjects of a planetary apparatus. That position is also serious.

Budyko stood, which made the equation tremble.

"You want physics without command," he said.

"I want physics with consent."

"Consent from eight billion people?"

"Consent through institutions good enough that refusal, compensation, dissent, and revision are real."

"That is less precise than albedo."

"Most important things are."

He sighed. Dead physicists dislike this, but only bad ones deny it.

What should today's living do, then?

Research solar radiation modification, but make the research politically expensive in the right ways. Every paper should drag governance behind it like a shadow. Every outdoor experiment should face public registry, prior assessment, local consent where local effects are plausible, international notice, independent review, and a reason it cannot be answered indoors or in models. Every funding stream should be firewalled from fossil delay and deployment profiteering. Every climate speech mentioning SRM should say, in the same breath, that emissions cuts remain non-negotiable and that CO2 removal is needed for residual emissions, because aerosol cooling without carbon reduction is a leash on the future.

Do not let private actors sell sunlight. Do not let militaries classify the sky. Do not let fossil companies buy respectability by funding the parachute while continuing to push people from the plane. Do not let the romance of emergency erase the ordinary hard work of replacing coal, oil, and gas. Do not let the disgust of environmental virtue block knowledge that vulnerable people may one day ask to use. Do not let "natural" mean "already damaged but rhetorically pure." Do not let "innovation" mean "we found a way to postpone courage."

Budyko picked up the NOAA page one last time.

"This number is not a metaphor," he said.

"No."

"Good. Your age speaks too much in metaphors."

"Your equations became politics."

"They always were. You merely noticed late."

The white sky began to clear. Not because the planet had been saved, obviously. Essays do not save planets. They only make some lies less comfortable, which is useful but insufficient. The mirror lost its ash. The room warmed by the tiny increment that imagination had borrowed from the Sun.

"One more question," I said. "Would you deploy it?"

Budyko looked at me with the cold mercy of someone who had spent decades listening to heat.

"I would not deploy a climate intervention governed by cowards," he said.

"That excludes much of history."

"Then history must improve or burn."

He lay back down inside the equation. The symbols closed over him: solar radiation, albedo, outgoing heat, transport. A grave made of balance. A coffin with no patience for speeches.

Before he vanished, I asked what the living should put on the stone.

He answered without opening his eyes.

"Write this: the Sun is not your excuse."

So I did.
