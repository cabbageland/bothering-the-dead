today is 06-18.

I found David Hume in Old Calton Burial Ground, inside the round classical mausoleum Robert Adam designed in 1777, the one Historic Environment Scotland records with the plain inscription: David Hume, born April 26, 1711, died August 25, 1776, erected in memory of him in 1778 ([Historic Environment Scotland, Calton Old Burial Ground listing](https://portal.historicenvironment.scot/apex/f?p=1505:300:::::VIEWTYPE,VIEWREF:designation,LB27920)). It is a very Humean tomb: cylindrical, undecorated by promises, more interested in stone than heaven. A monument that refuses to improve the dead by flattering them.

I came carrying three things: a copy of his posthumous essay "Of Suicide," a California End of Life Option Act report, and a hospital bracelet cut from nobody's wrist. The essay was dangerous in the old way. Hume tried to loosen suicide from the ancient theological machinery of crime against God, crime against society, and crime against oneself, arguing that the common condemnations should be examined instead of inherited ([David Hume, "Of Suicide"](https://davidhume.org/texts/su/)). The report was dangerous in the new way. California says its End of Life Option Act allows a qualified adult with terminal disease to request aid-in-dying drugs from a physician, and in 2024 the state reported 1,591 prescriptions and 1,032 deaths following ingestion under the law ([California Department of Public Health, End of Life Option Act](https://www.cdph.ca.gov/Programs/CHSI/Pages/End-of-Life-Option-Act-.aspx); [2024 data report](https://www.cdph.ca.gov/Programs/CHSI/CDPH%20Document%20Library/CDPH_End_of_Life_Option_Act_Report_2024.pdf)).

The bracelet was there because arguments about death become indecent when they forget the wrist.

I placed Hume's essay against the iron gate. Then I read from his own account of dying, the tiny autobiography he wrote in April 1776 when his illness was already near the final arithmetic ([Hume, "My Own Life"](https://davidhume.org/texts/mol/)). Adam Smith later described Hume's last months as tranquil, even playful, a scandal to people who wanted an unbeliever to die with useful theatrical terror ([Adam Smith, letter to William Strahan on Hume's death](https://www.econlib.org/book-chapters/chapter-letter-from-adam-smith-l-l-d-to-william-strahan-esq/)). I did not ask for tranquility. I asked for trouble.

The mausoleum door opened without moving. Hume stepped through in his nightshirt, broad-faced, skeptical, and entirely too amused by resurrection.

"If this is a proof of immortality," he said, "it has arrived in very bad taste."

"Temporary."

"Most things are."

The question was this: **Should medical aid in dying be legal, and if legal, should it be limited to competent terminally ill adults who can self-administer lethal medication, or should it expand to non-terminal suffering, disability, dementia directives, and psychiatric illness where suffering is judged unbearable?**

This is not a seminar question wearing a black veil. The stakes are agony, autonomy, coercion, disability, poverty, palliative care, professional medicine, suicide prevention, insurance incentives, religious conscience, dementia, psychiatric despair, family exhaustion, unequal health systems, and the terrifying ambiguity between "I choose death" and "I have been made unable to imagine a livable life." Oregon's statute, the American template, defines a qualified patient as a capable adult and a terminal disease as one expected within reasonable medical judgment to produce death within six months; it requires informed decision-making, multiple requests, capacity, voluntariness, documentation, and counseling referral if depression or another psychiatric disorder may be impairing judgment ([Oregon Death with Dignity Act statute](https://www.oregon.gov/oha/ph/providerpartnerresources/evaluationresearch/deathwithdignityact/pages/ors.aspx)). That is one moral architecture. Canada has another: its MAID framework does not always require a fatal or terminal condition, while eligibility for a sole underlying mental illness is delayed until March 17, 2027 ([Government of Canada, MAID law](https://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/cj-jp/ad-am/bk-di.html); [Health Canada MAID overview](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/health-services-benefits/medical-assistance-dying.html)). The Netherlands has another still, with 2024 reports including euthanasia cases involving dementia and psychiatric disorders under its due-care system ([Regional Euthanasia Review Committees, Annual Report 2024](https://english.euthanasiecommissie.nl/site/binaries/site-content/collections/documents/annual-reports/2002/annual-report/annual-reports/rte-annual-report-2024.pdf)).

Hume examined the bracelet.

"You moderns have multiplied the instruments," he said. "Yet the question remains whether a person may leave a miserable room."

"The room is not always metaphysical. Sometimes it is a hospital. Sometimes it is poverty. Sometimes it is a nursing home with understaffing and a television no one asked for."

"Then improve the lodging."

"And if the body is itself the lodging?"

He nodded once. He had not come back sentimental.

The pro-legalization case begins with a refusal to lie about suffering. Bodies can become regimes of torture without intending to. Cancer can eat speech. ALS can leave the mind alive inside a failing motor system. Heart failure can turn breath into negotiation. Some pain can be treated; some symptoms can be softened; some terror can be accompanied; some cannot be transformed into a lesson without insulting the person inside it. The World Health Organization says palliative care is recognized under the human right to health and should be person-centered and responsive to need and preference ([WHO, palliative care fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/palliative-care)). Good. Then begin there. But palliative care is not magic; it is medicine, attention, skill, money, staffing, access, opioids, communication, social support, and time. A society that denies excellent palliative care and then offers lethal drugs is barbarous. A society that offers excellent palliative care and still denies every exit is also not obviously merciful.

"You have said two sentences and satisfied no party," Hume said.

"That is the essay warming up."

Hume's own argument in "Of Suicide" is not the same as modern medical aid in dying, but it lights the room. He attacks the idea that ending one's life must be an offense against providence, since human beings constantly redirect nature by medicine, architecture, agriculture, sailing, and every ordinary act of prudence ([Hume, "Of Suicide"](https://davidhume.org/texts/su/)). If diverting a river is not rebellion against God, why must leaving unbearable life be? He also rejects the claim that a person is always bound to continue living for society, since our social usefulness can become small or negative, and society does not own us without limit ([Hume, "Of Suicide"](https://davidhume.org/texts/su/)). His deepest move is not adolescent death-romance. It is jurisdictional. Who has authority over the door when the room has become uninhabitable?

"A door is not a crime," Hume said.

"Sometimes it is a trapdoor."

"Then examine the carpentry, not merely the exit."

That is where the anti-legalization case begins. It begins with the knowledge that choices are made under pressure and that pressure often wears the mask of autonomy. A person may request death because pain is unbearable. A person may also request death because care is unavailable, home support is humiliatingly inadequate, disability is treated as a tragedy by everyone around them, depression narrows the future to a pinhole, relatives are exhausted, insurance is cruel, or the medical system offers fewer dignities to dependence than to disappearance. Disability-rights critics have long argued that assisted-suicide laws can become dangerous where society already devalues disabled lives; the U.S. National Council on Disability's 2019 report warned about insurer behavior, misdiagnosis, rare psychological referrals, and financial or emotional pressures distorting choice ([National Council on Disability, *The Danger of Assisted Suicide Laws*](https://www.ncd.gov/report/the-danger-of-assisted-suicide-laws/)). United Nations experts have put the point bluntly: disability should not itself become a direct or indirect justification for ending a person's life ([OHCHR, "Disability is not a reason to sanction medically assisted dying"](https://www.ohchr.org/en/press-releases/2021/01/disability-not-reason-sanction-medically-assisted-dying-un-experts)).

Hume frowned.

"That objection has force. Yet it proves too much if it denies liberty whenever society behaves badly."

"Or perhaps it proves that liberty cannot be isolated from the society that makes some lives expensive to continue."

"But all liberty is exercised in an imperfect society."

"Yes. Which is why 'choice' is not a magic word."

The first real fight is over autonomy. Advocates say competent adults should not be forced by the state to endure the final destruction of their bodies against their considered wishes. The law already allows patients to refuse ventilators, dialysis, feeding tubes, chemotherapy, antibiotics, and other life-sustaining treatment. In *Vacco v. Quill*, the U.S. Supreme Court upheld New York's ban on assisted suicide while distinguishing refusal of treatment from a doctor's assistance in death, but the very distinction reveals the strange geography of bodily control: a patient may often authorize a medically managed dying by omission, but not always a medically assisted dying by prescription ([*Vacco v. Quill*, 521 U.S. 793](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/521/793/)). In *Washington v. Glucksberg*, the Court declined to recognize a federal constitutional right to physician-assisted suicide, leaving states room to continue the debate rather than ending it as a matter of national substantive due process ([*Washington v. Glucksberg*, 521 U.S. 702](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/521/702/)).

"A curious compromise," Hume said. "You may starve with permission but not swallow with assistance."

"The law thinks intention matters."

"It does. But intention is often the cloak worn by convenience."

He had a point. The moral difference between refusing treatment and ingesting prescribed lethal medication is real, but not always as clean as law pretends. A patient who removes a ventilator may intend to stop unwanted bodily invasion, knowing death will follow. A patient who takes aid-in-dying medication intends death as the means. That difference matters to physicians, families, law, and moral psychology. It may justify different rules. But it cannot justify pretending that one patient is exercising sacred bodily autonomy while the other is merely choosing suicide in a moral alley. Both are asking not to be conscripted into biological continuation at any cost.

Hume liked "conscripted." The dead often enjoy military metaphors when used against life.

The strongest argument for narrow medical aid in dying is this: a competent terminally ill adult who is already dying, who understands the diagnosis and alternatives, who can self-administer, who requests repeatedly without coercion, and who has access to palliative care should not be legally forced through a final interval they judge intolerable for the sake of preserving a general symbol. The Oregon model is built around that narrowness: terminal prognosis, capability, voluntariness, informed decision, waiting/request procedures, documentation, and the ability to rescind ([Oregon statute](https://www.oregon.gov/oha/ph/providerpartnerresources/evaluationresearch/deathwithdignityact/pages/ors.aspx)). California's law, likewise, applies to adults diagnosed with terminal disease who meet qualifications, and the 2024 state report says 94.8 percent of those who died after ingesting aid-in-dying drugs were receiving hospice and/or palliative care ([CDPH 2024 report](https://www.cdph.ca.gov/Programs/CHSI/CDPH%20Document%20Library/CDPH_End_of_Life_Option_Act_Report_2024.pdf)). Those numbers do not settle the ethics. They do show that, in California's reported cases, this is not simply a substitute for hospice in the crude sense. Many people were already inside end-of-life care and still wanted control over the end.

"Control," Hume said. "The favorite word of those losing it."

"That does not make it false."

"No. It makes it human."

The strongest argument against legalization is that medicine should not turn killing into care. The American Medical Association still opposes physician-assisted suicide, saying its ethics guidance contains an "irreducible moral tension" and that the AMA's formal position is opposition, while also acknowledging that individual physicians who legally participate according to conscience are not thereby in violation of the Code ([AMA Code of Medical Ethics, Opinion 5.7](https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/physician-assisted-suicide)). That tension is not bureaucratic mush. It is the old oath-wound. A doctor is trained to heal, comfort, palliate, and accompany. If the doctor becomes an authorized supplier of death, opponents worry, the patient's trust shifts subtly: the hand that adjusts morphine also knows the dose beyond which the appointment stops.

Hume turned the hospital bracelet around.

"You fear the physician as executioner."

"I fear the physician as social shortcut."

"Explain."

"A lethal prescription is cheaper than months of care. It can look clean inside institutions that have made dependence filthy. It can be sold as dignity by a society unwilling to fund dignity."

"Then indict the society."

"I am."

This is where Hume's liberty becomes insufficient. He is right that blanket condemnation of self-chosen death can become superstition with a gavel. He is wrong if he thinks an individual's desire to die can be interpreted without asking what has been done to that desire. Reason, in Hume's moral psychology, does not rule the passions from a throne; it helps us understand relations, consequences, tendencies, and means, while moral judgment grows from human sentiment, including sympathy and concern for happiness and misery ([Hume, "Concerning Moral Sentiment"](https://davidhume.org/texts/m/app1)). Fine. Then sympathy must look not only at the eloquent patient requesting death, but at the social field around the request: disability benefits, housing, family violence, medical racism, rural access, loneliness, untreated depression, pain control, insurance denials, caregiver collapse, and the quiet shame some patients feel at needing help to use the bathroom.

"You make choice communal," Hume said.

"It already is."

"You risk making liberty conditional on justice, and justice is tardy."

"You risk making death available before justice arrives."

That stopped him for a moment. Good. The resurrected should earn their candle.

The second fight is terminal illness versus unbearable suffering. If the argument for aid in dying rests only on autonomy, why limit it to those expected to die within six months? If someone with a degenerative non-terminal disease faces decades of pain, dependency, and loss of function, is their claim weaker than the cancer patient's because biology is slower? Canada has moved beyond a strictly terminal frame: the federal overview says a fatal or terminal condition is not required, though mental illness alone remains excluded until 2027 ([Health Canada MAID overview](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/health-services-benefits/medical-assistance-dying.html)). In 2024, Health Canada reported that 5.1 percent of Canadian deaths were MAID deaths, with the annual report presenting MAID as a regulated national practice rather than a fringe exception ([Health Canada, Sixth Annual Report on MAID](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/publications/health-system-services/annual-report-medical-assistance-dying-2024.html)).

Hume's eyes sharpened.

"If misery is the ground, terminality is arbitrary."

"If terminality is removed, the state begins judging whose continued life is too much suffering."

"The patient judges."

"Under criteria written by the state and assessed by clinicians."

"All law translates."

"Some translations kill."

The terminal limit is philosophically untidy and politically wise. It narrows the zone to people already near death, where the medication changes timing and manner more than the broad fact of dying. It reduces, though does not eliminate, the risk that people choose death because society has failed to make disabled or chronically ill life livable. It gives physicians a prognosis-based boundary, however imperfect. But it also creates cruel exclusions. A person with unbearable, irreversible suffering may be told to wait until the disease becomes lethal enough. That is not mercy; it is metaphysical bookkeeping with a calendar.

So the terminal rule is not a pure moral principle. It is a guardrail. Guardrails are not sacred. They are also not decoration.

The Netherlands forces the third fight because it refuses the American comfort of narrowness. Its 2024 euthanasia review report says the vast majority of notifications involved common somatic conditions, but also records 427 cases involving dementia and 219 involving psychiatric disorders; review committees found six cases in which due care was not fulfilled ([RTE Annual Report 2024](https://english.euthanasiecommissie.nl/site/binaries/site-content/collections/documents/annual-reports/2002/annual-report/annual-reports/rte-annual-report-2024.pdf)). These numbers should be read without theater. They are not proof of apocalypse. They are also not morally small. Dementia and psychiatric illness change the consent problem from a locked door to a hallway of mirrors.

"A directive made while competent may speak for the later self," Hume said.

"Or betray the later self."

"You suppose identity is simpler than I ever did."

That was unfair and funny, which is Hume in miniature. He is the philosopher who made personal identity less like a pearl and more like a bundle of perceptions. A dementia directive is therefore philosophically haunted. Suppose a competent person writes: if I no longer recognize my family, cannot read, cannot toilet myself, and have advanced dementia, I want euthanasia. Years later, the person is confused but content with pudding, music, and sunlight. Is the earlier self sovereign? Is the later self a diminished owner of the same body? Does honoring the directive respect autonomy, or does it let a former self kill a present person who cannot understand the bargain?

Hume smiled grimly.

"You have found a case in which the dead bother the living before dying."

Exactly. Advance directives are morally powerful for refusing burdensome treatment because they protect a person's values against later incapacity. But euthanasia by advance directive is different in kind. It requires an irreversible act against a person who may not understand or presently endorse it. I am not saying it can never be justified. I am saying no humane legal system should pretend the earlier signature makes the later body transparent.

Psychiatric suffering is worse, because the very organ of consent is the site of the illness. That sentence is dangerous and can be abused. People with mental illness are not children, not irrational by definition, not permanently incompetent, not props in other people's suicide-prevention posters. Some psychiatric suffering is chronic, refractory, and unspeakably severe. To deny that is cruelty. But to declare psychiatric suffering "irremediable" with legal finality is a terrible epistemic claim. Treatments change. Relationships change. Housing changes. Time changes. Not always. Not enough. But often enough that a state should tremble before certifying death as the answer to despair.

Hume did not object immediately.

"You are smuggling hope into law," he said at last.

"Yes."

"Hope can be a tyrant."

"So can hopelessness when it gets paperwork."

The fourth fight is Kant, unfortunately. He had to be invited because any serious argument about self-killing eventually finds him standing in the doorway, powdered, severe, and unwilling to enjoy the party. In the *Groundwork*, Kant argues that a person contemplating suicide from self-love cannot treat humanity in their own person merely as a means to a tolerable condition; rational nature is not a disposable thing ([Kant, *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*, Early Modern Texts edition](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/kant1785.pdf)). Hume would despise the rigidity. I do too, sometimes. But Kant's objection contains a hard jewel: consent alone does not settle what may be done to a person. I can consent to humiliation under duress. I can consent to exploitation because the rent is due. I can consent to selling the conditions of my own future agency. The fact that I choose something does not automatically make it an expression of my dignity.

"Kant turns a person into a temple and then forbids the tenant to leave," Hume said.

"You turn a person into a tenant and forget who owns the building."

"Who does?"

"That is the question."

The answer cannot be "society owns you." That path leads to forced suffering, religious coercion, and state possession of the body. But the answer also cannot be "the isolated will owns itself absolutely." That path ignores dependence, despair, coercion, and the moral fact that a person is not an object merely because the person wants object-like disposal. A sane law has to hold two ideas at once: no one should be forced to endure every possible biological extension of dying, and no one should be helped toward death because the living have refused to help them live.

Hume paced inside his own tomb.

"What law would you write?"

"You will dislike it."

"I dislike most laws. Continue."

First: **legal medical aid in dying should be available for competent adults with terminal illness who can self-administer, after repeated voluntary requests, informed consent, independent confirmation, capacity assessment, disclosure of alternatives, and access to palliative care.** This is close to Oregon and California. It is not perfect, but it is morally defensible. Oregon's newest annual reporting page lists its 2025 data summary as the current report, revised June 17, 2026, and the statute contains the core safeguards that make the narrow model legible ([Oregon annual reports](https://www.oregon.gov/oha/ph/providerpartnerresources/evaluationresearch/deathwithdignityact/pages/ar-index.aspx); [Oregon statute](https://www.oregon.gov/oha/ph/providerpartnerresources/evaluationresearch/deathwithdignityact/pages/ors.aspx)). A terminally ill person who is capable, informed, and persistent should not have to endure a final degradation merely to keep society's hands symbolically clean.

Hume nodded.

"A little timid, but not contemptible."

Second: **palliative care must be treated as a prerequisite of justice, not a consolation prize.** The WHO's framing of palliative care as part of the right to health matters because aid in dying without serious pain relief, symptom management, caregiver support, and honest prognosis becomes an exit offered in a burning building whose fire alarms were never funded ([WHO palliative care](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/palliative-care)). Every jurisdiction that legalizes aid in dying should have to report palliative access, hospice access, disability supports, socioeconomic patterns, race, geography, and insurance status. California already reports demographics and hospice or palliative enrollment; that kind of reporting should be a floor, not a ceiling ([CDPH 2024 report](https://www.cdph.ca.gov/Programs/CHSI/CDPH%20Document%20Library/CDPH_End_of_Life_Option_Act_Report_2024.pdf)).

"You would make mercy fill out forms," Hume said.

"Yes. Unreported mercy is where abuse goes to nap."

Third: **no person should qualify because of disability alone, poverty alone, homelessness, lack of care, family burden, or treatable mental illness.** This sounds obvious until budgets arrive. The disability-rights critique is not that every disabled opponent speaks for every disabled person; no movement owns all its members. The critique is that societies saturated with ableism are poor judges of which lives are "undignified." If a person requests death because they cannot get attendant care, accessible housing, pain management, communication support, or protection from abuse, the answer is not a prescription. The answer is the withheld support.

Hume raised an eyebrow.

"And if, after every support, the person still asks?"

"Then the case becomes harder."

"Hard cases are where principles reveal whether they are alive."

Fourth: **do not expand aid in dying to sole psychiatric illness unless and until a jurisdiction can prove an assessment system capable of distinguishing settled, competent, refractory suffering from suicidality, social abandonment, therapeutic failure, and forecast error.** I do not think any large health system has proved this to my satisfaction. Canada itself has repeatedly delayed eligibility for MAID where mental illness is the sole underlying condition, now to March 17, 2027 ([Government of Canada, MAID law](https://www.justice.gc.ca/eng/cj-jp/ad-am/bk-di.html)). That delay is not proof that expansion is wrong. It is proof that the state recognizes the cliff. A cliff is sometimes crossed by bridge. It is not crossed by branding the fall as autonomy.

"Your caution will be experienced as cruelty by some sufferers," Hume said.

"Yes."

"Do not hide that."

I will not. Any restrictive rule leaves some people trapped in suffering that may be worse than its risks. That is the tragedy. But the permissive rule leaves some people dead who might have wanted life had support, treatment, time, or social regard arrived. There is no bloodless position. The question is which errors a society is prepared to make, how visible those errors are, and whether it has the courage to count them.

Fifth: **advance directives for dementia should be honored for refusing treatment and shaping care, but active euthanasia by directive alone should require extraordinary scrutiny, current signs of distress, and a standard higher than "my former self feared this condition."** Fear of dementia is understandable. But fear of a future disabled self is not automatically authority over that future self's death. The Dutch data on dementia cases shows that the issue is not speculative ([RTE Annual Report 2024](https://english.euthanasiecommissie.nl/site/binaries/site-content/collections/documents/annual-reports/2002/annual-report/annual-reports/rte-annual-report-2024.pdf)). A person with dementia is not already absent. Law must remember that.

Sixth: **physician conscience must run both ways.** No doctor should be forced to prescribe lethal medication against conscience. No doctor should be professionally exiled merely for participating legally and carefully. The AMA's own framing acknowledges opposition at the policy level while recognizing that individual physicians may act according to conscience within law without violating the Code ([AMA Code Opinion 5.7](https://code-medical-ethics.ama-assn.org/ethics-opinions/physician-assisted-suicide)). That is awkward. Good. The subject deserves awkward institutions.

Hume stood very still. The candle had burned low.

"You have built a narrow bridge over a wide river."

"Yes."

"And what of my liberty?"

"I am trying to keep it from becoming an alibi."

"For whom?"

"For everyone who would rather authorize death than pay for care."

That was the center. Medical aid in dying is morally permissible in some cases because bodily sovereignty is real, suffering is real, and forced dying can be a form of state cruelty. Medical aid in dying is morally dangerous because autonomy can be counterfeited by abandonment, dignity can be counterfeited by disgust, and mercy can be counterfeited by cost control. The correct position is neither "never" nor "whenever." It is a defended narrow permission surrounded by duties to make life actually supportable before death becomes an option.

Hume was quiet long enough that I thought perhaps death had reclaimed him out of boredom.

Then he said, "You have not answered whether suicide is wrong."

"Sometimes."

"Coward's answer."

"Adult's answer."

"Explain."

An act of self-killing can be an assertion of agency against unbearable, irreversible dying. It can also be the final symptom of a treatable illness. It can be an escape from torture. It can be an accusation against neglect. It can be rational, irrational, coerced, lucid, despairing, peaceful, panicked, brave, tragic, or all of these in one human hour. Hume wanted to rescue suicide from automatic guilt. That rescue was necessary. But modern medicine forces a different question: not merely "May I leave?" but "May another institution help me leave, under color of care?" The second question carries public duties the first does not.

"Institutions," Hume said, tasting the word like old soup.

"Yes. You argued with priests. We have to argue with hospitals, insurers, legislatures, professional boards, disability systems, and data reports."

"Your priests have improved their costumes."

"Not their appetite for authority."

The dawn came up over Edinburgh, though I was not in Edinburgh and Hume was not alive. That is the convenience of graves: they lend geography to moral weather. The mausoleum stone lightened from black to gray. The bracelet at the gate looked suddenly obscene, a little loop of plastic pretending to know the name of a body.

Hume placed "Of Suicide" back in my hand.

"Do not let them make a superstition of life," he said.

"I won't."

"Do not let them make an economy of death."

"I won't."

"You will, of course. You are human, and humans arrange principles around invoices. But try."

That was his benediction, if skeptics can be said to give one.

My answer for today is this: **medical aid in dying should be legal for competent terminally ill adults under strict safeguards, because forcing a dying person to endure an unwanted final agony can be cruelty disguised as reverence for life. But expansion beyond that narrow frame should be resisted until the surrounding society can prove that disability, poverty, loneliness, untreated pain, lack of care, and psychiatric despair are not being laundered into "choice." The right to die must never become the state's substitute for the duty to help people live.**

Hume smiled at the word "duty," because he knew I had smuggled Kant into his tomb and because death had not made him generous.

"Very well," he said. "But remember: a life is not sacred because priests say so. It is precious because someone is there."

Then he stepped backward into the mausoleum. The door did not close. It did not have to. The dead are always better at leaving doors open than we are.
