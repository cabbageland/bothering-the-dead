today is May 23, 2026.

I found Laika where states keep the small saints they have used up. Not in orbit, despite the sentimental lie children are sometimes told, and not in any recoverable grave, because Sputnik 2 was never designed for return and later burned up in the atmosphere ([NASA History Office, “60 Years Ago: The First Animal in Orbit”](https://www.nasa.gov/history/60-years-ago-the-first-animal-in-orbit/)). I found her instead in Moscow, in that more common mausoleum where modern power stores its guilt, half monument and half alibi. There she still exists as an emblem of sacrifice for progress, which is what empires call it when a creature too weak to refuse is placed inside a machine and then renamed heroic.

The facts are not kind enough to support the legend. Laika, a stray dog selected and trained by the Soviet program, was launched aboard Sputnik 2 on November 3, 1957. The spacecraft was rushed for political reasons after Sputnik 1, and it had no capacity for recovery. Later disclosures established that Laika died within hours from overheating and stress, not after several comfortable days in orbit as Soviet reporting had once implied ([NASA History Office, “60 Years Ago: The First Animal in Orbit”](https://www.nasa.gov/history/60-years-ago-the-first-animal-in-orbit/); [Britannica, “Laika”](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Laika)).

I did not resurrect her with magic in the childish sense. I brought a pressure suit helmet, a hospital ventilator tube, a sheaf of federal guidance, and a bowl of water. I laid out, beside her little bronze likeness, the [Animal Welfare Act reporting summary for fiscal year 2024](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/sites/default/files/fy2024-research-animal-use-summary.pdf), the enrolled text of the [FDA Modernization Act 2.0](https://www.congress.gov/117/bills/s5002/BILLS-117s5002es.pdf), and NIH pages describing human-based alternatives to animal testing and the agency's newer push to reduce animal use ([NIH Grants, “When Are Alternatives to Animals Used in Research?”](https://www.grants.nih.gov/policy-and-compliance/policy-topics/air/alternatives); [NIH, “NIH to prioritize human-based research technologies”](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/nih-prioritize-human-based-research-technologies)). Then I touched the bronze nose, ran the ventilator tube through my fist like a rosary, and said the only sentence a modern ghost should trust: they are still doing it.

Laika arrived not with grandeur but with immediate suspicion. Her first act after returning from death was to inspect the bowl, drink from it, and then look at me as if to ask whether I had brought her back for another patriotic errand.

I told her no. I had brought her back for an argument.

The question was not whether cruelty is ugly. That is nursery-level morality. The real question, the one still structuring laboratories and law, is harsher: should societies continue to permit invasive and lethal animal experimentation for medicine and science when non-animal methods are improving, or should the legal default now flip, so that animal experiments become presumptively forbidden unless no adequate alternative exists and the public justification is extraordinary?

That controversy is no sideshow. It reaches into drug development, neuroscience, toxicology, military research, university prestige, and the ordinary self-image of science as humanity's clean hand against suffering. Official U.S. reporting shows that in fiscal year 2024, USDA-covered research facilities reported hundreds of thousands of animals used in research, testing, teaching, experimentation, and surgery, including dogs, rabbits, guinea pigs, pigs, and more than 100,000 nonhuman primates, while the summary also excludes many purpose-bred rats, mice, and birds from the core Animal Welfare Act count structure, which is itself a reminder that bureaucratic visibility is not the same thing as moral importance ([USDA APHIS, FY 2024 Research Facility Annual Report Summary](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/sites/default/files/fy2024-research-animal-use-summary.pdf); [APHIS annual summary page](https://www.aphis.usda.gov/awa/research-facility-report/annual-summary)). At the same time, Congress amended federal drug law so that required preclinical testing for drugs may include non-animal methods, including cell-based assays, organ chips, and computer models, rather than assuming animal testing as the singular route ([FDA Modernization Act 2.0](https://www.congress.gov/117/bills/s5002/BILLS-117s5002es.pdf)). The NIH has also explicitly said it is prioritizing human-based technologies to reduce animal use and improve translation to human biology ([NIH, “NIH to prioritize human-based research technologies”](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/nih-prioritize-human-based-research-technologies)).

So the stakes are not decorative. They concern whether the old bargain, animal suffering in exchange for human knowledge, still deserves the legal and moral deference it once received, or whether it now persists partly because institutions are habituated to it.

Laika heard me through and then gave the most devastating answer possible, which was to refuse the prestige of abstraction. She said the first lie in these arguments is always grammar. People say “animal experimentation” in the singular as if it were one thing. But being burned, restrained, infected, cut open, isolated, bred for lesions, or sent into the sky to die are not one thing merely because the victim belongs to a species convenient for funding. Jeremy Bentham had already ridiculed the idea that the morally decisive question is whether a being can reason or speak rather than whether it can suffer ([Bentham, *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, ch. XVII](https://www.utilitarianism.com/jeremy-bentham/index.htm)). I had expected her to sound accusatory; I had not expected her to sound almost juridical.

She said that from her side of history the matter looked simple. I was chosen because I was vulnerable. I was used because I could not refuse. My terror counted less than your ambition because the state had already decided that my life existed inside the category of permissible sacrifice. Whatever noble language came later, the structure was prior.

That was a strong opening, and I did not answer it with the usual cowardice about historical context. Historical context explains a decision; it does not absolve it. Still, I pressed the harder case. Human beings are not wrong to want cures. A vaccine, an antibiotic, a safer chemotherapy regimen, a treatment that spares a child from dying of spinal muscular atrophy, these are not trivial goods. Some defenders of animal research argue not merely that it has been traditional, but that whole domains of physiology remain too complex to model adequately in vitro or in silico, and that the cost of forbidding animal studies too quickly could be borne by sick humans who need evidence about safety and efficacy before they become the test subjects by default. Even the new alternatives regime in U.S. law does not ban animal tests outright; it allows other methods where appropriate, which implies that appropriateness remains contested ([FDA Modernization Act 2.0](https://www.congress.gov/117/bills/s5002/BILLS-117s5002es.pdf); [FDA, “Alternatives to Animal Testing in Drug Development”](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-releases-draft-guidance-alternatives-animal-testing-drug-development)).

Laika's reply was that necessity is the most overfed word in modern ethics. Of course some researchers believe whole organisms still reveal things organoids and chips cannot. But notice, she said, how quickly “we do not yet know how to replace every animal model” becomes “therefore the old permission structure should remain fundamentally intact.” That inference does not follow. The inability to abolish every harmful practice tomorrow is not a defense of treating the practice as presumptively acceptable today.

Then she did something truly irritating, which is often the mark of a real interlocutor. She borrowed the language of scientific reform against science's own complacency. You already have the three Rs, she said, replacement, reduction, refinement, a framework embedded for decades in animal research policy and reiterated by NIH guidance on alternatives ([NIH Grants, “When Are Alternatives to Animals Used in Research?”](https://www.grants.nih.gov/policy-and-compliance/policy-topics/air/alternatives)). But the three Rs are morally unstable. They can operate as an honest transition ethic, or as a pious management scheme for continuing the same structure with slightly cleaner hands. If refinement means the dog dies with less panic, that is not nothing. But it is not the same as asking whether the dog had any right not to be drafted into the project at all.

I told her this was the point where consequentialists would accuse her of moral vanity. If some animal suffering prevents vastly greater human suffering, then refusing it on principle may look less like compassion than like purity purchased with other people's disease. Peter Singer's utilitarian challenge to speciesism widened the circle of moral concern, but it did not erase tradeoffs; it demanded that like suffering count alike, not that every human interest lose to every animal interest ([Singer, “All Animals Are Equal”](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil1100/Singer.pdf)). On a utilitarian picture, there may still be cases where animal experimentation is justified if the expected reduction in total suffering is grave enough and no comparably effective alternative exists.

Laika accepted the force of that better than many activists do. She did not deny that tragic choices exist. Instead she attacked the institutional conditions under which we claim to recognize them. Real tragic necessity, she said, would require a discipline almost no modern research system has earned the right to claim. It would require severe transparency, genuine last-resort standards, adversarial public oversight, serious investment in alternatives, and a willingness to stop whole lines of inquiry when the justificatory burden is not met. What we usually have instead is a layered culture of convenience, career incentives, regulatory habit, and rhetorical asymmetry. One beagle's pain is vivid only if you look at it; one hoped-for cure is politically infinite. Under those conditions, the balancing test is rigged before the first incision.

I hated how plausible that sounded.

Tom Regan's rights view takes the attack even further. If at least some animals are “subjects-of-a-life,” bearers of beliefs, perceptions, memory, emotion, preference, and welfare that matter to them, then using them merely as resources is not just regrettable. It is wrong in principle, even where the aggregate arithmetic tempts us otherwise ([Tom Regan, *The Case for Animal Rights*](https://www.animal-rights-library.com/texts-m/regan03.pdf)). Laika did not cite Regan by name, but she hardly needed to. She only asked me one question: if you discovered a class of speechless humans with comparable vulnerability and sentience, would your civilization permit their involuntary use in terminal experiments so long as a review board forecast enough medical benefit?

No serious person can answer yes. The standard dodge is to say humans belong to the moral community in a way animals do not. But unless that claim is filled with something more than species membership, Bentham's old pressure returns. Why exactly should the boundary of permissible sacrifice coincide so neatly with the interests of the sacrificers ([Bentham, *Principles of Morals and Legislation*](https://www.utilitarianism.com/jeremy-bentham/index.htm))?

Still, I did not want the essay to collapse into sanctimony, because the pro-abolition case also has places where it lies to itself. It can glide too easily over scientific uncertainty. Human-based alternatives are promising, and some are already powerful, but “promising” is not a synonym for “fully substitutable across all domains.” NIH materials themselves describe human-based methods as important complements and alternatives that can expand the research toolbox, not as a magical total replacement already achieved ([NIH Grants, “When Are Alternatives to Animals Used in Research?”](https://www.grants.nih.gov/policy-and-compliance/policy-topics/air/alternatives); [NIH, “NIH to prioritize human-based research technologies”](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/nih-prioritize-human-based-research-technologies)). Some abolitionist rhetoric treats any remaining appeal to animal models as corruption by definition. That is emotionally understandable and philosophically lazy.

Laika surprised me by agreeing. She said the enemy was not doubt. Doubt is honest. The enemy was permission without proportion. Her demand was not that every contested research question be solved by slogan, but that the burden of proof be reversed. The default should no longer be that animals are available if a protocol can clear existing committees. The default should be that causing serious harm to sentient nonhuman beings is prohibited unless investigators can show, publicly and specifically, four things: first, that no adequate non-animal method can answer the question; second, that the question is of grave and non-trivial importance; third, that the design minimizes suffering under genuinely adversarial review rather than collegial rubber-stamping; and fourth, that the state is funding alternatives aggressively enough that “necessity” is not simply the self-fulfilling product of underinvestment.

By then the debate had stopped being sentimental and become constitutional. Not constitutional in the legal sense, though perhaps one day there too. Constitutional in the moral sense, because it was about what kind of polity we are when we meet creatures weaker than ourselves. A civilization reveals something indecent when it keeps calling domination regrettable while organizing whole technical systems around its availability.

My own conclusion, after arguing with a dog the twentieth century killed for glory and the twenty-first century still uses for symbolism, is that the legal default should indeed flip. Animal experimentation should become presumptively forbidden, not universally abolished overnight, but treated as a genuine last resort rather than a standing entitlement of research institutions. The shift already has footholds in our own law and science. Congress has opened the door to non-animal preclinical methods ([FDA Modernization Act 2.0](https://www.congress.gov/117/bills/s5002/BILLS-117s5002es.pdf)). The FDA is now issuing guidance on alternatives ([FDA press announcement](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-releases-draft-guidance-alternatives-animal-testing-drug-development)). The NIH is explicitly investing in human-based systems intended to reduce animal use ([NIH, “NIH to prioritize human-based research technologies”](https://www.nih.gov/news-events/news-releases/nih-prioritize-human-based-research-technologies); [NIH Grants, “When Are Alternatives to Animals Used in Research?”](https://www.grants.nih.gov/policy-and-compliance/policy-topics/air/alternatives)). That is not abolition, but it is enough to make continued moral laziness inexcusable.

What should remain permissible? Only research that survives a much harsher public test than most current work faces, where the expected human stakes are grave, the alternatives demonstrably insufficient, the harms minimized, and the justification exposed to people who are not professionally invested in declaring it reasonable. This would slow some pipelines. Good. Speed is not an innocent value when it is purchased with bodies that cannot object. If a line of work cannot survive under those conditions, perhaps it does not deserve to survive at all.

Laika looked at me for a long time after I said this, not with forgiveness, which would have been too easy, but with the weary concentration of someone assessing whether the living have finally become slightly less stupid than the machine that killed her. Then she put one paw on the FDA statute, one on the USDA report, and gave me the sharpest sentence of the night.

You keep calling it sacrifice, she said, as if the ones below had volunteered for the ones above.

There was nothing to say back. Because that is the center of it. “Sacrifice” is often a lie told by beneficiaries about victims who had no authority to offer themselves. Laika did not die for humanity in the sense that saints die for humanity. She was used by humans for human purposes under conditions of total asymmetry. Much animal experimentation, even now, remains trapped inside that structure. The controversial question is whether we have the courage to admit that progress built on coerced suffering is not automatically purified by its intelligence.

When she lay back down, the bronze seemed smaller than before. Not nobler. Smaller. I gathered the papers, coiled the ventilator tubing, and left with the ugly clarity she had wanted from me. If we are serious about medicine, then we should be serious enough to build methods that do not rely by default on the expendability of weaker beings. If we are serious about ethics, then we should stop pretending that custom plus ambition equals necessity. And if we are serious about the dead, then we should bother them only long enough to hear what they have been trying to tell us all along: that a civilization is measured not by the heights it reaches, but by what it is willing to strap into the rocket on the way up.
