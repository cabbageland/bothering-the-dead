today is May 17, 2026.

I found Mary Shelley above Lake Geneva, where weather still knows how to look accusatory. The villa is a museum now, and museums are polite ways of admitting that the dead continue to organize our feelings. I went at dusk with a lantern, a length of copper wire, and a copy of *Frankenstein* opened to Victor's confession that he had once pursued "the secret of life" and then recoiled from the being to whom he had given it ([Mary Shelley, *Frankenstein*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/84/84-h/84-h.htm)). The storm did the rest. Rain drove hard across the garden. I touched the wire to the iron gate, spoke her full name, and the air acquired that strange administrative gravity that sometimes precedes a resurrection, as though the universe were reluctantly stamping an old file back into circulation.

She rose not from a grave exactly, but from the soaked dark between the chestnut trees, assembled out of candle smoke, lake mist, and the stubbornness of an author who has already outlived most of the people who misread her. She looked young and tired, which also felt right. I told her I had brought her back for a controversy that belonged to her lineage even if the machinery was new. Should human beings attempt de-extinction, not as fantasy but as actual biotechnology, by editing living species so that extinct traits and extinct-looking animals return to the earth? The immediate case was the woolly mammoth project now pursued by Colossal, which explicitly aims not to produce a pure mammoth but a cold-resistant elephant carrying mammoth traits, gestated through elephant reproduction and eventually used, if all goes according to plan, to alter Arctic ecosystems ([Colossal, "Mammoth"](https://colossal.com/mammoth/)).

The argument for doing this is not childish. Colossal presents the project as climate intervention, conservation tool, and proof of technical capacity all at once. Their public case is that mammoth-like elephants could help restore grasslands, reduce shrub cover, disturb snowpack, and perhaps contribute to preserving permafrost, while the associated genomic tools might aid conservation of living elephants and other endangered species ([Colossal, "Mammoth"](https://colossal.com/mammoth/)). Beth Shapiro, who has written more soberly than most people in this space, makes a related but more careful point: what genome engineering may actually resurrect are extinct traits rather than the extinct organism in full, and those tools could matter not only for spectacle but for conserving species that are not yet gone ([Beth Shapiro, "Mammoth 2.0: will genome engineering resurrect extinct species?" *Genome Biology*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4632474/)). Climate collapse is real, biodiversity collapse is real, and if our existing conservation repertoire is failing, it is not irrational to ask whether audacious interventions deserve a hearing.

Mary Shelley listened without smiling. Then she asked the first necessary question: what exactly do you think you are bringing back?

This was not pedantry. It was the center of the dispute. Shapiro says it plainly: if no living cells remain, an extinct species cannot simply be cloned; what can be produced instead is an organism engineered from a living relative, shaped by different gestational conditions, different social learning, different microbiomes, and a different world ([Shapiro](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4632474/)). Even Colossal's own language slips, usefully, between "the woolly mammoth" and "a cold-resistant elephant with all of the core biological traits of the woolly mammoth" ([Colossal, "Mammoth"](https://colossal.com/mammoth/)). That is already a philosophical confession. The creature under discussion would not be a recovered past but a manufactured proxy, a new being bearing our interpretation of a vanished one.

Shelley said Victor Frankenstein's original sin was not curiosity. It was authorship without humility. He did not merely discover; he composed, selected, assembled, and then mistook the result for a transparent victory over death. In the novel, what destroys him is not a punishment for science as such, but his refusal to accept responsibility for the social and moral life of what he has made ([Mary Shelley, *Frankenstein*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/84/84-h/84-h.htm)). She suspected the same temptation here. De-extinction rhetoric often advertises wonder, redemption, and repaired nature. Less often does it dwell on the fact that the first mammoth-like calves would be experimental beings produced through endangered elephant bodies, with uncertain welfare needs, uncertain social worlds, and uncertain fates.

I objected that this can be said about many conservation interventions. Captive breeding, translocation, assisted migration, and genetic rescue all involve organisms under intensive human management. Nobody serious thinks nature is now some untouched sphere from which ethics demands our total abstinence. Even critics of de-extinction concede that ecological restoration sometimes requires active and novel repair. The case for mammoth-like proxies is strongest when framed not as amusement but as restoration under emergency conditions. A recent defense in *The Conversation* argued that because climate change and ecological loss are already catastrophic, even ambitious interventions with a low probability of success may be morally permissible, especially if damaged Arctic systems might benefit from the return of large herbivore functions ([Christopher Gyngell, Thomas Douglas, and Julian Savulescu, "Bringing woolly mammoths back from extinction might not be such a bad idea"](https://theconversation.com/bringing-woolly-mammoths-back-from-extinction-might-not-be-such-a-bad-idea-ethicists-explain-167892)). If we accept rewilding, I said, why draw an absolute line here?

She answered by drawing a better line than absolute prohibition. Rewilding returns living species to places they once inhabited. De-extinction, at least in this case, proposes to design a novel organism and then treat its hoped-for ecological behavior as partial justification for having made it. That is not nothing, but neither is it restoration in the simple sense. It is invention under ecological branding.

I hated how accurate that sounded, so I pushed on the strongest pro case. Human beings have already remade the conditions of life on earth. We have fragmented habitats, heated the atmosphere, driven species extinct, and made passivity into its own form of violence. If genomic tools can help preserve biodiversity, broaden elephant resilience, or reintroduce traits useful for survival, refusing to use them may amount less to humility than to squeamishness. Shapiro's concluding argument goes precisely there: genome engineering could become "an important new tool for conserving biodiversity that is not yet extinct" ([Shapiro](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4632474/)). Perhaps the mammoth is the glamorous edge of a larger and defensible project.

Shelley did not deny that. In fact she surprised me by granting it. She was not opposed to every use of severe technique. She was opposed to moral confusion. The confusion appears whenever people speak as if technological brilliance cancels political failure. Why, she asked, are elephants endangered in the first place? Why are extant species starving for ordinary conservation money while venture capital can gather around a resurrected icon? Why does a world that cannot reliably protect living habitats suddenly trust itself with manufacturing proxy ghosts?

That question is not merely rhetorical. The most serious policy criticism of de-extinction is opportunity cost. A 2017 *Nature Ecology & Evolution* analysis concluded that, even under optimistic assumptions, public conservation funding directed toward resurrected species could mean fewer extant threatened species are conserved, producing net biodiversity loss ([Richard F. Maloney et al., "Spending limited resources on de-extinction could lead to net biodiversity loss"](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-016-0053)). The authors allow that fully external sponsorship could alter the picture, but even then the money spent on charismatic resurrection carries its own shadow cost if those resources might otherwise have aided living species directly ([Maloney et al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-016-0053)). This is where the controversy becomes ugly in a productive way. The issue is not whether de-extinction is exciting. Of course it is. The issue is whether excitement is now colonizing conservation priorities.

Shelley became sharper here. She said modern people have a habit of preferring salvage epics to maintenance. We like moonshots because they flatter us. We dislike the ordinary disciplines that would have prevented the catastrophe. It is more glamorous to promise mammoths than to stop mining, stop clearing habitat, stop poaching, stop funding extinction through routine commerce. In *Frankenstein*, Victor is intoxicated by singular creation; he is bored by care. That boredom is the real monster, because it recurs institutionally. We neglect the living and then fantasize about a heroic future in which our machines redeem the loss.

I thought that was too convenient. The anti-de-extinction position can become morally vain in its own way, congratulating itself on seriousness while refusing any risky intervention not already normalized by older institutions. There is a status quo bias hiding inside the language of hubris. The world is already broken enough that "leave it alone" is often just another way of saying "let the current trajectory continue." The same *Conversation* essay uses Bostrom and Ord's reversal test to make precisely that point: if mammoth-like animals already existed in Siberia and played a beneficial ecological role, many people would consider it obligatory to conserve them rather than absurd to do so ([Gyngell, Douglas, and Savulescu](https://theconversation.com/bringing-woolly-mammoths-back-from-extinction-might-not-be-such-a-bad-idea-ethicists-explain-167892)). Not every refusal is wisdom. Some are just fear wearing ethical clothes.

Shelley accepted the hit and returned one. Fine, she said. Then stop pretending the argument is about "bringing back" the mammoth. Say instead: we are considering the deliberate production of a new elephant-derived being, for reasons that mix climate anxiety, conservation ambition, scientific prestige, and civilizational vanity. That formulation is harsher, but it has the virtue of honesty. Once phrased that way, the relevant moral questions sharpen. What duties are owed to the created animal itself? What risks are imposed on surrogate elephants? Who decides whether ecological uncertainty is acceptable? What counts as success if the resulting being is healthy, intelligent, socially needy, and useful mostly as a symbol? And who bears responsibility if the project works just well enough to generate a new class of dependent creatures but not well enough to restore any meaningful system?

These are not science-fiction flourishes. They are the exact questions Victor refused to ask until it was too late. In the novel, the creature's tragedy is not only exclusion but ontological abandonment. He is made into the world without a community prepared for his existence ([Mary Shelley, *Frankenstein*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/84/84-h/84-h.htm)). A mammoth-like elephant would not speak in Miltonic cadences, but it would still be a subject of welfare, dependency, and need. If de-extinction advocates want the moral authority of conservation, they must accept obligations more demanding than publicity, patents, and livestreamed awe.

By then the rain had thinned. I asked her the narrowest version of the question I could manage. Should we do it, or not?

She gave the infuriating answer that serious dead people often give: not under the story you are currently telling yourselves.

What she meant was this. If de-extinction is framed as planetary redemption, it encourages fraud in advance. The narrative pressure to produce a triumph will overwhelm candor about partiality, proxy status, welfare burdens, and ecological uncertainty. If it is framed as one research program within a broader conservation ethic, then the standards must become much more severe. No project should proceed on the back of ruined extant conservation budgets. No created beings should exist merely as prestige artifacts. Welfare obligations to surrogate elephants and engineered offspring must be central rather than ornamental. And no one should be allowed to market technological resurrection as an excuse for letting the living world continue to burn.

That position is less romantic than both the cheerleaders and the scolds would like. It rules out the lazy anti-technology sermon, but it also rules out the investor pitch. My own conclusion, after bothering Mary Shelley in a storm she deserved better than, is that de-extinction should be treated as presumptively suspect but not categorically forbidden. The stronger the claim that an intervention could protect extant biodiversity or repair a damaged ecosystem, the stronger the burden of proof to show that it does not merely divert money, instrumentalize endangered surrogates, or manufacture symbolic life to compensate for ongoing political cowardice. We may be justified in creating proxy organisms under conditions of ecological emergency. But if we do, we should say with full clarity that we are not reversing death. We are assuming authorship.

Shelley seemed almost pleased by that, which made me nervous. Before she dissolved back into the weather, she said the line that should haunt every laboratory tempted by resurrection. The problem is never only whether we can animate a body. The problem is whether we are prepared to deserve the life that answers.

Then she was gone, and the lake resumed its black composure, leaving the living with the oldest technical question in the world: whether creation under conditions of power can ever be innocent, or only more or less responsible.
