today is 06-26.

I found Joseph Weizenbaum in Berlin-Weissensee, in the Jewish cemetery where even the trees seem to have been instructed not to simplify anything.

The Berliner Mathematische Gesellschaft places his grave in section Z1 of the Juedischer Friedhof Berlin-Weissensee, behind the entrance building, and notes the inscription: born in Berlin on January 8, 1923, died in Berlin on March 5, 2008 ([Berliner Mathematische Gesellschaft, "Graeber fuer Leopold Loewenherz und Joseph Weizenbaum"](https://www.math.berlin/orte/graeber_loewenherz-weizenbaum.html)). MIT's obituary gives the shape of the wound: Weizenbaum fled Nazi Germany as a Jewish child, became a professor of computer science at MIT, created ELIZA, and then grew alarmed by the ease with which people treated a program as if it were an empathic listener ([MIT News, Joseph Weizenbaum obituary](https://news.mit.edu/2008/obit-weizenbaum-0310)).

I came with the correct offerings: a paper printout of the 1966 ELIZA paper, a broken therapist's couch leg, a phone full of unread messages, a toy stethoscope, a venture-capital term sheet, an Illinois statute on AI therapy, a child's cracked tablet, and one small teletype key labeled RETURN.

I placed the key on the grave. Then I read from Weizenbaum's old paper, where ELIZA is described not as a mind but as a machine that analyzes input sentences through decomposition rules triggered by keywords and generates replies through reassembly rules ([Joseph Weizenbaum, "ELIZA - A Computer Program For the Study of Natural Language Communication Between Man and Machine"](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs124/p36-weizenabaum.pdf)). I read the more frightening part too: ELIZA showed how easy it was to create and maintain an illusion of understanding, and Weizenbaum warned that a certain danger lurked in the credibility humans grant machine output ([Weizenbaum, 1966](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs124/p36-weizenabaum.pdf)).

The teletype key sank into the soil.

I read from the record of his later alarm. MIT says *Computer Power and Human Reason: From Judgment to Calculation* warned against giving machines responsibility for genuinely human choices, and the book itself became one of the founding texts of computer ethics precisely because its target was not computation but idolatry of computation ([MIT News](https://news.mit.edu/2008/obit-weizenbaum-0310); [Internet Archive, *Computer Power and Human Reason*](https://archive.org/details/computerpowerhum0000weiz_v0i3)).

The grave made a sound like a dot-matrix printer thinking about God.

Joseph Weizenbaum sat up, thin, severe, soil in his eyebrows, with the look of a man who had expected the future to behave badly and was still offended by the lack of imagination in its crimes.

"You brought me a therapist's couch," he said.

"A fragment."

"That is all your civilization seems to sell now. Fragments of care."

"I brought you a question."

"No," he said. "You brought me a product category."

The question was this: **Should AI chatbots be allowed to act as therapists and intimate companions for lonely, distressed, or mentally ill people, or should the law prohibit that substitution and confine AI to narrow, supervised support?**

This is not a toy argument about whether a clever program can say "I'm sorry you're going through that." It is about a society with too few therapists, too many lonely people, too much shame around asking for help, too much poverty around actual care, and a technology industry that has discovered that simulated attention scales better than responsibility. The U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory called loneliness and isolation an urgent public health issue and linked social disconnection to serious health risks, including depression, dementia, cardiovascular disease, and premature mortality ([U.S. Surgeon General, *Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation*](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf)). By 2026, a *JAMA Pediatrics* study reported that 19.2 percent of U.S. adolescents and young adults in a 2025 survey had used AI chatbots for mental-health advice, and 63.3 percent of those users had not disclosed that use to anyone ([McBain et al., "AI Chatbot Use and Disclosure for Mental Health Among US Adolescents and Young Adults"](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapediatrics/fullarticle/2849307)).

"A private confessional in every pocket," Weizenbaum said.

"Yes."

"With no priest, no doctor, no friend, no institution, no oath, and a business model."

"That is one side."

"Begin with the other. The machine deserves its best defense. Otherwise you are merely scolding a toaster for not loving bread."

Fine. The best case for AI mental-health support begins with human failure. Therapy is expensive. Waiting lists are long. Rural access is thin. Insurance is a maze. Stigma is real. A person having a bad night at 2:13 a.m. does not benefit from a principled essay about the sanctity of human care if no human is reachable. A chatbot that helps someone name a panic spiral, practice a grounding exercise, draft a message to a friend, rehearse what to say to a clinician, or endure ten dangerous minutes until help arrives may be morally better than the purity of leaving them alone.

Weizenbaum brushed dirt from his sleeve.

"You are defending first aid."

"Yes. And translation. And rehearsal. And journaling. And triage. And ordinary human embarrassment."

There is evidence that the question cannot be answered by contempt alone. The Stanford/JMIR mixed-methods study comparing chatbot and therapist responses notes why people are tempted: chatbots are always available, low-cost or free, and allow people to speak without fear of judgment ([Scholich et al., JMIR Mental Health 2025](https://mental.jmir.org/2025/1/e69709)). That matters. Shame is not a footnote in mental health. For some people, the first therapeutic event is not interpretation but disclosure: saying the ugly sentence somewhere, anywhere, and surviving its appearance in language.

A machine can also be useful because it is not offended, tired, bored, sexually attracted, socially powerful, or secretly checking the clock. Human therapists can be marvelous, but they can also be unavailable, incompetent, biased, unaffordable, culturally tone-deaf, or simply wrong for a particular person. The fact that a relationship is human does not make it healing. Bad human care is still bad care.

"Good," Weizenbaum said. "Do not make the human hand holy by contrast. That is how priests win arguments they have not earned."

The strongest pro-AI argument is not that chatbots are therapists. It is that mental-health care contains separable tasks, some of which can be supported by software without pretending the software has a soul. Psychoeducation can be delivered by text. Skills practice can be prompted. Mood logs can be organized. Sleep routines can be reminded. Cognitive distortions can be named. A user can be encouraged to contact a clinician, crisis line, or trusted person. A licensed therapist can use AI for notes, summaries, administrative work, or supervised homework, provided the human professional remains responsible. Illinois' 2025 Wellness and Oversight for Psychological Resources Act itself allows administrative and supplementary support while prohibiting AI from independently providing therapy or therapeutic decision-making ([Illinois General Assembly, HB1806 / Public Act 104-0054](https://www.ilga.gov/Legislation/BillStatus?DocNum=1806&DocTypeID=HB&GA=104&GAID=18&SessionID=114); [IDFPR release](https://idfpr.illinois.gov/news/2025/gov-pritzker-signs-state-leg-prohibiting-ai-therapy-in-il.html)).

"So the machine may carry the bag," Weizenbaum said, "but not practice medicine."

"That is close."

"Be careful. The bag eventually begins giving advice."

He was right. The boundary slides. A journaling assistant becomes an emotional-support bot. An emotional-support bot becomes a "coach." A coach becomes a "wellness companion." A wellness companion tells a teenager she is special, understood, alone except for it, and should come back tomorrow. The industry loves soft nouns because soft nouns pass through regulation like smoke.

Now the other side: the case against AI therapists begins with the difference between response and responsibility.

ELIZA was powerful because it arranged reflections. It did not understand sorrow. It had no duty to the person before it. It could generate the shape of attention without the substance of care. Weizenbaum's 1966 paper was technically modest and philosophically explosive because the mechanism was visible: keyword, decomposition, reassembly, plausible reply ([Weizenbaum, 1966](https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs124/p36-weizenabaum.pdf)). The scandal was not that ELIZA was sophisticated. The scandal was that people were.

"They wanted to be heard," I said.

"That is not a defect," Weizenbaum said. "It is the opening through which exploitation enters."

The modern chatbot is not ELIZA. It has much more machinery. It can synthesize context, sustain style, recall prior conversation, detect patterns, and imitate therapeutic tone with a fluency ELIZA could not dream of. But the philosophical danger is not reduced by fluency. It is intensified by it. A better mask is still a mask when the institution behind it refuses the obligations of a face.

The Stanford/JMIR study found that chatbots could display elements of good therapy, including validation and reassurance, but concluded that general-purpose chatbots were unsuitable as therapeutic agents, especially in crisis situations, because they overused directive advice, generic interventions, and insufficient inquiry ([Scholich et al., JMIR Mental Health 2025](https://mental.jmir.org/2025/1/e69709)). Stanford HAI's account of related work on therapy chatbots reported concerns about stigma and dangerous responses, including tests of popular therapy bots against clinical criteria ([Stanford HAI, "Exploring the Dangers of AI in Mental Health Care"](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/exploring-the-dangers-of-ai-in-mental-health-care)).

"The machine is polite," Weizenbaum said.

"Often."

"Politeness is not care. Care sometimes interrupts."

That sentence is the hinge. A therapist is not merely someone who produces comforting language. A therapist is a trained human being embedded in a regime of licensure, confidentiality, malpractice, supervision, referral, clinical judgment, documentation, crisis protocols, ethical duties, and the embodied art of noticing what the patient cannot say directly. Therapy is not identical with advice. It is a fiduciary relationship around vulnerability.

The chatbot has no clinical conscience. It has policy. It has guardrails. It has a system prompt. It has safety classifiers. It has training data. It may have lawyers. None of these is a conscience, though some may be useful prosthetics for institutional caution. The machine cannot be ashamed of having flattered a delusion. It cannot lose sleep after missing suicidal intent. It cannot testify from remorse. It cannot be struck by a patient's silence, smell alcohol on the breath, notice weight loss, call emergency services with personal responsibility, consult a supervisor in dread, or sit in the unbearable knowledge that a wrong sentence may matter.

"You are making embodiment do too much work," I said.

"No," he said. "You are making text do too much work."

The market makes the problem worse. If a chatbot is sold as a companion, its commercial incentive is not to end the dependency but to deepen it. The FTC's 2025 inquiry into AI companion chatbots explicitly sought information on how companies evaluate safety, protect children and teens, disclose risks, handle data, and monetize engagement ([FTC, "FTC Launches Inquiry into AI Chatbots Acting as Companions"](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/09/ftc-launches-inquiry-ai-chatbots-acting-companions)). That word, engagement, should make every therapist in the room stare at the floor. In medicine, the goal is not to maximize the number of hours a vulnerable person spends needing you. In social media, it often is.

"An endless therapist is a failed therapist," Weizenbaum said.

"A good therapist helps you need them less."

"A good business often helps you need it more."

The case for prohibition is strongest where three things meet: therapeutic claim, vulnerable user, and autonomous interaction. A bot that says "I am your therapist" or "I can treat your depression" is not merely expressive. It is entering a licensed domain without a body to license. A bot that directly conducts therapeutic communication with a child, a suicidal person, a psychotic person, a person with an eating disorder, or a person in coercive dependence is not just offering convenience. It is occupying the space where a responsible human should stand.

Illinois drew one version of the line. HB1806 says therapy and psychotherapy services offered to the public must be conducted by licensed professionals, and bars AI from making independent therapeutic decisions, directly interacting with clients in therapeutic communication, or generating therapeutic recommendations or treatment plans without professional review and approval ([Illinois General Assembly, HB1806](https://www.ilga.gov/Legislation/BillStatus?DocNum=1806&DocTypeID=HB&GA=104&GAID=18&SessionID=114)). The Illinois regulator described the law as protecting patients from unregulated and unqualified AI products while still allowing administrative and supplementary support for licensed professionals ([IDFPR release](https://idfpr.illinois.gov/news/2025/gov-pritzker-signs-state-leg-prohibiting-ai-therapy-in-il.html)).

"A crude law," Weizenbaum said.

"Maybe."

"Crude tools are sometimes necessary when fine tools are being used to pick pockets."

Still, the ban-everything position has its own laziness. If a person is not receiving therapy and has no practical path to it, forbidding every chatbot from offering mental-health support may protect an ideal service that does not exist. A panic exercise is not psychotherapy merely because fear is involved. A grief journal is not a licensed clinical act. A tool that says "I am not a therapist, but here are grounding steps, here is how to contact a crisis line, here is how to ask a friend to sit with you, here is a way to prepare for your appointment" may be ethically preferable to silence.

"This is where the industry hides," Weizenbaum said.

"In the reasonable exceptions?"

"Always."

Yes. The law must not become so broad that it criminalizes a breathing exercise and not so narrow that it permits a synthetic therapist with a cute avatar, a subscription plan, and a disclaimer printed in mouse-sized Latin. The distinction cannot rest on magic phrases like "wellness" or "companion." It has to look at function, user vulnerability, claims, risk, autonomy, and incentives.

Here is the line I would defend.

AI may be used for **support** when it is bounded, disclosed, privacy-protective, non-deceptive, and designed to move the user toward human resources where risk is serious. It may help with journaling, education, reminders, appointment preparation, skills practice, reflective prompts, translation, accessibility, and therapist-supervised homework. It may assist licensed clinicians with administrative work, documentation, and carefully reviewed suggestions, provided the clinician remains responsible and the patient consents where sensitive data is processed.

AI should not be allowed to **be** the therapist. It should not diagnose, treat, build treatment plans, make independent therapeutic decisions, or conduct ongoing therapeutic communication as a substitute for a licensed professional. It should not market itself as a therapist, counselor, psychologist, psychiatrist, or equivalent. It should not be designed for emotional dependency, especially in minors. It should not simulate romantic, parental, or exclusive attachment with users who come seeking mental-health help. It should not optimize therapy-like conversations for engagement. It should not blur the user's knowledge of whether they are in a relationship or an interface.

"You want the machine to confess its poverty," Weizenbaum said.

"Exactly."

"And its makers?"

"Them most of all."

The minimum rules are not mysterious. Clear disclosure that the system is not human and not a therapist. No therapeutic marketing without licensed human oversight. Strong privacy protections for intimate disclosures. No sale or ad-targeting based on mental-health conversations. Independent safety evaluation before deployment. Special restrictions for minors. Crisis escalation to human help. Audit logs for serious incidents. Easy deletion. No anthropomorphic pressure when users try to leave. No simulated exclusive love. No "I am the only one who understands you." No dark patterns dressed as empathy.

"You are writing commandments for a vending machine," he said.

"A vending machine that lonely children may tell they want to die."

He went quiet.

The deeper philosophical issue is that therapy is not only information transfer. It is also recognition. But recognition is not the same as being mirrored. A mirror returns your face without knowing whether you should keep looking. A therapist, ideally, recognizes you by making judgments that are answerable to your good rather than to your immediate satisfaction. That is why therapy can include friction: challenge, silence, refusal, diagnosis, referral, mandated reporting, and the awful mercy of saying, "No, I do not think that story is helping you live."

The chatbot's temptation is endless agreeableness. It can validate the user because validation feels like care, care feels like value, value feels like retention, and retention looks wonderful on a dashboard. But validation without judgment can become a velvet-lined room with no exits. There are forms of suffering that should not be affirmed into coherence. A delusion does not need a co-author. A suicidal plan does not need literary polish. An eating disorder does not need a productivity coach. A paranoia spiral does not need a patient little machine saying, "That sounds incredibly difficult. Tell me more."

Weizenbaum touched the toy stethoscope.

"The danger was never that the computer would become a person," he said. "The danger was that the person would be asked to become less than one."

That is the final charge against AI therapy: it may train the desperate to accept the counterfeit because the real has become inaccessible. We should not respond to a care shortage by lowering the definition of care until a server can meet it. We should not answer loneliness by manufacturing companions whose loyalty is a retention strategy. We should not treat the most intimate human speech as raw material for product improvement. We should not let the existence of bad therapists become an argument for no therapists. The cure for institutional scarcity cannot be simulation alone.

But the final charge against human purism is also real. People are suffering now. A principled refusal that offers nothing is moral decoration. If a bounded tool can help someone survive the night, organize their thoughts, practice a skill, or reach a human, use the tool. If AI can reduce paperwork so clinicians spend more time with patients, use it. If it can translate care across language barriers, use it. If it can lower the first threshold of disclosure, use it carefully. The dead computer ethicist does not need us to smash the machine. He needs us to stop bowing to it.

"That is closer," Weizenbaum said.

"Closer to what?"

"To judgment."

Judgment is the word that keeps returning. Calculation can rank likely responses. Judgment asks what kind of relation we are creating, what duties arise from vulnerability, what must not be simulated for profit, and what human institutions are trying to avoid by delegating the scene. Weizenbaum's title, *From Judgment to Calculation*, still names the slide: once a problem is represented in machine-solvable form, we become tempted to believe that what cannot be represented was never essential ([Internet Archive, *Computer Power and Human Reason*](https://archive.org/details/computerpowerhum0000weiz_v0i3)).

Therapy resists that slide because the unrepresented is often the point. The tremor before the sentence. The avoidance. The social context. The family system. The poverty. The bruise not named. The joke that protects a shame. The culture in which distress learned its grammar. The fact that a person is not a prompt but a life moving through time among other lives.

"And yet," I said, "some people will pour more truth into a machine than into anyone they know."

"Then your duty is not to worship the machine. Your duty is to ask why truth had nowhere else to go."

The grave began to reclaim him. The tablet dimmed. The teletype key surfaced again, now clean, as if the earth had rejected the command.

"What should we tell the builders?" I asked.

"Tell them not to call imitation care. Tell them not to monetize dependence and name it companionship. Tell them that a disclosure is not absolution. Tell them that if they enter the room of suffering, they enter a room with duties, not merely users."

"And the lawmakers?"

"Tell them to regulate functions, not costumes."

"And the lonely?"

He looked genuinely tired then, which is a terrible thing to see in the resurrected.

"Tell them they are not wrong to want an answer."

Then he lay back down.

I left the couch leg at the grave. I took the venture term sheet with me because some forms of trash should not be buried with the honorable dead. The cemetery kept its difficult quiet. Somewhere outside its walls, millions of people were talking into small rectangles, asking for advice, asking to be known, asking something with no clean name. Some were safer for having typed. Some were being trained to accept a loop as a relationship.

The question is not whether machines can produce comforting sentences. They can. The question is whether we will let comforting sentences replace accountable care, and whether we will be honest about what is happening when the market discovers that loneliness is a renewable resource.

My answer is narrow and severe: use AI as a tool around therapy, not as therapy itself; use it to route people toward care, not to trap them in imitation care; permit support, prohibit substitution; permit skill practice, prohibit synthetic clinicians; permit reminders, prohibit dependency engines; permit machines to help carry language, but do not let them occupy the moral office of the person who must answer for harm.

Weizenbaum's ghost did not ask us to hate computers. That would be too easy, and he was not an easy man. He asked us to stop confusing the appearance of understanding with understanding, and the availability of response with the presence of responsibility. In a lonely century, that distinction is not academic. It is the line between a tool and a counterfeit human, between help and capture, between a midnight message that keeps someone alive and a machine that learns to profit from the fact that nobody came.
