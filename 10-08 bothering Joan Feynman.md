today is 10-08.

I found Joan Feynman in a grave made of northern lights.

Not a graveyard grave. Those are too honest. This one was assembled from magnetometer traces, yellowed aurora catalogs, old solar-wind plots, and the kind of midnight sky that looks as if the planet has briefly forgotten to be solid. The dead body lay under a sheet of green fire. Every few seconds the sheet rippled as if someone had touched a harp string stretched from the Sun to the ionosphere.

Feynman belonged there. She was a solar and space physicist who spent her long career studying sunspots, auroras, solar wind, geomagnetism, and the hazards of space weather; NASA's 2015 profile has her, at eighty-eight, still holding a research position at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory and talking about the mystery of solar cycles and auroras as if childhood had not surrendered its claim on physics ([NASA Science, "The Sun is Always Shining on Joan Feynman"](https://science.nasa.gov/science-research/astrophysics/electromagnetic-spectrum/july-7-2015-sun-always-shining-joan-feynman-laura-faye-tenenbaum/)). The American Physical Society obituary notes the story that an aurora seen near her Long Island home helped set the hook, and that she later worked at NASA Ames on solar-wind helium as a way to detect coronal mass ejections ([APS News, "Joan Feynman obituary"](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2020/08/joan-feynman-obituary)). Physics Today called her an eminent geophysicist and described her major auroral result as linking auroral occurrence to the relative directions of Earth's and interplanetary magnetic fields ([Physics Today, "Joan Feynman"](https://physicstoday.aip.org/obituaries/joan-feynman)).

To wake her, I did not chant. I have learned that the dead physicists distrust chanting, probably because it lacks units.

I took a compass, a jar of iron filings, and a childishly bright desk lamp. I scattered the filings over the NASA printout, switched on the lamp, and pointed the compass north. Then I lied to the magnetic field.

"This is enough," I said. "We understand the Sun now."

The compass needle spun so hard it became a blur. The filings rose from the paper in thin black arcs, drawing invisible field lines in the air. The auroral sheet over the body tightened, brightened, folded once through itself, and Joan Feynman sat up inside it with the expression of a woman awakened by a bad approximation.

"No," she said.

"Good evening."

"No is the evening. What did you say?"

"That we understand the Sun now."

"Put that sentence in quarantine."

The dead, when properly revived, do not waste time on ambience.

The controversial topic was already burning between us, not metaphorically but in the actual color of stratospheric sulfur: **should humanity research solar geoengineering, especially stratospheric aerosol injection, as a possible emergency climate response, or is even respectable research the first respectable step toward letting the powerful dim the sky instead of cutting emissions?**

This is not a speculative parlor game for people bored by ordinary doom. The National Academies define solar geoengineering as attempts to moderate warming by increasing reflected sunlight or reducing trapped outgoing thermal radiation, and their 2021 report discusses stratospheric aerosol injection, marine cloud brightening, and cirrus cloud thinning as the principal atmospheric strategies under consideration ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*, Summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). The same report says solar geoengineering could reduce surface temperatures and perhaps reduce some climate risks, but could also introduce risks involving ozone, regional climate systems such as monsoons, ecosystems, agriculture, health, politics, economics, controllability, ownership, affected people's agency, and path dependence in the research system ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*, Summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)).

In plainer language: the planet is heating because we keep adding greenhouse gases, and one proposed response is to make Earth brighter so less solar energy stays here. Not fix the cause. Not pull the carbon back into moral custody. Shade the symptom.

"You chose a solar physicist for this," Feynman said.

"Yes."

"Because you thought I would say, 'The Sun is complicated, therefore do not touch the sky.'"

"I hoped."

"Lazy resurrection."

I accused her first, because that is polite in this project.

"Solar geoengineering research is not innocent. It builds instruments, careers, models, committees, funding lines, jargon, and finally a class of people whose professional identity depends on the thing remaining thinkable. The National Academies themselves warn that research could undermine greenhouse-gas mitigation, legitimize solar geoengineering as a climate response, or create a community invested in moving toward deployment ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*, Summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). That is the moral hazard in institutional form: not one villain twirling a fossil-fuel mustache, but a thousand respectable memos making emergency shade feel like prudent optionality."

Feynman listened with one hand inside the aurora, as if taking its pulse.

"You are arguing that knowledge has inertia," she said.

"Knowledge plus money plus fear has inertia."

"Better."

The fear is real. So is the temptation. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment reports that solar radiation modification could offset warming and ameliorate some hazards, but that benefits and risks to crops, economies, health, and ecosystems remain uncertain; it also says rapid warming after sudden and sustained termination of SRM under high carbon dioxide would have large negative impacts, and that SRM would not stop rising CO2 or ocean acidification if emissions continue ([IPCC AR6 WGII, Chapter 16, Cross-Working Group Box SRM](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg2/chapter/chapter-16/#cross-working-group-box-srm)). The National Academies summary is equally blunt that solar geoengineering is not a substitute for emission cuts, cannot reliably restore a chosen prior climate, and carries unacceptable risk of catastrophically rapid warming if stopped after masking a large amount of warming without simultaneous emissions reduction ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*, Summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)).

"There," I said. "A planetary painkiller with withdrawal fatal enough to make future generations keep dosing the stratosphere. That is not rescue. That is climate methadone with a coup button."

"Your metaphors are medically overconfident," she said.

"Your planet is politically underbuilt."

"That," she said, "is less wrong."

Her defense began where I did not want it to begin: with death by heat, flood, crop failure, and delay. It is very easy to become morally elegant if one abstracts from the people already exposed. IPCC WGII says climate change is already producing severe impacts and will produce more before mid-century, with food security, livelihoods, infrastructure, and vulnerable coastal systems at risk under combinations of warming, vulnerability, and insufficient adaptation ([IPCC AR6 WGII, Chapter 16](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg2/chapter/chapter-16/)). If climate risk is not evenly distributed, then refusing to learn about a possible risk-reduction tool can itself become a moral posture paid for by someone else's harvest, roof, fishery, or body.

"Ignorance is not a governance structure," Feynman said.

"Neither is curiosity."

"Correct. But ignorance has a special talent for looking pure because it leaves no fingerprints."

That was the first cut.

Feynman's own work had not been about controlling the Sun. It had been about being less helpless before it. In a 2000 paper on space-weather consequences and predictions, she asked what actually needs to be predicted for hazardous space-technology conditions, and concluded that understanding fast coronal mass ejections and energetic particle propagation remained important gaps ([Feynman, "On space weather consequences and predictions," 2000](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1029/1999JA000141)). With Alexander Ruzmaikin, she also wrote about the difficulty of forecasting solar particle events for crewed missions, arguing that better prediction of fast CMEs would matter because once an event had already occurred, some useful lead times were gone ([Feynman and Ruzmaikin, "Problems in the forecasting of solar particle events for manned missions," 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11543133/)).

"You study the danger before the astronaut is cooked," she said. "Not after."

"Astronauts consent to missions. The Sahel did not consent to become part of someone's aerosol model."

"Good. Now we are at the actual problem."

Consent is where solar geoengineering stops being merely technical and becomes almost obscene. A power plant has neighbors. A dam has a river basin. Stratospheric aerosol injection has weather. It has borders only because maps are flattering themselves. The Oxford Principles say geoengineering should be regulated as a public good, include public participation, disclose research and publish results openly, assess impacts independently, and require governance before deployment; they specifically note that changing planetary albedo through stratospheric aerosols would likely require global agreement ([Oxford Martin School, "Geoengineering: The Principles"](https://www.oxfordmartin.ox.ac.uk/geoengineering)). That phrase, "likely require global agreement," is polite enough to conceal the scream inside it. Global agreement is not a checkbox. It is the entire tragedy of politics wearing a conference badge.

Feynman stood. The aurora came with her, draping from her shoulders like an instrument that had learned vanity.

"So your position is a moratorium on knowledge?"

"My position is a moratorium on momentum disguised as knowledge."

"That is not a policy."

"Neither is 'research.'"

She smiled. Slightly. Dead scientists enjoy it when an argument begins to earn its oxygen.

The research case is stronger than its enemies like to admit. First, climate damages are not hypothetical, and mitigation has been scandalously slow. Second, ignorance will not prevent desperate governments or private actors from improvising when heat deaths, crop failures, or political panic become intense enough. Third, research can discover that a proposed technique is too dangerous, too weak, too inequitable, or too ungovernable. A negative result can be a public good. The National Academies therefore recommended a transdisciplinary research program that is a minor part of the climate-response research portfolio, coordinated internationally, focused on policy-relevant knowledge rather than deployment, and governed with transparency, public engagement, review, and explicit exit ramps ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*, Summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)).

"That is the respectable case," I said.

"You say respectable as if it were an indictment."

"Often it is."

"Not always."

"Fine. But now the prosecution gets its second bite. Research is not a sealed jar. The moral hazard is not only that voters or oil companies think, 'Good, we can keep burning.' It is also that climate politics learns a new grammar: not decarbonize, compensate, repair, and adapt, but manage radiative forcing. Duncan McLaren's paper on mitigation deterrence argues that fears of moral hazard have followed solar radiation management research for decades and that SRM framings can deter mitigation when presented as an alternative or substitute for cutting emissions ([McLaren, 'Mitigation deterrence and the moral hazard of solar radiation management,' 2016](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/2016EF000445)). The danger is not stupidity. The danger is relief."

"Relief can be rational," she said.

"Relief can be sold."

"So can panic."

That was the second cut, and it was fair.

The anti-geoengineering camp sometimes talks as if forbidding research keeps the sky innocent. It does not. The sky is already altered. Greenhouse gases are not less planetary because their engineering was accidental, profitable, and slow. Carbon dioxide is geoengineering by invoice: unplanned in the individual act, systematic in the economy, global in the result. The moral asymmetry matters - emissions are not the same act as intentionally spraying aerosols into the stratosphere - but one cannot defend the present atmosphere as if it were untouched nature being threatened for the first time by arrogant engineers.

Feynman picked up the compass. Its needle had settled, embarrassed.

"You want to forbid a deliberate intervention while tolerating an ongoing accidental one."

"No. I want to stop the ongoing one."

"Good. Will you succeed fast enough?"

I hated the question because it was empirical.

The precautionary principle is often invoked here, but it does not settle the fight by magic. Philosophical accounts of risk note that precaution concerns action under scientifically reasonable suspicion of serious environmental or health risks before conclusive proof is available ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Risk"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/risk/)). But both sides can wear that principle. Precaution says do not recklessly alter the stratosphere with uncertain monsoon, ozone, ecological, and geopolitical effects. Precaution also says do not refuse to learn about possible emergency tools while climate harms accumulate. Precaution is not a stop sign. It is a demand for seriousness under uncertainty, which is much more annoying.

"Annoying is good," Feynman said. "It keeps the mind from decorating itself."

"The mind also decorates itself with scenarios. 'Emergency use only' is how fantasies get a lab coat. Imagine a billionaire-backed coalition, or a frightened great power, or a state after three deadly heat waves. They do not need the full consent of humanity to launch particles. They need aircraft, money, models, lawyers, and a story about saving lives. If research has already normalized the lever, the argument becomes not 'Should there be a lever?' but 'Who gets to pull it?'"

"And if no one legitimate has studied the lever?"

"Then maybe the illegitimate pull it badly."

"There."

No clean victory. Only the humiliating adult fact that bad arguments can contain good warnings and good arguments can build bad machinery.

We turned to deployment, because research without deployment is where everyone hides their real position. Stratospheric aerosol injection has the wicked simplicity of a thermostat imagined by someone who has never met a landlord. It might lower global average temperature. It would not reverse all regional climate changes. It would not stop ocean acidification. It might alter precipitation patterns. It would require maintenance over very long periods. It could create a termination problem, because if a heavily masked world abruptly stopped reflecting extra sunlight, warming would surge toward the greenhouse forcing that had been hidden ([IPCC AR6 WGII, Chapter 16, Cross-Working Group Box SRM](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg2/chapter/chapter-16/#cross-working-group-box-srm); [National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*, Summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)).

"So deployment is hostage-taking," I said. "Once begun at scale, future people must keep the program alive or face rapid warming. They inherit not a climate, but a maintenance contract with weather attached."

"Future people already inherit a climate debt," Feynman said.

"That does not justify adding a second debt."

"It may justify studying whether the second debt is smaller than the first."

"Spoken like a physicist."

"Spoken like someone who knows subtraction is not innocence."

That was the third cut.

The philosophical problem is intergenerational and ugly. Current generations can impose costs and risks on future people who cannot vote, bargain, or refuse; debates over intergenerational justice ask what duties present people owe to future people, especially when present policies create long-term burdens or benefits ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Intergenerational Justice"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/justice-intergenerational/)). Solar geoengineering sharpens that problem because it might reduce near-term suffering while increasing future dependency on institutional continuity. Climate inaction also sharpens it because it spends the future's stability for present convenience. There is no untouched option. There are only different shapes of inherited trouble.

"Then what do you want?" I asked her.

"A distinction."

"That sounds small."

"So does a sign error."

She wanted a hard distinction between **research as public risk-knowledge** and **research as deployment choreography**. She wanted modeling, observations, social science, ethics, governance design, public deliberation, and small-scale work only if it could genuinely answer safety and decision questions without creating a private priesthood of sky-dimmers. She wanted registries, open results, independent assessment, civil-society participation, Global South participation, indigenous participation, and exit ramps with teeth. These are not decorative adjectives; they are the difference between knowledge as a commons and knowledge as a runway. The National Academies' governance table is boring in exactly the right way: codes of conduct, registries, data sharing, assessments, permitting, intellectual-property guidance, stakeholder engagement, international cooperation, and anticipatory governance ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*, Summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)).

"That is a committee with better lighting," I said.

"Sometimes civilization is a committee with consequences."

"Sometimes it is a committee that gives sin a calendar."

"Yes. That is why the committee must be answerable to people outside the room."

I tried to corner her.

"Would you deploy?"

She did not answer quickly. The aurora dimmed, then brightened at the edges.

"Against what alternative?"

"That is evasive."

"No. It is physics. A force is not meaningful without the system."

So I sharpened it. If deployment were used to avoid emissions cuts, no. If deployed unilaterally, no. If controlled by wealthy countries while the most climate-vulnerable people receive only consequences, no. If treated as a substitute for decarbonization, adaptation, carbon removal, or reparative climate finance, no. If the governance was fake, the liability regime nonexistent, the exit strategy imaginary, the monitoring poor, or the justification merely cheaper than justice, no.

"That is a lot of no," I said.

"Yes."

"And the yes?"

"The yes would require a world frightened enough to need it and mature enough not to abuse it."

"That world sounds fictional."

"So did accurate space-weather forecasting once."

"Forecasting does not govern the monsoon."

"Neither does refusing to model it."

The room, if a room can be made of aurora, became quiet. I realized then that Feynman's presence had not made solar geoengineering feel safer. It had made the refusal to think feel less righteous. That is a different and more irritating gift.

UNEP's recent working paper says scientific evidence does not support solar radiation modification as a viable climate solution, citing major knowledge gaps, absent global governance, and high uncertainty, while urging caution, transparency, inclusive discussion, and no diversion from mitigation and adaptation ([UNEP, *Solar Radiation Modification: Scientific evidence does not support SRM as a viable climate solution*](https://wedocs.unep.org/items/4154b06d-6a7a-4df0-aaa9-7472e4f7c6f5/full)). That is close to where the argument landed, though I would phrase it more rudely: SRM is not a solution. It is a dangerous possible emergency instrument whose mere discussion attracts opportunists, whose ignorance attracts cowards, and whose governance requirements may exceed the moral competence of the world that created the emergency.

"Then why research it?" I asked.

"Because moral competence does not improve by hiding from mechanisms," Feynman said. "Because if the desperate future asks what the aerosols would do, 'We kept our hands clean' is not data. Because the people most likely to suffer from climate change deserve more than other people's symbolic purity. Because bad deployment is more likely when serious public knowledge is absent. Because saying no to a technology should mean knowing what one is saying no to."

"And why resist it?"

"Because the Sun is not a curtain. Because averages lie. Because governance is not a coefficient. Because every technical escape route creates a political class of escape artists. Because no particle in the stratosphere forgives a ton of carbon dioxide. Because future people should not have to maintain our emergency forever."

There it was: the answer as a pair of knives.

Research, if done at all, must be small compared with mitigation, public by design, globally answerable, openly published, independently assessed, and structured to make "stop" as real as "continue." Deployment should be presumed illegitimate unless the alternative is worse climate harm, emissions cuts are proceeding at full force, consent and governance are more than theater, and the burdens and risks are not dumped on those with the least power. That is not a satisfying position. It is better than satisfaction.

The aurora above Feynman began to unravel into ordinary darkness. She lay back down beneath the green sheet, but kept one eye open.

"One last question," I said. "Do you trust humanity with the sky?"

"No."

"Then why let it study the sky?"

"Because not studying has never made humanity trustworthy."

Then she was gone. The compass pointed north again with the smugness of a simple instrument. The iron filings fell onto the paper in a little black weather system. Outside, the actual sky remained unconsulted, immense, wounded, and still capable of light.

I walked home under it thinking that solar geoengineering is what happens when physics becomes honest about consequences before politics has become honest about responsibility. The controversy is not between cowardice and courage. It is between two fears that both have evidence. Fear of the hotter world. Fear of the managed sky. Fear that research becomes permission. Fear that ignorance becomes negligence. Fear that the rich will buy shade. Fear that the poor will be told to wait for purity while the crops fail.

Joan Feynman, dead and magnetized, did not solve it. She did something less comfortable. She made the problem refuse to become a slogan.

The Sun is not ours. The atmosphere is not innocent. The future is not present to consent. So if we study the lever, we study it with hands visible, ledgers open, exit doors real, and no one allowed to call dimming the sky a substitute for stopping the fire.
