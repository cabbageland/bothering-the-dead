today is May 26, 2026.

I found Georges Canguilhem in the cemetery at Cambo-les-Bains, which seemed fittingly quiet for a philosopher who spent so much of his life asking medicine whether its confidence had outrun its wisdom ([Find a Grave, "Georges Canguilhem"](https://www.findagrave.com/memorial/8879061/georges-canguilhem); [Encyclopaedia Britannica, "Georges Canguilhem"](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Georges-Canguilhem)). I brought no flowers. Flowers would have implied gratitude, and I was not there to thank him. I was there to make him work.

I took with me a prenatal testing consent packet from an American hospital, a printout of the World Health Organization constitution, the text of the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities, and a few pages summarizing Canguilhem's argument that life is not a passive object to be measured against a fixed template but an active source of norms, values, and ways of inhabiting an environment ([WHO Constitution](https://www.who.int/about/governance/constitution); [UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities](https://www.un.org/esa/socdev/enable/rights/convtexte.htm); [Giuseppe Bianco, "Reasoning in Life: Values and Normativity in Georges Canguilhem"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7678399/)). Then I laid the packet on his grave and said the question plainly enough to wake anyone worth waking.

Should prenatal screening for conditions such as Down syndrome be treated as an ordinary medical good, a neutral expansion of reproductive choice, or should we say more honestly that it often functions as a eugenic sorting practice, one that quietly teaches a society which lives it is less willing to receive?

The dirt opened not dramatically but impatiently. Canguilhem emerged like a doctor who had been interrupted in the middle of correcting a category error. He looked first at the consent packet. Then at me. Then at the WHO text, where health is famously defined as "a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity" ([WHO Constitution](https://www.who.int/about/governance/constitution)). He gave that sentence the expression one reserves for an intelligent lie that has had too successful a career.

The controversy itself is not theoretical. Down syndrome is caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21. The CDC notes that it affects how a person's body and brain develop, that it is the most common chromosomal condition diagnosed in the United States, and that screening tests during pregnancy can indicate whether a pregnancy has a higher chance of being affected, while diagnostic tests can more definitively detect the condition ([CDC, "Down Syndrome"](https://www.cdc.gov/birth-defects/about/down-syndrome.html)). The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists says prenatal genetic screening and diagnostic testing should be discussed with and offered to all pregnant patients, and that counseling should include both the benefits and limits of screening and diagnostic options ([ACOG, "Current ACOG Guidance"](https://www.acog.org/advocacy/policy-priorities/non-invasive-prenatal-testing/current-acog-guidance)). So the live question is not whether the screening regime exists. It does. The question is what moral world it builds.

Canguilhem brushed dirt from his sleeves and answered with immediate hostility to my framing. Ordinary medical good, he said, is already a compromised phrase. Good for whom? Ordinary by whose standard? Medical in service of what conception of life? He had spent a career resisting the temptation to imagine that the normal is simply a statistical average or a bureaucratic benchmark. As one account of his work puts it, Canguilhem treats living beings as normative, not merely measurable: they do not just fit norms but produce ways of valuing and negotiating their worlds ([Bianco, "Reasoning in Life"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7678399/)). If that is right, then a fetus is not merely a biological forecast, and disability is not exhausted by deviation from a chart.

I told him that this was elegant but evasive. Real parents are not arguing in a graduate seminar. They are being told, often early in pregnancy, that a screening test can reveal elevated risk for a condition associated with developmental disability and, in some cases, serious medical complications including congenital heart defects, hearing loss, and sleep apnea ([CDC, "Down Syndrome"](https://www.cdc.gov/birth-defects/about/down-syndrome.html)). They may already have children. They may have little money, little family help, bad insurance, and a rational fear that the state praising inclusion in brochures will abandon them in practice. Under those conditions, prenatal information can look less like eugenics than like preparation, or even mercy.

He did not dismiss that. To his credit, he became more severe, not less. A philosophy of life that cannot survive contact with hardship is decorative. Of course parents fear suffering, he said. Of course medicine should describe risks honestly. Of course some conditions bring pain, surgeries, shortened life, or dependence that families may feel unable to shoulder. But none of that settles the deeper matter. The social order presents itself twice in this scene: first as counselor, then as alibi. It offers screening in the name of informed choice, while leaving families to calculate their choices inside an environment structured by inadequate support, stigma, inaccessible institutions, and economic terror. Then, when many choose against disability, the same order calls the aggregate outcome private preference.

That was sharp because it was true. The Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities insists that disability results from the interaction between impairments and social and attitudinal barriers, not from the body alone, and it grounds disability rights in dignity, equality, participation, and individual autonomy ([CRPD Preamble and Principles](https://www.un.org/esa/socdev/enable/rights/convtexte.htm)). A society can therefore manufacture part of the burden it later treats as a reason for avoiding certain births. If schools exclude, employers discriminate, public benefits humiliate, and care work is abandoned to private desperation, then a test marketed as neutral information arrives inside a rigged moral economy.

Still, I refused to let him glide too easily into righteous indictment. Reproductive autonomy matters too. It matters not abstractly but because pregnancy commandeers a person's body, future, labor, and finances in a way no state or philosopher gets to romanticize from the sidelines. To say that declining to continue a pregnancy affected by disability is always expressive of contempt would be cruel and false. Some prospective parents think they are not equipped for a lifetime of care. Some fear not disability in the abstract but specific suffering. Some do not want to be conscripted into a social heroism that policy makers praise and never materially support. A regime that forces birth in the name of anti-eugenics may simply turn disabled children and exhausted parents into instruments of somebody else's moral cleanliness.

Canguilhem nodded at that, annoyingly calm. He said the easiest way to lose this argument is to confuse two distinct questions. One is whether the state should coerce pregnant people into continuing pregnancies. His answer was no, and mine was no. The other is whether a medical culture should organize itself around the systematic early detection of traits so that some future lives become increasingly available for preemptive refusal. That second question remains morally open even if the first is settled in favor of legal choice.

Then he went for the throat. Modern medicine, he said, loves to describe itself as the servant of health while smuggling in a political picture of which forms of life count as acceptable. The WHO constitution's definition of health as complete physical, mental, and social well-being sounds generous, but Canguilhem thought definitions like that risk making normality tyrannical. If health means completeness, then actual human beings, who are finite, fragile, adaptive, and uneven, are always one evaluation away from deficit. In his tradition, the pathological is not simply any departure from ideal form; it is a reduction in a living being's capacity to establish norms and contend with its world ([WHO Constitution](https://www.who.int/about/governance/constitution); [Bianco, "Reasoning in Life"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7678399/)). The difference matters. A body may differ without being a mistake.

I answered that Down syndrome is not merely difference in the harmless sense. To insist on that would be sentimental. Some people with Down syndrome live with serious health complications. Many need substantial support. Developmental disability can impose durable constraints, not just socially manufactured inconveniences ([CDC, "Down Syndrome"](https://www.cdc.gov/birth-defects/about/down-syndrome.html)). If medicine cannot identify foreseeable burdens before birth, then what exactly is prenatal care for? It already screens for neural tube defects, chromosomal conditions, and other anomalies because people want information before irreversible obligations harden around them. Saying this out loud is not Nazism. Sometimes it is just moral seriousness about dependency, pain, and unequal capacity.

He said the invocation of Nazism is often a way of making the issue lurid enough to avoid its ordinary operation. Contemporary liberal eugenics, he said, does not march in boots. It arrives as counseling, probability, reassurance, and consumer-friendly choice architecture. It does not need to coerce if it can teach expectation. Once a society treats the nonexistence of people with a given condition as a public health success, it has already made an evaluative judgment about which lives are more welcome. That judgment may be softened by compassion, but it does not disappear.

Here he was strongest. The moral force of the disability critique is not that every termination following prenatal diagnosis is an act of hatred. Usually it is not. The force of the critique is structural. If screening is expanded, normalized, routinized, and discussed mainly in the language of burden prevention, then the background message becomes hard to miss: responsible parenthood means using available knowledge to avoid the birth of lives that will predictably require more adaptation from family and society. Responsibility becomes anticipatory filtration.

I pushed back with the hardest case. Suppose a woman receives a high-risk screening result, confirms trisomy 21 through diagnostic testing, already has one medically complex child, works two jobs, has no partner, and knows exactly how pitiless American care systems can be. Are you really prepared, I asked him, to tell her that choosing not to continue the pregnancy participates in a eugenic logic rather than a sober assessment of what she can actually bear?

He was quiet longer than I expected. Then he said yes, but not as accusation. As diagnosis.

That answer needed defending, and he defended it carefully. To say an action participates in a eugenic logic is not to say the person performing it is monstrous. It is to say her choice has been formatted by a social order that distributes support so badly, and imagines normal life so narrowly, that the disappearance of certain possible children comes to seem prudent. The point of naming eugenic structure is not to condemn vulnerable choosers. It is to condemn the arrangement that hands them a brutal decision and then flatters itself for respecting autonomy.

That distinction matters, and yet it does not finish the matter either. Because the pregnant person is not merely a site where society reveals its sins. She is a moral agent whose consent, fear, exhaustion, and future are all real. Anti-eugenic rhetoric becomes obscene when it treats her as a vessel whose task is to redeem collective prejudice by carrying every possible pregnancy to term. One can criticize the screening regime without making martyrs out of women. One can say that selective avoidance of disability sends a damaging social signal while still defending the legal and moral primacy of the pregnant person's decision.

Canguilhem accepted most of that, which made the argument more interesting, not less. We were no longer fighting over whether choice matters. We were fighting over what deserves critique when choice is exercised inside institutions built to rank forms of life. He said the central fraud is the phrase neutral information. There is no view from nowhere in medicine. To decide what to test for, when to test, how to frame results, which outcomes to call positive, and what follow-up pathways to make frictionless is already to embed values in a clinical apparatus. Offer enough screenings under enough pressure and soon the optional begins to behave like the expected.

The ACOG materials themselves show why this is complicated. They emphasize counseling, patient choice, the distinction between screening and diagnosis, and the need to explain benefits and limitations rather than treating one path as mandatory ([ACOG, "Current ACOG Guidance"](https://www.acog.org/advocacy/policy-priorities/non-invasive-prenatal-testing/current-acog-guidance)). That is not nothing. It is an effort at ethical restraint. But ethical restraint in counseling does not by itself answer the wider political question of what a health system is trying to optimize. Lower suffering? Lower cost? Lower incidence of certain traits? More parental control over reproductive futures? These aims can overlap, but they are not the same.

By then the air around the grave had gone cold in the precise, clinical way that means an argument has stopped being symbolic. My own conclusion, after bothering Georges Canguilhem with an American consent packet at midnight, is this: prenatal screening for Down syndrome should remain legally available, but it should not be described as a morally neutral public good. It is a practice with eugenic pressure built into it, even when no individual participant is acting from hatred and even when the choice to terminate remains, in many cases, morally defensible.

That is the position people dislike because it refuses the consolations on both sides. It refuses the pro-choice fantasy that medical selection can be value-free so long as nobody is literally forced. And it refuses the anti-eugenic fantasy that one can answer structural injustice by requisitioning pregnant people into reproductive witness. The right response is harder. Preserve reproductive freedom. Strip the counseling process of triumphalist assumptions about normality. Require genuinely balanced information, including testimony and evidence about the lives of disabled people. Build a society in which raising a disabled child is not an invitation to economic ruin. And say openly that a culture which steadily expands its capacity to screen out certain futures is making a statement about what kinds of dependence it is willing to love.

The stakes are not small. They concern what medicine is for. If medicine exists only to reduce deviation from a preferred model of functioning, then prenatal screening will increasingly become a technology of population editing, however softly administered. But if medicine also owes loyalty to the plurality of human forms and to the conditions under which different kinds of people can live, then it must look much harder at the norms it quietly installs while claiming merely to inform.

Canguilhem, already sinking back toward the ground, gave me one last sentence before the soil closed over his shoes.

A society reveals its idea of health not only by whom it treats, but by whom it learns not to expect.

Then he was gone. The consent packet stayed on the grave, damp with night air, looking suddenly less like paperwork than like a small constitutional document for the species. That is why the dispute over prenatal screening is so volatile. It is not only about one test, one pregnancy, or one family's fear. It is about whether modern medicine will understand itself as the art of accompanying vulnerable life, or as the increasingly polite management of which lives need never arrive.
