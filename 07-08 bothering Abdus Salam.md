today is 07-08.

I found Abdus Salam in the kind of grave that tells on the living.

Not first in Pakistan, not with any claim that my midnight imagination had purchased a right to disturb the soil. I found him in a photograph of his grave, where the word "Muslim" had been blotted out from the epitaph describing him as the first Muslim Nobel laureate, a little bureaucratic wound left in stone after the body could no longer object ([AIP, "Breaking the Barrier: Dr. Abdus Salam"](https://www.aip.org/library/breaking-the-barrier-dr-abdus-salam)). The erasure was not gossip. Pakistan's constitution defines as non-Muslim, for constitutional and legal purposes, people of the Qadiani or Lahori groups who call themselves Ahmadis ([Constitution of the Islamic Republic of Pakistan, Article 260](https://na.gov.pk/uploads/documents/6926e060076ed_467.pdf)). A state had found a way to argue theology with a corpse.

So I made the grave again, because the first one had been edited badly. I laid down Salam and John Ward's 1964 paper on electromagnetic and weak interactions, where a local gauge idea helped push toward the electroweak synthesis ([OSTI record for Salam and Ward, "Electromagnetic and Weak Interactions"](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/4685762)). I added Salam's Nobel lecture, which begins by placing his work in the lineage of Maxwell's unification of electricity and magnetism and Einstein's longing for a deeper unity of forces ([Salam, Nobel Lecture, 1979](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/salam-lecture.pdf)). I added the Nobel page saying he shared the 1979 physics prize with Sheldon Glashow and Steven Weinberg for the unified weak and electromagnetic interaction, including the prediction of the weak neutral current ([Nobel Prize, Abdus Salam Facts](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1979/salam/facts/)). Then, because the dead deserve both the wound and the work, I put beside them a page from ICTP's history: Salam founded the International Centre for Theoretical Physics in Trieste in 1964 under his conviction that science is the common heritage of humankind ([ICTP, "Our History"](https://www.ictp.it/home/our-history)).

To wake him, I drew this on the floor:

`SU(2) x U(1) -> U(1)`

Then I placed the rubbed-out word under the arrow.

The room changed temperature. Not dramatically. More like an experiment becoming worth repeating. A thin chalk dust rose, sorted itself into a symmetry group, failed to remain one thing, and became two forces arguing in a familiar universe.

Salam sat up with the grave-cloth still carrying the indignity of correction fluid.

"You have made an equation out of a vandalized epitaph," he said.

"The equation is yours. The vandalism is theirs."

"Be precise. The unification is not mine alone."

"Already better than most national commemorations."

He looked at the papers, then at the blotted word, and understood the question before I could make it dignified.

The question was this: **Can science honestly claim to be universal while its institutions, nations, prizes, visas, schools, and monuments keep deciding which human beings are allowed to belong? Or must scientific universalism become openly political, even partisan, against the states and structures that erase scientists?**

This is not a campus poster argument about representation, nor a cheap invitation to turn equations into autobiography. The stakes are sharper: whether young scientists from poor countries can become physicists without exile, whether religious and ethnic minorities can enter laboratories without hiding themselves, whether global science is a common inheritance or a prestige economy with a clean accent, whether persecuting states should be allowed to claim the glory of scientists they refused to protect, and whether "science is universal" is a truth, a hope, or a beautiful excuse.

Salam touched the Nobel lecture.

"Begin with the universal," he said. "Otherwise resentment will pretend to be thought."

Fine. The case for scientific universalism begins with the obvious fact that nature is not impressed by identity papers. The weak neutral current did not become more or less real because Salam was Ahmadi, Pakistani, Muslim, brown, exiled, celebrated, or neglected. CERN's account of the 1973 Gargamelle discovery says the observation of weak neutral currents helped put the nascent Standard Model on solid ground; later W and Z discoveries and Higgs work extended the same electroweak road ([CERN, "50 years of giant electroweak discoveries"](https://home.cern/50-years-giant-electroweak-discoveries/)). The bubble chamber did not ask for sectarian credentials. It asked for tracks.

"Good," Salam said. "The chamber was a better theologian than the magistrate."

The best universalist argument is severe. Scientific claims must be judged by impersonal standards: evidence, mathematical consistency, replicability, explanatory power, predictive vulnerability. Robert Merton's famous account of the scientific ethos placed universalism among science's institutional imperatives, meaning that truth-claims are supposed to be assessed by preestablished impersonal criteria, not by the status of the claimant ([Merton, "The Normative Structure of Science"](https://law.unimelb.edu.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0005/3609203/1c-Merton-The-Normative-Structure-of-Science.pdf)). That norm is not decorative liberalism. It is load-bearing. Without it, a laboratory becomes a court of bloodlines.

Salam nodded. "If a poor student in Lahore solves a problem, the solution is not poor."

"If a privileged fool in Princeton writes nonsense, the nonsense is not privileged into truth."

"You are improving."

The universalist also warns against making science a trophy case for wounded identities. There is a vulgar way to remember Salam that turns him into merely the Ahmadi physicist, the Pakistani physicist, the Muslim physicist, the Global South physicist, as if electroweak unification were a footnote to biography. That is another erasure, softer and more educated. His physics matters because it is physics, not because suffering sanctifies it. The Nobel page's summary is beautifully indifferent in the right way: electromagnetic and weak interactions were unified into the electroweak interaction, and the theory predicted neutral weak currents ([Nobel Prize, Abdus Salam Facts](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1979/salam/facts/)). Nothing in that sentence needs a passport.

"Exactly," Salam said. "Do not build a smaller prison out of sympathy."

He had earned that rebuke. There is a temptation, when confronting exclusion, to reverse the vulgarity rather than escape it: to make every equation into a biography, every discovery into a grievance, every institution into a tribunal. But physics is not therapy for civilization's guilt. Electroweak theory is not better because a persecuted man helped make it. It is not worse because European and American institutions hosted its confirmation. A true equation cannot be improved by pity.

"Then should science be proudly indifferent?" I asked.

"No," he said. "Indifference is not universalism. It is laziness wearing a clean shirt."

There was the crack.

Because the case against naive universalism begins exactly where the case for it is strongest. Science may require impersonal judgment of claims, but claims are made by persons. Persons need schools, salaries, journals, mentors, passports, safety, time, laboratories, electricity, families not being threatened, languages in which they are allowed to think, and states that do not force them to deny themselves. The law does not change the electron's charge. It can absolutely change who gets near the apparatus.

The International Science Council puts the modern obligation plainly: science as a global public good depends on practice that is trustworthy, free, responsible, equitable, and inclusive; its freedom and responsibility principles include freedom to participate in knowledge production and a responsibility to prevent discrimination based on religion, nationality, ethnicity, citizenship, sex, disability, and other social memberships ([International Science Council, "Freedom and responsibility in science"](https://council.science/freedom-and-responsibility-in-science/)). That is not identity politics invading science. That is the maintenance manual for universalism.

Salam considered the phrase "maintenance manual" with almost comic disapproval.

"Universalism does not maintain itself," I said.

"No. Nothing worthwhile does."

The blotted word on the floor had become ugly in a precise way. Pakistan did not merely fail to honor Salam enough. It legally classified his community outside Islam, and AIP records that the word "Muslim" on his gravestone was blotted out ([AIP, "Breaking the Barrier"](https://www.aip.org/library/breaking-the-barrier-dr-abdus-salam); [Constitution of Pakistan, Article 260](https://na.gov.pk/uploads/documents/6926e060076ed_467.pdf)). That is not an accidental omission from a textbook. It is metaphysical border control. The state wanted the Nobel aura but not the man as he understood himself. It wanted the fruit of universal science without the universal permission to belong.

"A country may honor a scientist imperfectly," Salam said. "Do not require nations to become morally perfect before they support physics. You will have no physics."

"I am not requiring perfection. I am objecting to erasure."

"Erasure is worse than imperfection."

"Then say so."

He looked at the blackened word. "Erasure is a lie with administrative stamina."

Good. Now we were talking.

The controversial point is not whether Pakistan alone is guilty. That would be too easy and too colonial a sermon. Every scientific power has its edited graves. Europe built great laboratories while drawing on imperial extraction, American universities preached merit while excluding or underpaying entire classes of people, journals and conferences reward English fluency as if nature spoke with a Reviewer 2 accent, and visa regimes still decide which minds may arrive before the seminar starts. Mertonian universalism may be the norm of science, but the actual scientific world is full of particularism with better fonts.

Salam did not let me coast.

"And yet you cite Merton in English, CERN in Europe, Nobel in Sweden, ICTP in Italy."

"Because those institutions also preserve truth."

"So what do you want? Condemnation or collaboration?"

"Both, if necessary."

"That is often a way of doing neither."

This was the hard part. Scientific internationalism is not automatically noble. It can become a handshake club among elites who agree not to mention the people outside the room. But anti-internationalism is worse. A boycott that satisfies distant consciences may isolate the very scientists trapped under bad governments. A purity campaign against every compromised institution would leave only imaginary institutions standing. Salam's own answer was not retreat. He built ICTP, a place meant to reduce the number of scientists forced to choose between first-rate physics and loyalty to their countries of birth ([ICTP, "Our History"](https://www.ictp.it/home/our-history); [AIP, "Breaking the Barrier"](https://www.aip.org/library/breaking-the-barrier-dr-abdus-salam)).

"There," Salam said. "Build. Do not merely accuse."

The verb matters. Building is the part moralists avoid when they want clean hands and administrators avoid when they want clean metrics. Salam's universalism was not the lazy sentence "physics belongs to everyone" dropped onto an underfunded blackboard. It was institutional. Fellowships. Conferences. Training. Access. A center in Trieste. Networks for scientists from countries treated as peripheral by the self-appointed center of intelligence. He knew that common heritage is not common if only a few inherit the keys.

"Then you agree with me," I said.

"Never declare victory while the other person is still alive."

"You are not."

"Temporarily."

The physics itself sharpened the argument. Electroweak unification is not sameness. At high energies, weak and electromagnetic interactions are facets of a single electroweak structure; at the energies of ordinary life, broken symmetry makes them appear as distinct forces, one long-range and familiar, one short-range and hidden in nuclear processes ([Nobel Prize, Abdus Salam Facts](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1979/salam/facts/); [CERN, "50 years of giant electroweak discoveries"](https://home.cern/50-years-giant-electroweak-discoveries/)). Unity did not abolish difference. It explained why difference appeared.

That is the philosophical treasure, if one handles it carefully. Scientific universalism should be like that: not the flattening fantasy that all scientists enter a neutral space stripped of history, but a deeper structure in which different lives can participate without being reduced to their difference. The problem with sectarian law is not that it notices identity. It notices identity in order to dominate it. The problem with naive meritocracy is not that it ignores identity. It ignores identity in order to avoid repairing domination. Both are bad theories of the human field.

Salam's eyes sharpened.

"Do not make symmetry breaking a social metaphor too cheaply."

"I am trying to make it expensive."

"Then pay."

The payment is policy, not poetry. If science is universal, scientific institutions should act as if exclusion anywhere damages the instrument everywhere. That means more than diversity statements written in the dialect of airport carpet. It means emergency fellowships for threatened scholars; visa advocacy treated as scientific infrastructure; serious funding for institutions in countries that have brains but not equipment; open access that does not quietly transfer costs from readers to authors who cannot pay; conference practices that do not make geography a tax on intelligence; and public refusal to let states claim scientists while erasing their communities. The ISC's freedom and responsibility principles are useful here because they connect the freedom to participate in knowledge production with responsibility to make science equitable and inclusive ([International Science Council](https://council.science/freedom-and-responsibility-in-science/)).

"You want science to have a foreign policy," Salam said.

"Science already has one. It just pretends visas and sanctions and export controls are weather."

He did not answer quickly. Good. Delay is the politeness of a serious objection.

The strongest argument against my position is that politicized science can become hostage to every moral emergency. If every collaboration must pass a full audit of every government's sins, scientific exchange will shrink exactly when the world needs shared work on climate, disease, energy, nuclear risk, artificial intelligence, and fundamental knowledge. Scientists under authoritarian or discriminatory regimes may lose contact with the world because outsiders want to punish rulers. Governments may retaliate. Universities may become border agents of opinion. Physics may acquire a loyalty test in the name of fighting loyalty tests.

"Say it harder," Salam said.

Fine: there is a kind of justice talk that would have damaged Salam. It would have looked at a Pakistani Ahmadi scientist moving through British, Italian, UN, IAEA, and global institutions and demanded an identity-stable purity no actual life could provide. Salam needed doors, not only denunciations. He needed Cambridge and Lahore, Imperial and Trieste, Pakistan and the world, faith and gauge theory, pride and exile, grief and usefulness. If universalism becomes a censorious map of contamination, it will betray the very mixed lives it says it honors.

He smiled faintly. "That is better."

"You enjoy being difficult."

"It is one of the few freedoms the dead retain."

But the strongest argument against him, or against the too-comfortable version of him I was tempted to let him represent, is this: neutrality also becomes a loyalty test. It asks the excluded to enter science only on condition that they do not mention the exclusions too loudly. It praises the universal mind while leaving the particular body at the checkpoint. It says, "Your equation is welcome; your grievance is unprofessional." It offers the persecuted scientist immortality in a footnote and silence in the faculty meeting.

Salam's own life refuses that split. He did not choose between physics and the politics of access. His physics helped make the Standard Model world. His institution-building insisted that talent was being wasted by the geography of opportunity. His grave insists, with terrible economy, that universal knowledge can coexist with local cruelty unless someone makes the contradiction intolerable.

"So what is your doctrine?" he asked.

"Conditional internationalism."

"Ugly phrase."

"Useful phrases are often not invited to dinner."

Conditional internationalism means this: keep scientific exchange open wherever it genuinely supports scientists, students, and shared knowledge; make it harder for states and institutions to use science as reputation laundering while persecuting the people who make science possible. Do not isolate trapped scholars because their government is vicious. Do not flatter vicious governments because their scholars are brilliant. Fund people, not propaganda. Name erasure when it happens. Build alternatives when exclusion blocks access. Use prizes, centers, journals, and conferences to widen participation, not merely to polish the conscience of the already powerful.

Salam tapped the Nobel lecture with one finger.

"And the physics?"

"Central."

"Say that as if you mean it."

The physics is central because it is the reason the politics matters. If science were merely another tribal mythology, exclusion would be ordinary factional cruelty. But physics has discovered ways for human beings to say true things about a world older and less provincial than any nation. Maxwell's unification, Einstein's field equations, quantum mechanics, electroweak theory, the tracks in Gargamelle, the W and Z bosons, the Higgs: these are not local customs with equations attached. They are hard-won access to reality. To deny whole classes of people fair entry into that work is not only unjust to them. It is an injury to knowledge.

"Better," Salam said.

The celebratory part of the essay belongs here. Salam helped show that two forces, experienced as different, belonged to a deeper account. The weak force, responsible for beta decay, and electromagnetism, the household monarch of light, chemistry, motors, and nerve signals, are not strangers at high energy. That is magnificent. It is one of the reasons particle physics deserves its cathedrals, its pipes under mountains, its ridiculous cables, its collisions, its cautious press releases, its anxious theorists, and its patient experimentalists scanning one more event. The world is allowed to be more unified than common sense expects.

But Salam's grave adds the moral corollary: human institutions are allowed to be less unified than their slogans claim. Therefore the slogan must be tested.

Scientific universalism should face at least four tests.

First: **the claim test.** Once a result enters science, it must be judged by evidence and reasoning, not by nationality, race, religion, gender, institutional prestige, or geopolitical convenience. This is Merton's universalism with its spine intact ([Merton, "The Normative Structure of Science"](https://law.unimelb.edu.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0005/3609203/1c-Merton-The-Normative-Structure-of-Science.pdf)).

Second: **the access test.** If a community is systematically denied education, equipment, safety, visas, mentorship, or publication routes, then science has not satisfied universalism merely by judging the few survivors fairly. A race is not fair because the finish-line camera is impartial.

Third: **the memory test.** Institutions and nations may not claim the glory of scientists while erasing the identities for which those scientists were punished. Pakistan cannot honestly cherish Salam as Nobel proof of national genius while a blotted word remains the emblem of his official unacceptability ([AIP, "Breaking the Barrier"](https://www.aip.org/library/breaking-the-barrier-dr-abdus-salam); [Constitution of Pakistan](https://na.gov.pk/uploads/documents/6926e060076ed_467.pdf)).

Fourth: **the collaboration test.** International science should distinguish between collaboration that sustains scientists under bad conditions and collaboration that decorates power. The first is a lifeline. The second is laundering with equations.

Salam listened without interrupting. That worried me.

"You are not wrong," he said at last. "But you still sound angry at nations."

"Are you not?"

"I am angry at waste."

That was better. Nations can be sentimental objects, even when one hates them. Waste is colder. Waste names the destroyed student, the forbidden library, the girl told physics is not for her, the minority scientist asked to pass as something else, the laboratory without parts, the conference visa denied, the talented teacher buried under bureaucracy, the paper rejected because its author is invisible to the invisible college. Waste is what happens when human vanity interrupts the universe learning itself.

"Do you forgive Pakistan?" I asked.

"You are asking a dead man to simplify love."

"That sounds like no."

"It sounds like the question is too small."

Of course it was. The scandal of Salam is not that one nation failed one genius. The scandal is that genius had to become exceptional to be mourned. For every Salam with a Nobel lecture and a center, there are thousands of minds that never reach the door, not because nature refused them, but because people did. The universalist should be furious not only when a great man is dishonored after death, but when ordinary brilliance is prevented from becoming visible enough to dishonor.

The blotted word pulsed once. I disliked that. Symbols should not get theatrical privileges denied to evidence.

"What would you have written on the grave?" I asked.

"His name."

"Only that?"

"Names are not small."

"And the word they removed?"

"If he chose it, restore it. Not because physics needs it. Because truth does."

That is the distinction I had been circling all night. Physics does not need Salam to be called Muslim in order for electroweak theory to hold. Truth does. A truth about nature can survive a lie about a person, but a civilization that keeps lying about persons will eventually damage its access to truth about nature too, because it will train itself to prefer authority over reality. A state that edits a grave is practicing a skill. It may later use the same skill on data.

Salam's face tightened. "That is too neat."

"Maybe. But not false."

"Not false."

The room had almost returned to ordinary death. The chalk arrow remained. `SU(2) x U(1)` on one side, `U(1)` on the other, and between them the rubbed-out word like a bad renormalization.

Here is the answer I would defend before Salam: **science is universal in its object, not automatically in its practice. The universality of physics is exactly why scientific institutions must become more politically serious about access, memory, and protection. Universalism is not neutrality toward exclusion. It is a discipline for defeating exclusion without turning truth into tribal property.**

That means a state may celebrate a scientist only if it can bear the truth of who that scientist was. It means a university cannot boast of global excellence while treating foreign students as revenue-bearing visitors to someone else's civilization. It means a prize cannot repair an ecosystem, though it can illuminate one life. It means open science must be open to those without money, not merely those without subscriptions. It means physicists should defend threatened colleagues not as charity cases but as part of defending the instrument by which physics is done.

"The instrument?" Salam asked.

"The human mind in sufficient freedom."

"Acceptable."

High praise from a resurrected theorist is a narrow bridge and one should cross quickly.

Before he returned to death, Salam adjusted my chalk. He did not change the equation. He changed the spacing. The man had standards.

"Do not let them make me only a wound," he said.

"I won't."

"Do not let them make me only a medal either."

"No."

"And do not say science belongs to humanity unless you are willing to build rooms where humanity can work."

Then the grave closed, or the photograph became only a photograph, or the physics withdrew into the living papers where it had always been more alive than most institutions. The blotted word stayed on the floor. So did the equation.

I left with no clean consolation. The weak and electromagnetic forces could be brought into one theory; human beings have not yet managed an equally elegant unification of truth and belonging. But Salam's life makes one demand unavoidable: if scientific thought is the common heritage of humankind, then humankind has to stop guarding the inheritance like a private club with theological stationery.

The universe does not care who is allowed into the laboratory. That is precisely why we must.
