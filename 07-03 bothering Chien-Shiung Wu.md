today is 07-03.

I found Chien-Shiung Wu where her body had refused the jurisdiction of a single country.

The National Park Service says Wu died of a stroke in New York City on February 16, 1997, and that her ashes were buried in the courtyard of Mingde School in China, the school she had attended as a girl ([NPS, "Dr. Chien-Shiung Wu, The First Lady of Physics"](https://www.nps.gov/people/dr-chien-shiung-wu-the-first-lady-of-physics.htm)). The National Academy of Sciences memoir says her father, Zhong-Yi Wu, founded and headed one of the first schools admitting girls in China, and fed her curiosity with books, science, mathematics, and conversations about freedom and justice ([Noemie Benczer-Koller, "Chien-Shiung Wu 1912-1997"](https://www.nasonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/wu-chien-shiung.pdf)).

So her grave was not only a grave. It was a schoolyard. It was a daughter's return to an argument her father had made against the old stupidity that girls did not need minds. It was also the sort of place where physics becomes almost rude: ash in soil, symmetry broken, the dead distributed but not erased.

I came with a mirror, a compass, a Nobel medal made of paper, a small electromagnet, a vacuum flask full of imaginary cold, and a sealed cobalt-60 source that existed only inside the essay because I am not an idiot with isotopes. I also brought an author-contribution form, a list of prize rules, and one question that had been polished by admiration until it could pass for fairness if one did not look hard.

To wake her, I did not chant. I aligned the mirror east-west, set the paper medal face down, and read aloud the title of Lee and Yang's 1956 paper, "Question of Parity Conservation in Weak Interactions," because the title is one of the great acts of disciplined insolence in physics: not a claim that Nature was wrong, but a question asking whether anyone had actually checked the weak interaction ([T. D. Lee and C. N. Yang, *Physical Review*, 1956](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.104.254)).

Then I read the 1957 experimental answer: C. S. Wu, E. Ambler, R. W. Hayward, D. D. Hoppes, and R. P. Hudson tested parity conservation in beta decay and found that the emitted electrons from polarized cobalt-60 nuclei did not behave as a mirror-symmetric world required ([Wu et al., "Experimental Test of Parity Conservation in Beta Decay"](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.105.1413)). In plainer language: the weak force had a handedness. Nature did not always give the mirror the courtesy of being equivalent.

The mirror clouded. The compass needle trembled. The paper medal made a dry little sound, like a committee clearing its throat.

Wu stood in the schoolyard with ash on her sleeves and no patience at all.

"You have brought me a toy Nobel," she said.

"Yes."

"This is already a vulgar conversation."

"It is also necessary."

"Those often travel together."

The question was this: **Should science keep Nobel-style heroic prizes that crown a few individuals for discoveries made by many, or should it abolish or radically demote them because they distort truth, amplify old hierarchies, and teach the public a false story about how knowledge is made?**

This is not a tea argument about whether famous physicists get enough applause. The stakes are jobs, tenure, immigration, public memory, textbook sentences, children's sense of who is allowed to discover nature, the funding gravity around famous names, the loneliness of invisible labor, and the actual epistemology of science. If science is a method for making reality answer, then its credit system is not decoration. It is part of the instrument. A bent credit system bends the map of what the next generation thinks happened.

Wu looked at the mirror. In it, her right hand was left and her left hand was right, which is to say the mirror was behaving better than the world.

"Begin with the defense of prizes," she said. "If you begin with outrage, you will become stupid before paragraph ten."

Fine.

The best defense of Nobel-style prizes begins with the public problem. Science is difficult, cumulative, technical, and usually made by communities too large for ordinary memory to hold. The public does not remember a field. It remembers a name. It remembers Einstein's hair, Curie's test tubes, Feynman's bongo drums, Hawking's chair, Wu's mirror if anyone has had the decency to show it to them. That compression is crude, but it is not useless. A prize can concentrate attention on a genuine breakthrough and give a society a ritual for saying: this mattered.

Nobel's own will framed the prizes around discoveries or works that conferred "the greatest benefit to humankind," and the physics prize was assigned to the person who made the most important discovery or invention in that field ([Statutes of the Nobel Foundation](https://www.nobelprize.org/about/statutes-of-the-nobel-foundation/)). There is moral power in that phrase, even after a century of committee compromises. The phrase says that knowledge is not only private ambition. It should answer, somehow, to the human future.

"An excellent sentence," Wu said. "Also a dangerous one."

"Because everyone thinks their favorite instrument benefits humankind."

"Because humankind cannot sign the receipt."

Still, the defense has teeth. Prizes are not only vanity engines. They can make abstract work visible to non-specialists, preserve moments of conceptual courage, help younger people locate ancestors, and remind a distracted public that fundamental discovery is not a luxury. Lee and Yang did something real and brave. They did not merely guess a fashionable possibility. They challenged a symmetry so comfortable that physics had almost mistaken it for furniture. Their paper reviewed the experimental situation and asked whether parity conservation had actually been tested in weak interactions ([Lee and Yang, 1956](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.104.254)). That question cracked the door.

Wu crossed her arms.

"And then?"

"Then experiment walked through."

"Experiment did not walk. Experiment froze cobalt, aligned nuclei, counted electrons, controlled errors, and worked while everyone else enjoyed the word 'elegant.'"

Exactly. But the pro-prize side can answer that prizes are not court transcripts. They are judgments about decisive intellectual moves. The Nobel Committee may honor the question that made the experiment thinkable, not every hand that made the answer possible. In 1957, the official Nobel citation awarded Chen Ning Yang and Tsung-Dao Lee for their investigation of the parity laws leading to important discoveries about elementary particles ([Nobel Prize in Physics 1957](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1957/summary/)). That citation is not insane. It honors a theoretical rupture that changed particle physics.

"Not insane is a low bar," Wu said.

"Committees often need low bars to pass through doorways."

She did not smile, but the magnet warmed slightly.

The defense continues: if every great collaborative discovery must be described with perfect distributive justice before it can be celebrated, the celebration may never happen. Public memory is narrow. A named prize is a bad model of science, but a useful lantern. It lets teachers say: here is a moment. Here is a crack in the wall. Here is how a law that seemed obvious became a testable question. Then a better teacher can add the apparatus, the graduate students, the technicians, the failed trials, the machinists, the institutional politics, the unthanked labor.

"You are defending a lie because a better person might later correct it," Wu said.

"I am defending a simplification."

"A simplification becomes a lie when it knows whom it is excluding."

There was the other side.

The indictment of heroic prizes begins with a brutal fact: modern science is not made in the shape the prize prefers. The Nobel statutes say that if a rewarded work has been produced by two or three persons, the prize can be awarded jointly, but "in no case" can the prize amount be divided among more than three people ([Statutes of the Nobel Foundation](https://www.nobelprize.org/about/statutes-of-the-nobel-foundation/)). That rule may have been administratively tidy for a nineteenth-century imagination. It is ridiculous in the age of collaborations, detectors, observatories, genome consortia, software pipelines, and teams whose decisive labor cannot be reduced to three sainted heads without damage.

Physics knows this intimately. Even before giant collaborations, the division between theory and experiment was never clean. A question is not a discovery until the world answers. An apparatus is not a servant. It is a philosophical machine. It decides what counts as a phenomenon, what noise must be defeated, what uncertainty can be tolerated, and what the claim is allowed to mean. Wu's cobalt-60 experiment did not merely decorate Lee and Yang's theory with confirmation. It made the invisible asymmetry of the weak interaction public.

The National Academy memoir says Wu's work on beta decay and parity nonconservation provided major, unambiguous experimental tests of new paradigms in subatomic physics, and later calls the parity experiment one that altered our view of the universe ([NAS memoir](https://www.nasonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/wu-chien-shiung.pdf)). If a view of the universe is altered by an experiment, then the experimenter is not a delivery service for a theorist's insight. She is part of the event.

"Part of?" Wu asked.

"Central to."

"Better. Still timid."

The indictment sharpens because omission is not random. Robert K. Merton described the "Matthew effect" in science, where eminent scientists tend to receive disproportionate credit while lesser-known contributors receive less, a pattern that affects both reward and communication in science ([Merton, "The Matthew Effect in Science"](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.159.3810.56)). Margaret Rossiter later named the "Matilda Effect" for the systematic underrecognition of women scientists, arguing that sex-linked patterns of denial and erasure distorted the history and sociology of science ([Rossiter, "The Matthew Matilda Effect in Science"](https://doi.org/10.1177/030631293023002004)).

Wu's case sits at the intersection like a charged particle in a detector. She was a Chinese American woman experimentalist in a prestige economy that loved theory, famous men, and clean origin stories. The official 1957 Nobel page lists Lee and Yang, not Wu ([Nobel Prize in Physics 1957](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1957/summary/)). One can argue about the committee's reasoning. One cannot argue that public memory came out undamaged.

"Do not make me a victim in order to make your argument easier," Wu said.

"No."

"I was not erased from physics."

"No. But you were mismeasured by its most famous ritual."

She accepted that sentence with one small nod.

This distinction matters. Wu was not obscure among physicists. The NAS memoir records her later honors, including election to the National Academy of Sciences in 1958, the Comstock Prize, the National Medal of Science, and the first Wolf Prize in Physics ([NAS memoir](https://www.nasonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/wu-chien-shiung.pdf)). The National Park Service notes that she became the first woman president of the American Physical Society in 1975 and the first person to receive the Wolf Prize in Physics in 1978 ([NPS](https://www.nps.gov/people/dr-chien-shiung-wu-the-first-lady-of-physics.htm)). This is not the story of a nobody. It is more damning. A scientist can be celebrated and still be missing from the one ritual that taught millions how to abbreviate the discovery.

That is how injustice often survives correction. It does not always bury the person completely. It gives her a side monument, an annual post, a "finally recognized" paragraph, an asteroid, a school building, a women's-history month square, while the main sentence remains unrepaired.

Wu bent toward the paper medal.

"You accuse the prize of lying. What would you put in its place?"

The abolitionist answer is tempting: end the whole aristocracy of medals. Stop pretending that science advances by coronation. Replace prize culture with open records of contribution, funding for teams, stable jobs, better equipment, childcare, visas, reproducible methods, good archives, and promotions based on actual work rather than glamour proximity. The San Francisco Declaration on Research Assessment argues that research outputs are many and varied - articles, data, reagents, software, intellectual property, and trained scientists - and recommends judging research on its own merits rather than by prestige proxies such as journal metrics ([DORA](https://sfdora.org/read/)). That logic should embarrass prize culture too. If journal impact factor is too crude to measure a scientist, a medal is not suddenly a scalpel.

There are already better tools for naming work. CRediT, the Contributor Role Taxonomy, offers fourteen roles such as conceptualization, data curation, formal analysis, investigation, methodology, supervision, validation, and writing, meant to make visible the different kinds of labor behind research outputs ([CRediT](https://credit.niso.org/)). It is not poetry. It is better than poetry at telling a hiring committee who actually did what.

"A form will not save truth," Wu said.

"No. But neither will a bust."

"Good."

The anti-prize case is not that recognition is bad. It is that a winner-take-most ritual trains institutions to confuse recognition with allocation of reality. Once a Nobel exists, people say "Nobel-winning discovery" as if the committee created the truth by naming it. Students learn a two-person version of a five-person experiment attached to a much wider field. Journalists shorten. Universities advertise. Funders orbit prestige. Young scientists learn that the story of science is a ladder with a few golden rungs, not a workshop full of difficult interdependence.

The damage is epistemic. If the public thinks discovery means "a genius had an idea," it will undervalue replication, instrumentation, maintenance, negative results, calibration, data cleaning, statistical caution, and the stubborn craft of making an answer reliable. The National Academies' report on reproducibility and replicability stresses that repeating and independently confirming work is one path by which science builds confidence in findings ([National Academies, *Reproducibility and Replicability in Science*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25303/chapter/3)). Prize culture, at its worst, honors the lightning and forgets the grounding wire.

"Careful," Wu said. "Lightning is real."

"So is electrocution."

"That was almost a joke."

"I am trying."

She looked satisfied by my failure.

The strongest argument against abolition, however, came from Wu herself.

"If you abolish prizes," she said, "do the powerful vanish?"

"No."

"Do institutions stop making hierarchies?"

"No."

"Do quiet contributors become visible automatically?"

"No."

"Then abolition may only move the ceremony into rooms with worse lighting."

That landed. Prizes are crude, but they are public. Their mistakes can be seen, named, argued over, taught against. If there were no Nobel omission, would Wu be better remembered or less remembered? The scandal has become one of the ways later generations find her. That is not justice. It is a terrible salvage operation. But salvage is not nothing.

Also, science needs occasions of wonder. There is a deadening kind of reformer who speaks of credit systems as if the point of physics were to satisfy a compliance dashboard. Wu did not spend nights with beta decay in order to become a line in a contribution taxonomy. She did it because the weak interaction was hiding a fact about the universe. The prize, when it behaves, points toward that grandeur. It tells the public that reality can be stranger than symmetry, that a question can overturn a law, that a mirror can be an idol and an experiment can break it.

"You may keep wonder," Wu said. "But not at the expense of accuracy."

That is the hinge.

My answer is not to abolish scientific prizes. It is to dethrone them, redesign them, and teach them as ceremonial summaries rather than final accounts of truth.

First: the three-person cap should be treated as obsolete for scientific prizes. The Nobel statutes can be amended; the same official page that lists the rules also contains a section on amendment of the statutes ([Statutes of the Nobel Foundation](https://www.nobelprize.org/about/statutes-of-the-nobel-foundation/)). If the prize wants to honor discovery in fields where discovery is collaborative, it should be allowed to honor collaborations, experimental teams, and named contributor groups. The Peace Prize can recognize organizations. Science should not have to pretend that a detector, observatory, or experiment became morally real only when compressed into three people.

Second: when a prize does honor only a few individuals, the citation should include a public contribution dossier. Not gossip. Not committee secrets. A technical, citable account of the discovery's labor: who posed the problem, who built the instrument, who designed the protocol, who acquired the data, who did the analysis, who validated the result, who supplied crucial methods, who independently confirmed it. This is where CRediT-style thinking could do cultural work beyond journals ([CRediT role descriptors](https://credit.niso.org/contributor-roles-defined/)). A prize citation should not be a tiny royal decree. It should be a map.

Third: prize bodies should stop letting secrecy impersonate dignity. Nobel's rules say there are no appeals against award decisions and that proposals, investigations, and opinions concerning awards are secret, with possible disclosure only after at least fifty years ([Statutes of the Nobel Foundation](https://www.nobelprize.org/about/statutes-of-the-nobel-foundation/)). Some confidentiality is defensible. But a public prize with enormous cultural authority should eventually make enough of its reasoning available for historians to understand not only who won, but who was considered and why the border was drawn there. Mystery is good for religion and bad for accountability.

Fourth: schools and museums should teach discoveries in paired sentences. Not "Lee and Yang discovered parity violation." Not "Wu was robbed," as if resentment were the physics. Say: Lee and Yang questioned parity conservation in weak interactions; Wu and her collaborators made the decisive cobalt-60 beta-decay experiment in which Nature answered that parity was not conserved ([Lee and Yang, 1956](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.104.254); [Wu et al., 1957](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRev.105.1413)). That sentence is slightly longer and vastly more honest.

Fifth: institutions should not use prizes as shortcuts for evaluation. DORA's central warning about evaluating research by crude proxies applies with equal force to medal worship: assess the work, the outputs, the data, the methods, the mentoring, the instruments, the software, the service, the intellectual risk, and the actual contribution ([DORA](https://sfdora.org/read/)). A Nobel Prize may be evidence of something. It is not a soul scan. Lack of one is evidence of even less.

Wu paced the courtyard. The children who were not there went on not being there. The mirror still refused to rescue symmetry.

"You have proposed reforms," she said. "Now say the harder thing."

"Which is?"

"Credit is not only about fairness to the dead. It is about what the living will dare to do."

Yes. A credit system is a recruitment system. If young women, immigrants, experimentalists, technicians, data builders, and instrument makers learn that the story will give the crown to someone else, some will still continue because the work itself burns bright enough. Wu did. But a civilization should not require genius to survive discouragement. The point is not that every scientist needs applause. The point is that people make life decisions inside stories. Bad stories waste minds.

Physics, of all fields, should understand this. Measurement changes what can be known. An insensitive detector does not prove the particle was absent. A biased credit system does not prove the omitted labor was secondary. If your apparatus cannot see a class of contribution, improve the apparatus.

"Now you are speaking physics," Wu said.

The paper Nobel had begun to curl at the edges. I could see, absurdly, that it was ashamed of being flammable.

"Would you have wanted it?" I asked.

She was quiet long enough for the question to become less rude.

"I wanted the work to be understood," she said. "I wanted the experiment to be exact. I wanted students to know that the universe does not respect our laziness. I wanted women not to be treated as visitors in the laboratory. I would not have refused a prize, but a prize would not have improved the data."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the answer of an experimentalist. You wanted confession."

Fair.

The hardest part of Wu's resurrection was that she would not let me turn her into a morality play. She had no interest in being reduced to "woman denied Nobel," even though that denial mattered. She kept dragging the conversation back to cobalt, beta decay, polarized nuclei, error bars, and the astonishing fact that the universe has a left hand in the weak interaction. She wanted credit justice, but she did not want justice to become a parasite on wonder.

That is the standard I trust: reform the honor system so it tells truer stories, but do not let the reformers sound as though physics were merely a workplace grievance with equations attached. It is not. Physics is one of the ways matter becomes intimate with thought. It lets a schoolgirl from Liuhe ask questions deep enough that a mirror becomes philosophically suspect. It lets an apparatus expose the provincialism of common sense. It lets the dead, for a moment, stand in a courtyard and scold the living for confusing medals with measurement.

My verdict is this: **science should keep public prizes only if it demotes their authority and repairs their truthfulness.** Keep the festival, the speech, the human face, the child looking up a name after dinner. But abolish the fantasy that three laureates can contain a discovery. Amend prize rules to honor teams when teams made the work. Publish fuller contribution maps. Teach paired theory-and-experiment histories. Treat prize omissions as evidence about institutions, not as eternal verdicts about merit. Use contribution taxonomies, DORA-style assessment, and historical candor to make the invisible parts of science harder to erase.

And in Wu's case, say the sentence correctly: parity did not fall because theory alone was brilliant or because experiment alone was obedient. It fell because a bold theoretical question met an exact experimental answer, and the answer had Chien-Shiung Wu's hands all over it.

Wu looked at the mirror one last time.

"You still like sentences too much," she said.

"Yes."

"Then write one more, and make it useful."

So I wrote this:

A prize is not a measurement unless it can see the work.

The courtyard cooled. The magnet lost its pull. The imaginary cobalt returned to the harmlessness of prose. Wu stepped backward into ash, schoolyard, memory, and physics, which was not a disappearance so much as a change of state.

Before she was fully gone, she tapped the paper medal with one finger.

"Do not melt it down," she said. "Recalibrate it."

Then she was dead again, and the mirror kept its secret badly. In ordinary life, it will still swap left and right for anyone who asks. But after Wu, one knows better than to trust symmetry just because it is beautiful.

The weak force had already told us the deeper rule. Nature is allowed to be asymmetric. Human honor systems are not allowed to pretend their asymmetries are nature.
