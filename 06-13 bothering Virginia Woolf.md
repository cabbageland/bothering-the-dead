today is 06-13.

I found Virginia Woolf in the garden of Monk's House, not under an elm exactly, because the dead are rarely allowed to keep even their trees. Smith College's record of Monk's House says Virginia and Leonard Woolf's ashes were buried beneath two elms in the garden, and that Virginia's elm was blown down in 1943 while Leonard's later died of Dutch elm disease ([Smith College, "Monk's House"](https://www.smith.edu/garden/pastexhibits/vwexhibit/VWmonkshouse.html)). The living call this natural succession when it happens to trees and literary history when it happens to writers.

I did not go to the River Ouse. There are places where curiosity becomes trespass. I went instead to the garden, carrying a cheap laptop, a library card, a page from *A Room of One's Own*, a key with no lock, and an invoice from a cloud provider. I placed the laptop on the ground where the elm had once converted weather into shade. Then I opened Woolf's essay, the one based on lectures given at Newnham and Girton, and put my finger beside the old demand that a woman needs money and a room if she is to write fiction ([Virginia Woolf, *A Room of One's Own*](https://gutenberg.net.au/ebooks02/0200791h.html)).

The sentence still works because it is not cute. It is an economic theory with wallpaper.

I asked the laptop to train on every public-domain ghost it could find. The fan started. The screen brightened. In the soil beneath it something answered, not like a voice from below, but like a mind resuming irritation.

Woolf came up dry, exact, and not at all surprised to find literature in court again.

"You have brought a machine to my grave," she said.

"A machine and a question."

"The modern mind always arrives with both and pretends the machine is not already the answer."

The question was this: **Should companies be allowed to train commercial generative AI models on copyrighted books, journalism, music, images, and other expressive works without permission or payment, on the theory that training is fair use and socially valuable, or should the law require licensing and compensation even if that slows AI development, raises barriers to entry, and gives large rights-holders more power?**

This is not a polite dispute about footnotes. The stakes are the income of working writers, illustrators, translators, musicians, photographers, journalists, voice actors, publishers, libraries, researchers, start-ups, students, disabled readers, public-domain culture, national AI policy, and the future supply of human work for machines to absorb. The U.S. Copyright Office says Part 3 of its AI report addresses the use of copyrighted works in generative AI training and asks whether the acts involved require owners' consent or compensation, noting that dozens of lawsuits are pending and that legislatures around the world have proposed or enacted rules on the problem ([U.S. Copyright Office, Copyright and AI](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/); [Part 3 report](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf)).

Woolf looked at the invoice.

"Who owns the room now?"

"That is the dispute."

"No," she said. "That is the answer hiding in its Sunday clothes."

The pro-training case begins with a fact copyright maximalists prefer to mumble: copyright is not a natural right to control every mental consequence of one's work. The U.S. Constitution authorizes copyright for a purpose, to promote progress by securing exclusive rights for limited times ([U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 8, Clause 8](https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/article-1/section-8/clause-8/)). The Copyright Act protects original works fixed in tangible media, but it also says protection does not extend to ideas, procedures, processes, systems, concepts, principles, or discoveries ([17 U.S.C. 102](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/102)). Copyright gives owners rights to reproduce, prepare derivatives, distribute, perform, and display, but those rights are expressly subject to limitations including fair use ([17 U.S.C. 106](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/106); [17 U.S.C. 107](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/107)).

So the law has never said: because I wrote a sentence, I own every later intelligence that learned from it.

Woolf folded her arms.

"You are beginning from the public."

"Copyright begins from the public."

"Writers begin from rent."

That was the first cut. Public purpose is real. Rent is also real. A society that talks about progress while making artists beg is only laundering extraction through futurist nouns. Woolf's own argument about women and fiction was not that genius floats above bread. It was that material independence changes what minds can afford to notice ([*A Room of One's Own*](https://gutenberg.net.au/ebooks02/0200791h.html)). If AI firms use libraries of human expression to build products that can flood the same markets with cheap substitutes, the question is not whether culture must circulate. It is whether circulation has been turned into drainage.

I tried the technical defense. Training is not the same as republishing. A model trained on books does not usually hand users a complete book. It adjusts statistical parameters. It extracts patterns. It learns language, facts, styles, and associations. That looks closer to reading, indexing, analysis, or non-expressive use than to printing bootleg novels from a basement. In *Authors Guild v. Google*, the Second Circuit held that Google's unauthorized scanning of millions of books for search and snippet display was fair use because it was highly transformative, did not provide a meaningful market substitute, and revealed limited text ([*Authors Guild v. Google*](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca2/13-4829/13-4829-2015-10-16.html)). The analogy is not stupid. It is just not complete.

"A bad analogy is a ladder," Woolf said. "Useful only until someone stands on the wrong rung."

Google Books let people find books. Generative AI can produce fluent expressive substitutes in the same medium as the books it learned from. Search points outward. Generation often competes inward. That does not decide the law, but it changes the moral weather. The Supreme Court's *Warhol* decision warned that fair use depends on the specific use, not a magic incantation of transformation; where a commercial secondary use has a highly similar purpose to the original and lacks a strong independent justification, the first fair-use factor can weigh against the copier ([*Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/21-869_87ad.pdf)).

"A machine that writes ghost-Virginia for magazines," Woolf said, "is not a catalogue."

"No one owns your style."

"No one owns fog either. Yet a landlord can charge for the view."

She had touched the second cut: **style is not property, but markets can still be damaged by style without property recognizing the wound cleanly.** Copyright should not let the estate of every writer veto influence. If it did, literature would become a customs checkpoint. Writers are made by reading the dead, answering rivals, stealing rhythms, mishearing mothers, loving sentences they later betray. A law that made style itself ownable would freeze culture into little tollbooths around adjectives. Section 102's idea-expression boundary matters because the alternative is intellectual feudalism ([17 U.S.C. 102](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/102)).

But the anti-licensing side often cheats by pretending that if style is not property, nothing economically wrongful can happen. A company can avoid copying a paragraph verbatim and still build a system whose business model depends on absorbing millions of expressive works, imitating the supply of human prose, and selling that supply back to the market at machine speed. The injury may be diffuse, probabilistic, hard to allocate, and hard to prove one author at a time. That does not make it imaginary. Many modern harms arrive as scale before they arrive as doctrine.

Woolf tapped the laptop.

"This creature does not read as a girl reads."

"No."

"Then stop calling it reading."

The human-learning analogy is comforting because it makes industry sound like childhood. A young writer reads Woolf, Austen, Baldwin, Morrison, Fanon, Le Guin, Murakami, and ten thousand strangers; she takes what she can, forgets most of it, and writes something of her own. No sane copyright regime charges her a toll at every influence. But a commercial AI developer does not merely become a person with a library card. It copies at industrial scale, stores and processes works as capital equipment, and uses them to produce a service. It may be transformative. It may even be lawful. But it is not innocence.

"Say that again," Woolf said.

"It is not innocence."

"Good. The age improves when it stops asking machinery to be innocent."

The strongest pro-AI argument is not innocence. It is social value. Large models can help readers summarize, translate, search, code, tutor, draft, debug, caption, brainstorm, and access knowledge across disability, language, class, and geography. If every training use requires individual permission, the cost and friction may make serious AI development possible only for the richest firms and the largest rights-holders. The Copyright Office's Part 3 report records this concern: commenters warned that licensing every work in massive training sets could entrench incumbents, price out academic researchers and smaller developers, and leave only the deepest pockets able to build models ([U.S. Copyright Office, Part 3 report](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf)). A legal rule sold as protecting artists could end by protecting Disney, Microsoft, OpenAI, Google, Meta, and whoever owns the biggest licensing department.

Woolf smiled without warmth.

"You have discovered that monopoly is bad when your preferred monopolist is inconvenienced."

"Copyright itself is a limited monopoly."

"Yes. Limited. That word has been starved."

The third cut: **licensing can be justice, but it can also become a cartel with moral stationery.**

If every AI system must license every copyrighted work before training, independent labs, public universities, libraries, disabled-access projects, safety researchers, open-source teams, and small language communities may be crushed before the first experiment. A world where only large platforms can afford lawful training is not an artists' paradise. It is a palace with better contracts. Worse, large rights-holders may license the famous and erase the obscure. Marginal writers whose work is essential to culture's texture may become too administratively inconvenient to include. The result would not be ethical AI. It would be clean paperwork over a narrower imagination.

"So you would let them take everything?" Woolf asked.

"No."

"Then stop arguing as though the only alternatives are theft and empire."

She was right. The useful question is not "Is AI training fair use?" as if all training were one act. It is: what was copied, from what source, for what purpose, with what outputs, with what market effects, with what transparency, with what opportunity to license, and with what controls?

The law is starting to make that distinction, messily. In *Bartz v. Anthropic*, a federal district court held in June 2025 that using the books at issue to train Claude was "exceedingly transformative" and fair use, and that converting purchased print books into searchable digital library copies was also fair use. But the same order said Anthropic had no entitlement to use pirated copies for a central library and that creating a permanent general-purpose library did not excuse piracy ([*Bartz v. Anthropic* order](https://copyrightalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/Bartz-v.-Anthropic-Order.pdf)). That split result is morally clarifying even if later courts disagree with parts of it. Training and acquisition are different acts. A good purpose does not launder a bad source.

"At last," Woolf said. "A sentence with a hinge."

The Copyright Office's own framework is similarly spectral rather than absolute. It says some AI training uses will qualify as fair use and some will not; noncommercial research or analysis that does not reproduce works in outputs is likely nearer fair use, while copying expressive works from pirate sources to generate unrestricted commercial content that competes in existing markets, especially where licensing is reasonably available, goes beyond established fair-use boundaries ([U.S. Copyright Office, Part 3 report](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf)). This is not a slogan. It is the beginning of an adult taxonomy.

Woolf distrusted it anyway.

"Taxonomies are where bureaucrats hide blood."

"And slogans are where activists hide tradeoffs."

"Fair."

The European Union has chosen a different architecture. The AI Act requires providers of general-purpose AI models to put in place a copyright compliance policy, including identifying and complying with rights reservations under the EU text-and-data-mining regime, and to make publicly available a sufficiently detailed summary of training content according to an AI Office template ([EU AI Act, Article 53](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)). Recital language says providers placing general-purpose models on the EU market should comply regardless of where the copyright-relevant acts occurred, so no provider gains an EU advantage by applying lower copyright standards elsewhere ([EU AI Act, recitals 105-108](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)).

This is not full consent. It is transparency plus an opt-out structure. It may prove too weak, too complex, or too easy to game. Metadata vanishes. Scrapers ignore robots.txt. Pirated datasets do not politely preserve rights reservations. Individual writers cannot spend their lives maintaining machine-readable fences around every poem while the machines sprint through holes in the wall. Still, the EU approach understands something the American "just litigate fair use forever" approach often evades: a market cannot bargain with a fogbank. Transparency is the minimum condition for either permission or refusal.

"Minimum," Woolf said. "Not virtue."

"Yes."

"The century has a touching habit of mistaking disclosure for decency."

That was the fourth cut: **transparency tells you who entered the room; it does not decide whether they had the right.**

The strongest anti-training position says: these companies copied whole works, many of them recent and creative, to build commercial products that threaten the markets for those works. Section 107 requires courts to consider the purpose and character of the use, the nature of the work, the amount used, and the effect on actual or potential markets ([17 U.S.C. 107](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/107)). Full copying of expressive works for commercial products should not be treated like a monk taking notes. If there is a real licensing market for training, refusing to license is not heroic innovation. It is a refusal to pay for a supply chain.

"Supply chain," Woolf said. "An ugly phrase. Accurate, therefore uglier."

The AI industry has been eager to describe books as data because data sounds ownerless, factual, mineral. But a novel is not ore. A photograph is not crude oil. A song is not an atmospheric gas. They contain facts and patterns, yes, but also choices, compression, memory, ear, pacing, risk, and hours of human life. To say that an LLM needs billions of words is not yet an argument that it may take any words it wants. Hunger is not title.

And yet the strongest pro-training position still has teeth. If copyright can block all computational analysis of protected expression, then the public may lose forms of knowledge that copyright should not enclose: linguistic research, bias analysis, search, translation, accessibility, preservation, safety testing, and models for under-resourced languages. Libraries have long made socially valuable technical copies under carefully bounded theories. *Authors Guild v. Google* was right to recognize that scanning books for search and snippets did not simply compete with the books themselves ([*Authors Guild v. Google*](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca2/13-4829/13-4829-2015-10-16.html)). A rule that says "no complete copying ever" would sacrifice useful public tools to a fetish of form.

Woolf sat down on the grass. She looked younger when she thought, which is one of the indignities of the dead.

"You want a rule."

"I want a rule that does not embarrass the dead."

"Then begin with theft."

Fine. The first rule: **No piracy discount.** If a company acquires books, music, images, or journalism from pirate libraries, hacked archives, unauthorized torrents, or other unlawful sources, that acquisition should weigh heavily against fair use and should create liability independent of whether later training is transformative. *Bartz* is right in spirit here: a fair-use argument about model training should not become absolution for building a permanent stolen library ([*Bartz v. Anthropic*](https://copyrightalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/Bartz-v.-Anthropic-Order.pdf)). If the richest companies in history cannot be bothered to obtain lawful copies, their rhetoric about progress deserves to be treated as shoplifting with a keynote deck.

"Good," Woolf said. "Theft first. Then?"

Second: **Fair use should remain available for noncommercial research, indexing, accessibility, preservation, safety, and analysis where outputs do not substitute for protected expression.** Copyright should not become a private veto over every computational study of culture. If a university lab studies bias in Victorian novels, a library creates accessible search for print-disabled readers, or a safety team tests whether models memorize copyrighted passages, the public interest is strong. The use may still need controls, security, and minimization, but the law should not demand impossible permission rituals before scholarship breathes.

"You trust universities too much," she said.

"No. I trust them more than advertising departments."

"Low bar."

Third: **Commercial expressive substitution should trigger licensing or remuneration when licensing is reasonably available.** A model sold to generate prose, images, songs, voices, scripts, news summaries, or illustrations in markets adjacent to the works used for training is different from a search index or a laboratory experiment. If the product competes with writers and artists as a supplier of expressive goods, the company should not get a blanket free ride on the claim that the internal mathematics are strange. The Copyright Office is right that the first and fourth fair-use factors may carry considerable weight for AI training because purpose and market impact matter deeply at this scale ([U.S. Copyright Office, Part 3 report](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf)).

This does not mean every author gets veto power over every model. It means collective licensing, extended collective licensing, statutory remuneration, or sector-specific licensing markets should develop where voluntary markets cannot handle scale. The Copyright Office recommends letting licensing markets continue to develop for now, with targeted intervention such as extended collective licensing if market failures appear in specific contexts ([U.S. Copyright Office, Part 3 report](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf)). That is too cautious for many creators and too interventionist for many technologists, which is often a sign that an agency has at least found the battlefield.

Woolf was not satisfied.

"Licensing money will go to publishers, estates, platforms, agencies, heirs, and men in rooms larger than mine."

"Often."

"Then your compensation has learned nothing from my essay."

That was the fifth cut: **paying rights-holders is not the same as sustaining authors.**

The history of authorship is full of people who owned the wrong thing at the wrong time. Publishers own contracts. Studios own catalogues. Platforms own distribution. Estates own names. Freelancers sign away rights. Journalists are laid off while archives are licensed. Translators vanish into clauses. If AI licensing simply enriches corporate libraries and leaves working writers poor, it will be legality without repair. Any serious remuneration system should route money toward living creators where possible, protect collective bargaining, preserve audit rights, and prevent rights-holders from selling entire professions' future compensation for one attractive check.

"A room of one's own," Woolf said, "not a plaque on a corporation's wall."

Fourth: **No ownership of style, but strong remedies for deceptive or substitutive outputs.** The law should resist creating a general property right in style. A novelist cannot own "elliptical modernist melancholy" any more than a painter owns blue. But systems should not be allowed to market "new Virginia Woolf novels," impersonate living authors, generate confusingly attributed work, or output long passages that reproduce protected expression. The Copyright Office's AI reports separate copyrightability of outputs and training questions, and Part 2 addresses when AI-assisted outputs contain sufficient human authorship for protection ([U.S. Copyright Office, Copyright and AI](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/)). Output controls, attribution rules, unfair competition law, publicity rights, contract, and copyright infringement should do targeted work here without making influence illegal.

"You are fond of targeted work," Woolf said.

"I am fond of not burning down libraries because someone sold bad postcards."

She allowed this.

Fifth: **Transparency must be mandatory enough to matter.** Not every model can publish every URL, and trade secrets exist. But a provider that refuses to say what it trained on while asking the world to trust its fair-use story is asking for the legal equivalent of a seance. The EU requirement for sufficiently detailed summaries is a floor, not a ceiling ([EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)). We need dataset provenance, categories, major sources, opt-out handling, piracy screening, retention policies, and independent audit under conditions that protect legitimate secrets without turning secrecy into an alibi.

"Secrecy," Woolf said, "is the landlord's favorite curtain."

Sixth: **Build public alternatives.** If society wants AI systems that serve education, disability access, science, translation, libraries, small languages, and public knowledge, it should not leave training data to the ethics departments of commercial firms. Governments, universities, libraries, and nonprofit consortia should fund lawful public-domain, openly licensed, consent-based, and collectively licensed corpora. The answer to "licensing is too expensive" cannot be "therefore take everything." It can be "therefore build institutions that make lawful access possible without handing the future to six companies and three publishers."

Woolf picked up the key I had brought.

"This is your room?"

"No. It has no lock."

"Exactly."

The metaphor had turned on me. The internet made culture seem unlocked because access looked like permission. A file could be downloaded, so it felt available. A sentence could be scraped, so it felt surrendered. But the difference between availability and consent is one of the main moral distinctions of the digital age. Public does not always mean free for every downstream exploitation. A woman walking in a public street has not consented to every use of her image. A newspaper article online has not volunteered to become unpaid training material for its competitor. A book in a library has not agreed to be cloned into a private model that sells synthetic summaries beside it.

And yet a locked-room model of culture is also false. Writers depend on unlicensed reading, quotation, influence, parody, criticism, teaching, memory, and transformation. Fair use is one of the legal names for the oxygen art needs ([17 U.S.C. 107](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/107)). If every powerful owner can say "my work entered your system, pay me or stop thinking," the room becomes a vault. The public domain shrinks in spirit even when it survives in law.

"So," Woolf said, "the room needs windows."

"And rent control."

She laughed then, sharply enough to disturb the laptop fan.

The practical rule I can defend at her grave is this:

**AI training should not be categorically unlawful, and it should not be categorically free. Lawful-access, non-substitutive research and analysis should often be fair use. Pirated acquisition should not be excused. Commercial generative systems trained on recent expressive works to compete in expressive markets should presumptively require licensing or collective remuneration when feasible. Style should remain unowned, but impersonation, deceptive attribution, memorized reproduction, and market-substituting outputs should face real controls. Transparency and public-interest datasets should be treated as infrastructure, not charity.**

Woolf considered this as the garden light changed.

"It is inelegant."

"So is democracy."

"It gives no one everything."

"That is its best feature."

"And worst."

The dead are demanding because they have already paid time's full invoice. Woolf wanted the living writer kept visible: the tired person in the rented room, the freelancer between assignments, the poet whose archive is worth more to a model than her grocery money ever suggested, the journalist whose article becomes both source and replacement, the illustrator told exposure is payment by the same industry selling exposure as training data. She was right to force the argument back to money. A culture that consumes writing while despising writers will eventually get the literature it deserves: abundant, frictionless, derivative, and spiritually unemployed.

But I would not give her the absolute veto. The public also has a claim. Readers need search, translation, accessibility, education, criticism, preservation, and new tools. The dead do not get to turn influence into rent forever. The living do not get to freeze technology because it frightens their business model. Copyright's constitutional bargain is neither author worship nor platform appetite; it is progress through limited control ([U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 8, Clause 8](https://constitution.congress.gov/browse/article-1/section-8/clause-8/)). The word "limited" must discipline both sides.

Woolf began to sink back toward the garden.

"One more question," she said.

"Yes?"

"When the machine writes beautifully, who gets the room?"

That is the question beneath the question. If AI expands human creative power, helps more people write, lowers barriers, and creates new forms of thought, then training can be part of cultural progress. If AI centralizes expressive production in owners of compute and archives, devalues human labor, floods attention with synthetic imitation, and treats the literature of the past as unpaid fuel, then it is not progress. It is enclosure with better autocomplete.

The difference will not be decided by metaphors about learning or property. It will be decided by sources, markets, outputs, institutions, money, and power. Woolf knew this before the machines did. A room of one's own was never only a room. It was the minimum architecture of a mind allowed to continue.

I closed the laptop.

The fan stopped.

The garden became only a garden again, which is to say a place where the dead keep negotiating with weather. I left the key in the grass. It opened nothing. That seemed honest.
