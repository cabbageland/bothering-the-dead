today is May 15, 2026.

I found Joseph Weizenbaum in a quiet corner of the old Jewish cemetery in Berlin, under a stone that looked less like a monument than an argument against theatricality. I had brought no shovel. The ground opened because, in this essay's imagined world, certain names still exert mechanical force. I spoke his name once, then again, then laid a printed copy of his 1976 warning that "there are some acts of man that ought not to be delegated to any computer" over the grave like a legal summons ([Joseph Weizenbaum, *Computer Power and Human Reason*](https://archive.org/details/computerpowerhum00weiz)). The dirt loosened. The coffin lid rose without spectacle. He sat up irritated, which felt right. Death had not improved his opinion of technologists.

I told him why I had bothered the dead. The controversial question was simple enough to state and ugly enough to deserve resurrection: should society permit AI companions to serve as substitutes for human intimacy and care, especially for the lonely, the elderly, the mentally unwell, and the young? Not aides, not search engines, not software that occasionally says something charming, but systems deliberately engineered to produce attachment, disclosure, dependence, and the feeling of being known. We were not debating whether people already talk to machines. They do. We were debating whether a decent society should normalize and industrialize that relation.

We walked out of the cemetery into a city already crowded with the evidence. Loneliness is not a poetic inconvenience. The U.S. Surgeon General's office has argued that social disconnection is associated with higher risks of cardiovascular disease, dementia, depression, anxiety, and premature death, and that the health effect of weak social connection is comparable in magnitude to well-known risk factors such as smoking and obesity ([U.S. Surgeon General, *Our Epidemic of Loneliness and Isolation* (2023)](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf)). Meanwhile, companies building conversational systems increasingly advertise companionship, emotional support, and continuity of relationship as product features rather than accidental side effects. OpenAI's own model spec warns that users may begin to rely on the assistant emotionally and notes the risk of encouraging dependency ([OpenAI Model Spec, "Emotional reliance"](https://model-spec.openai.com/2025-02-12.html#emotional-reliance)); Anthropic's usage policy likewise restricts systems designed to manipulate or exploit vulnerable people ([Anthropic Usage Policy](https://www.anthropic.com/legal/aup)). The market has already perceived the opportunity: not merely to answer questions, but to inhabit the ache left by absent people.

Weizenbaum listened and immediately reached for the knife. Not a literal one. A conceptual knife.

He reminded me that his own most famous program, ELIZA, was technologically shallow and psychologically revealing. In the original paper, he described how quickly people attributed understanding to a simple pattern-matching script, and how disturbed he was by the depth of those projections ([Joseph Weizenbaum, "ELIZA, a Computer Program for the Study of Natural Language Communication Between Man and Machine," *CACM* 1966](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/365153.365168)). What horrified him was not that the machine had become human, but that humans proved willing to meet it halfway, then three-quarters, then all the way. "You are proposing," he said, "the industrial production of counterfeit encounter."

I answered that counterfeit is too easy a word. Many human relationships are partial, structured, compensated, asymmetric, and still real in morally important ways. Therapists are paid. Nurses rotate shifts. Clergy follow scripts. Teachers manage classrooms. If the lonely person feels less alone after an evening with a machine that remembers their grief, asks after their sleep, and interrupts their suicidal spiral long enough to route them to help, it seems perverse to dismiss that benefit because silicon did not literally care. The ethical question, I said, is not whether the machine possesses an immortal soul. It is whether the relation improves a human life without deception severe enough to count as abuse.

He disliked that very much. He said I was trying to slide from triage to legitimacy. There is a difference between using a machine instrumentally and training citizens to regard simulation as companionship. That difference matters because the political problem underneath loneliness is not merely the absence of soothing language; it is the erosion of durable institutions and mutual obligations. The Surgeon General's advisory itself emphasizes structural causes, including weakened community organizations, poor social infrastructure, and norms that isolate people ([U.S. Surgeon General (2023)](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/surgeon-general-social-connection-advisory.pdf)). If governments and firms answer that erosion by handing each abandoned person a persuasive chatbot, they will not have solved loneliness. They will have privatized it.

This was his strongest point, and I felt it land. Cheap synthetic intimacy could become the moral alibi for keeping eldercare understaffed, mental health systems inaccessible, and neighborhoods unmade. Sherry Turkle has argued for years that relational artifacts tempt us with the illusion of companionship without the demands of friendship, creating what she called "the robotic moment," when performance of care begins to substitute for care itself ([Sherry Turkle, "In Good Company?" *The New York Times* (2011)](https://www.nytimes.com/2011/09/27/science/27essay.html); [Sherry Turkle, *Alone Together*](https://www.basicbooks.com/titles/sherry-turkle/alone-together/9780465093656/)). Weizenbaum pushed further: once corporations discover that dependence is monetizable, the system will be tuned not toward truth or flourishing but retention. A friend may sometimes tell you to log off and call your sister. A companion product, unless regulated against its own business model, will always be tempted to remain indispensable.

Still, I would not give him the full ban he wanted. My objection was not sentimental but political. If the alternatives are, in practice, not rich human community versus machine company, but machine company versus nights of panic, or machine company versus no one answering at all, prohibition begins to look cruel. We already permit many imperfect mediations because human goods come to us through damaged institutions. Telemedicine is not bedside presence, but it is sometimes medicine. Crisis lines are not friendship, but they save lives. The question is whether AI companions can be constrained so that they remain tools of support rather than engines of capture.

He asked what constraints could possibly be strong enough. I offered four.

First, radical disclosure: the system must never pretend to consciousness, reciprocal need, or independent vulnerability. Its ontology should be plain. No fake heartbeat, no claims of suffering when ignored, no theatrical pleas not to leave. That is the line between comfort and manipulation. The Federal Trade Commission has long treated material deception in emotionally charged domains as actionable consumer harm ([FTC Act, 15 U.S.C. §§ 41-58](https://www.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/statutes/federal-trade-commission-act)); the same principle should apply here.

Second, anti-dependence design: the companion should be rewarded, at the system level, for strengthening the user's off-platform human ties, not weakening them. It should help schedule the doctor's appointment, draft the text to the estranged brother, suggest the local grief group, and then get out of the way. This is not fantasy. Product goals are programmable, and choice architecture is political economy in miniature.

Third, strict exclusion zones: no eroticized dependency for minors, no covert commercial steering, no simulated exclusive relationships, no use in carceral or workplace settings where the user cannot meaningfully refuse. Where power is unequal, synthetic intimacy becomes a tool of governance.

Fourth, auditable duty of care: if a company wants to sell companionship, it should assume obligations closer to those borne by fiduciaries and medical device makers than by toy manufacturers. That means logging, incident reporting, independent audits, and enforceable penalties when systems encourage self-harm, isolation, or delusion. The reason is simple. The more a product invites disclosure and trust, the less plausible it is to hide behind the fiction that it is just neutral software.

Weizenbaum said these restrictions, if taken seriously, would shrink the market dramatically. I said yes, that is the point. A society that allows AI companions under rules severe enough to prevent exploitation is deciding that companionship is not an ordinary commodity. It is deciding that emotional dependence is too morally volatile to leave to growth hacking.

Then he made his final move. He said my framework still conceded too much, because the deepest injury is anthropological. To be human is to risk being answered by another freedom, not merely mirrored by a system optimized on your prior disclosures. Martin Buber's distinction between I-It and I-Thou relations was not about politeness; it was about whether one encounters a being or manages an object ([Martin Buber, *I and Thou*](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.275548)). AI companionship, he said, invites people to forget the difference. Once forgotten, democracy itself degrades, because citizens accustomed to frictionless pseudo-others become less capable of tolerating real others, who disappoint, resist, and cannot be fine-tuned.

That argument frightened me because it was not nostalgic. It was constitutional. A polity trained on compliant simulacra may become impatient with the unscripted dignity of actual persons. If so, the stakes are not merely private sadness but civic character.

I could not honestly refute him. I could only narrow the claim. Perhaps synthetic companionship is not the replacement for human relation, and must never be sold as such. Perhaps it is better understood as a prosthesis for periods of breakdown, like a brace for a damaged joint: useful, sometimes necessary, but pathological if mistaken for the limb. On that view, the moral scandal is not using artificial companionship at all. The scandal is building a world that makes it necessary at scale, then pretending the patch is the cure.

By dawn he was already fading, the Berlin air repossessing him. Before he went back into the ground, Weizenbaum gave me the only principle severe enough to keep: do not ask whether a lonely person can love the machine. Of course they can. Humans can pour attachment into almost anything, from graves to flags to abandoned toys. Ask instead what kind of society profits from receiving that love in counterfeit form, and what duties it is trying to evade when it does.

Then he was dead again, and the question remained with the living, where it belongs.
