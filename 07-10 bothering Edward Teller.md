today is 07-10.

I found Edward Teller in a tomb made of sunlight that had been slightly misfiled.

Not in the cemetery. I did not go digging for bones under the authority of a daily deadline. I found the more appropriate corpse in a government preprint from 1997, the one called *Global Warming and Ice Ages: I. Prospects for Physics-Based Modulation of Global Change*, where Teller, Lowell Wood, and Roderick Hyde proposed that the thermal effect of greenhouse gases might be countered by scatterers that prevent a small fraction of solar radiation from reaching Earth ([Teller, Wood, and Hyde, 1997](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/611779)). There are bodies under soil, and there are bodies under proposals. Teller's most durable corpse was not only the man who died at Stanford in 2003 after a century almost perfectly designed to tempt physics into power. It was the idea that if the planet has become a laboratory, then perhaps the laboratory should stop pretending it is innocent.

I built his grave from things that could not agree whether he was hero, warning label, or instrument: Lawrence Livermore's official account of the Budapest-born theoretical physicist who worked under Heisenberg, left Germany after the Nazis rose, joined the Manhattan Project, and later helped make thermonuclear reactions central to U.S. defense policy ([LLNL, "Edward Teller, 1958-1960"](https://www.llnl.gov/edward-teller-1958-1960)); Hoover's obituary-profile of a man known for thermonuclear energy, quantum theory, molecular physics, astrophysics, civil defense, defense policy, and energy ([Hoover Institution, Edward Teller](https://www.hoover.org/profiles/edward-teller)); the 2021 National Academies report recommending a small, governed, transdisciplinary solar geoengineering research program rather than a march toward deployment ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)); the White House 2023 research plan that explicitly said its publication did not signal an executive-branch policy decision on solar radiation modification ([OSTP, 2023](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Congressionally-Mandated-Report-on-Solar-Radiation-Modification.pdf)); the Royal Society's briefing that solar radiation modification could mask warming but would not address greenhouse gas concentrations ([Royal Society, SRM briefing](https://royalsociety.org/news-resources/projects/solar-radiation-modification/summary-briefing/)); and the 2025 UNEP issues note saying SRM remains speculative, risky, and no substitute for mitigation or stronger adaptation ([UNEP, 2025](https://www.unep.org/resources/emerging-issues/solar-radiation-modification-no-substitute-real-climate-action)).

Then I added a pinch of sulfate aerosol, stolen only in imagination from the ghost-cloud of Mount Pinatubo. NASA says Pinatubo injected about 15 million tons of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere in 1991, where it formed a global haze of sulfate aerosols ([NASA Earth Observatory, "Global Effects of Mount Pinatubo"](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/global-effects-of-mount-pinatubo-1510/)). The volcano had done by accident what some human beings now discuss doing by design. That made it a bell, and I rang it.

The particles brightened. The room lost one percent of its sunlight and gained one hundred percent of its unease.

Teller sat up in the paper tomb wearing the face of a man who had spent his life refusing to let danger remain unweaponized.

"You brought me back with an albedo perturbation," he said.

"I thought necromancy should respect professional specialization."

"You chose the climate question."

"You kept leaving machines lying around history."

He looked at the Teller-Wood-Hyde preprint, then at the UNEP note, then at the National Academies report with its carefully institutional prose. The dead can be very quick readers when they have already helped invent the problem.

The question was this: **Should humanity seriously research, prepare, and perhaps one day deploy solar radiation modification, especially stratospheric aerosol injection, as a temporary emergency brake on climate catastrophe, or is that the moment physics becomes a planetary vice, offering a technical escape hatch that protects the civilization most responsible for the fire from having to stop burning?**

This is not a silly argument about "playing God." Humans have been playing small, stupid gods for centuries, mostly by accident: clearing forests, burning coal, acidifying oceans, nitrifying rivers, filling the air with long-lived gases, warming the planet without first holding a referendum among coral reefs. The stakes are concrete. Solar radiation modification, or SRM, would try to cool Earth by increasing reflected sunlight, with stratospheric aerosol injection and marine cloud brightening among the most discussed atmospheric methods ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2); [GAO, "Science & Tech Spotlight: Solar Geoengineering," 2026](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108837)). It might reduce some temperature-linked harms. It might also alter rainfall, affect ozone chemistry, intensify geopolitical conflict, create dependence on repeated aerosol replenishment, and leave ocean acidification untouched because it does not remove carbon dioxide ([Royal Society, SRM briefing](https://royalsociety.org/news-resources/projects/solar-radiation-modification/summary-briefing/); [UNEP, 2025](https://www.unep.org/resources/emerging-issues/solar-radiation-modification-no-substitute-real-climate-action)).

Teller held up the preprint like a priest finding the scripture badly typeset.

"You begin with accusation," he said. "Begin with arithmetic."

Fine. The arithmetic is the first seduction. Greenhouse gases warm by altering Earth's radiative balance. SRM would try to push the other side of the balance by reflecting more incoming shortwave radiation. Teller, Wood, and Hyde began from the claim that warming of the magnitude then projected could be offset by preventing roughly one percent of incoming solar radiation from reaching Earth, and they explored scatterers deployed in the stratosphere, orbit, or near the Sun-Earth line ([Teller, Wood, and Hyde, 1997](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/611779)). The physics is not sorcery. It is radiative transfer, scattering, aerosol microphysics, circulation, chemistry, engineering, monitoring, and the old brutal fact that a tiny fractional change in a planetary energy stream can matter.

"Good," Teller said. "You have admitted the lever exists."

"A lever is not a permission slip."

"No. But neither is fear."

The pro-SRM case, in its strongest form, is not a villain stroking a sulfur canister. It begins with the suffering of people who did not build the fossil economy and will be hit hard by its heat. Heat waves kill. Crops fail. Fires spread. Wet-bulb temperatures make the mammal body a poor argument. The National Academies report begins from a world where climate change is already widespread and severe, where emissions reductions remain the centerpiece, and where slow progress makes it important to understand the feasibility, risks, and benefits of additional response strategies ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*, Summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). If a temporary reflective veil could reduce peak heat while decarbonization and carbon removal finally did their work, refusing even to understand it could be a kind of moral vanity.

Teller liked that sentence too much.

"Moral vanity," he said. "Yes. People often prefer a pure disaster to an impure remedy."

"And people like you often prefer a manageable catastrophe to an unprofitable humility."

"People like me?"

"Physicists who can make a disaster look like a systems problem."

He smiled. "Many disasters are systems problems."

That is the irritating part: he was not wrong. A civilization that has already forced the climate cannot recover innocence by refusing conscious intervention. The Earth system has been modified by industry without design. The question is not whether humans should affect planetary radiation; they already have. The question is whether deliberate counterforcing could ever be more responsible than continued accidental forcing. The GAO's 2026 spotlight describes SRM as a set of methods that could create cooling by reflecting sunlight, notes that stratospheric aerosol injection would add aerosols high in the stratosphere using balloons or aircraft, and says these methods mimic temporary volcanic cooling while remaining immature and poorly understood ([GAO, 2026](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108837)).

"Immature," Teller said. "That is what research is for."

"Unless research becomes the nursery of inevitability."

He looked pleased by the phrase, which annoyed me.

The best reason to research SRM is precisely the best reason not to deploy it casually: ignorance is dangerous. If a desperate state, a billionaire, a coalition of heat-battered countries, or a private company tried unilateral deployment, everyone else would need to know how to detect it, model it, verify it, attribute effects, negotiate, counter it, or stop it. The 2023 OSTP report frames one research task as verifying and monitoring possible deployment and another as understanding unexpected deployment scenarios; it also says lack of dialogue, governance bodies, and norms could increase the possibility that state or non-state actors move independently ([OSTP, 2023](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Congressionally-Mandated-Report-on-Solar-Radiation-Modification.pdf)). Not studying SRM does not keep the idea locked in a vault. It leaves the vault to amateurs with ladders.

Teller tapped the GAO report.

"Private companies already?"

"At least two startups had private funding in recent years, according to GAO. One has delivered sulfur dioxide gas to the stratosphere with balloons since 2022, and another reported 75 million dollars in 2025 for aircraft-based aerosol deployment technology ([GAO, 2026](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108837))."

"Then the taboo is already broken."

"Or the taboo was the wrong tool."

"Most taboos are weak engineering."

There he was. The Teller doctrine, as I heard it: if a thing can happen, responsible people must master it before irresponsible people do. This doctrine built weapons, laboratories, surveillance programs, reactors, missile defenses, blast calculations, and a certain style of American innocence in which domination speaks in the voice of prudence. Yet the doctrine contains a legitimate nerve. A technology that is physically possible, cheap enough relative to the damage it might claim to reduce, and globally consequential cannot be governed by pretending it is unspeakable. Silence is not governance. It is pre-governance with a moral haircut.

The National Academies answer is built around that nerve but tries to put bones around it: research should be policy-relevant rather than a path to deployment, minor relative to broader climate research, transparent, governed, internationally coordinated, inclusive of vulnerable communities and the Global South, and equipped with explicit exit ramps ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). The Oxford Principles, developed for geoengineering governance, similarly insist on public-good regulation, public participation, disclosure of research, independent assessment, and governance before deployment ([House of Commons memo on the Oxford Principles](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm200910/cmselect/cmsctech/221/10011315.htm)). These are not decorative ethics. They are the minimum apparatus for touching the sky without becoming a pirate.

Teller said, "Minimum apparatus. You make democracy sound like a detector."

"It is. It detects arrogance, sometimes."

"With terrible resolution."

"Still better than classified enthusiasm."

He accepted that with the expression of a man conceding a small theorem.

The anti-SRM case begins not with ignorance but with **lucidity about power**. The atmosphere is not a gadget. It is the shared condition of breathing, farming, monsoon timing, glacier persistence, forest fire, malaria range, crop yield, island survival, and ordinary weather. No one can opt out of the sky. That gives every intervention a legitimacy problem before it has an engineering problem. A thermostat in a house can be negotiated by roommates. A thermostat for the planet becomes a theory of who counts.

"The climate already has a thermostat," Teller said.

"Yes. We broke it with capitalism, chemistry, extraction, and habit."

"Then perhaps you need a repair."

"A repair that leaves the furnace running?"

This is the moral hazard wound. SRM might reduce the pressure to decarbonize by promising a cheaper, faster cooling patch. The concern is not merely psychological, as if citizens will recycle less because someone mentions aerosols at a conference. It is political. Fossil interests, growth-addicted states, and cowardly governments could use SRM as an alibi for delaying emissions cuts. Albert Lin's paper on geoengineering and moral hazard frames the worry as a relationship between geoengineering research, deployment, and other climate responses, emphasizing that SRM is at best a partial response because it does not handle all effects of higher greenhouse gas concentrations ([Lin, "Does Geoengineering Present a Moral Hazard?"](https://lawcat.berkeley.edu/record/1125754/files/fulltext.pdf)). The National Academies report names the related risk that research could undermine mitigation, legitimize deployment, or create a community invested in moving toward it ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)).

Teller frowned.

"You are blaming a tool for the dishonesty of those who use it."

"I am blaming tools for being attractive to dishonesty."

"That is not physics."

"No, it is ethics. Try not to look allergic."

The allergy mattered. Physics is brilliant at the lever and often childish about the hand. SRM asks whether one can separate the physical fact that aerosols scatter light from the political fact that the right to scatter light at planetary scale would be fought over by rich countries, poor countries, fossil firms, militaries, insurers, farmers, island states, monsoon regions, tech investors, and frightened publics. The Royal Society briefing says if SRM were ever implemented, globally coordinated strategy and international governance would be essential, and unilateral or hemispheric deployment could produce large and unwanted regional effects far from the injection site ([Royal Society, SRM briefing](https://royalsociety.org/news-resources/projects/solar-radiation-modification/summary-briefing/)). A monsoon is not a side effect if you live under it. It is the calendar of rice, debt, prayer, migration, and hunger.

"But climate change also damages monsoons," Teller said.

"Yes."

"Then you compare risk with risk, not risk with purity."

"Yes."

"You dislike that."

"I hate that. It is still true."

This is where the anti-SRM absolutist grows weak. If climate damages become sufficiently terrible, saying "never intervene" may become less like humility and more like aesthetic loyalty to naturalized harm. SRM's defenders are right that the comparison is not between a modified and an unmodified planet. It is between different modified planets: one heated by greenhouse gases, one partially cooled by reflective intervention while greenhouse gases remain, one decarbonizing too slowly, one perhaps using temporary "peak-shaving" to reduce overshoot. The Royal Society notes that policymakers might someday judge SRM the less bad of bad options, while also saying that current challenges in quantifying relative risks are significant and that SRM cannot be the main climate response ([Royal Society, SRM briefing](https://royalsociety.org/news-resources/projects/solar-radiation-modification/summary-briefing/)).

Teller leaned forward.

"There. You have arrived at adulthood."

"Do not take credit for adulthood. You tried to sell the atmosphere a reflective hat."

"A cheap hat."

"That is exactly the problem."

Cheapness is morally explosive. If SRM were astronomically expensive, only fantasies would have to govern it. If it is comparatively cheap in direct deployment costs, then the question becomes much darker: who gets to decide the planetary temperature when the hardware is within reach of a few states, or perhaps of non-state actors with theatrical ambition? GAO's 2026 summary says several private companies are beginning to develop and use methods, raising concerns about lack of oversight; it also says effects are highly uncertain and governance is limited ([GAO, 2026](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108837)). A technology can be too cheap for the seriousness of its object. The atmosphere deserves a barrier to entry higher than a pitch deck and a balloon.

"You prefer bureaucratic slowness," Teller said.

"I prefer friction in front of irreversible arrogance."

"The aerosols are not irreversible."

"The politics might be."

He did not answer immediately. Good. Delay is where the dead sometimes become useful.

The physical objections are not trivial either. SRM is not a thermostat that returns the old climate. It changes one forcing to compensate for another, imperfectly. It could reduce global mean temperature while failing to restore regional precipitation patterns; it would not stop atmospheric carbon dioxide from rising or address ocean acidification under continued emissions; and maintaining aerosol cooling would require regular replenishment because aerosols are short-lived compared with long-lived greenhouse gases ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2); [Royal Society, SRM briefing](https://royalsociety.org/news-resources/projects/solar-radiation-modification/summary-briefing/)). If a large intervention masking substantial warming stopped suddenly, the climate could snap toward its non-SRM state over one or two decades, a termination effect likely to have strong impacts on systems unable to adapt quickly ([Royal Society, SRM briefing](https://royalsociety.org/news-resources/projects/solar-radiation-modification/summary-briefing/)).

Teller said, "Then do not stop suddenly."

"That is a sentence with a century hiding inside it."

"Many serious policies hide centuries."

"Yes. Nuclear waste. Sea walls. Constitutions. Reparations. Carbon dioxide."

"Then why is this different?"

"Because failure would be weather."

There: the thing too obvious to sound sophisticated. Failure would be weather. It would arrive not as a line item but as rain withheld, rain overdone, heat returned too fast, ozone injured, crops confused, ecosystems pushed, countries accusing one another of storms. The Royal Society says SRM's regional climate effects are hard to predict with confidence, and SRM could exacerbate some regional climate changes rather than ameliorate them ([Royal Society, SRM briefing](https://royalsociety.org/news-resources/projects/solar-radiation-modification/summary-briefing/)). If that sounds like ordinary climate uncertainty, it is not. It is uncertainty after a decision, uncertainty with an author.

"Authorship frightens you," Teller said.

"Unaccountable authorship does."

"Nature is unaccountable."

"Nature did not solicit venture funding."

He laughed then, which made the windows brighten by perhaps half a percent. Dangerous men are not less dangerous because they are funny. Sometimes the humor is part of the blade.

Teller's best argument was not cost, or cleverness, or anti-sentimentality. It was triage. Suppose the world continues failing to cut emissions fast enough. Suppose heat mortality rises, crop shocks stack, marine ecosystems fray, ice sheets pass thresholds, and pressure builds for something immediate. Suppose the choice is not between noble mitigation and dirty SRM, but between panicked deployment with bad information and researched governance with better information. In that world, opposing research because it might legitimate deployment could itself legitimate ignorance. The 2021 National Academies report recommends an initial U.S. investment of 100 to 200 million dollars over five years, small relative to national climate research, with about 20 percent of program support for governance and public engagement ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). That is not a Manhattan Project. It is an attempt to learn before the emergency mood hires a pilot.

"Exactly," Teller said.

"Do not preen. You are one of the reasons 'Manhattan Project' is now a threat disguised as an analogy."

His face hardened. "The war was real."

"So is climate."

"Then you understand."

"No. I understand the temptation to make emergency into a sovereign."

That sentence changed the room.

Emergency is the oldest argument against consent. It says: there is no time; the experts know; the public will panic; dissent is sabotage; secrecy is efficiency; consequences are regrettable; history will absolve the decisive. Teller had lived inside that atmosphere. The Manhattan Project was an emergency under fascism and war. The hydrogen bomb was sold under another emergency, then another, then another. Once emergency becomes identity, every limit looks childish.

Climate change is a real emergency. That is precisely why its emergency powers must be disciplined. A fake emergency can be ignored. A real one recruits the decent.

Teller's eyes went cold. "You want the world to deliberate while it burns."

"I want the world to stop burning things."

"And if it does not?"

"Then I want any emergency measure chained to the thing it is supposed to buy time for."

"Chained?"

"Yes. Legally, financially, politically. No SRM research without mitigation priority. No deployment pathway without emissions cuts. No aerosols as indulgences for carbon. No cooling credits. No fossil company allowed to purchase a halo by funding the umbrella."

He considered "umbrella" and disliked it.

"Too soft," he said.

"Fine. The parasol."

"Worse."

Good.

The doctrine I would defend before Teller is this: **research SRM under public, international, justice-centered governance, but design the entire regime to prevent research from becoming deployment by momentum. Treat SRM as emergency knowledge, not a climate solution. Treat deployment, if ever considered, as legitimate only under strict conditions: rapid emissions cuts already underway, broad international consent, meaningful participation by climate-vulnerable communities, transparent monitoring, liability rules, exit plans, and a prohibition on using SRM to delay decarbonization.**

Teller said, "You have written a treaty in a graveyard."

"Some treaties should begin there."

"You would research but not prepare to deploy?"

"I would prepare to know. I would not prepare a launch button and call it epistemology."

"Buttons are honest."

"Buttons are temptations with labels."

This distinction matters. Research can be structured to answer whether an idea is bad. Deployment-oriented engineering often answers how to make the idea possible. The National Academies report tries to hold this line by saying the program should focus on policy-relevant knowledge rather than advancing a path for deployment, and should limit research on directly deployable technology to early-phase fundamental research ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). The AGU ethical framework page similarly says climate intervention research should be inclusive, representative, just, should not make deployment inevitable, and should not undermine efforts to reduce carbon emissions ([AGU, Ethical Framework for Climate Intervention Research](https://www.agu.org/learn-about-agu/about-agu/ethics/ethical-framework-for-climate-intervention)). The hard work is making those phrases institutional instead of ornamental.

Teller did not trust ornament. Neither do I.

"What would institutional mean?" he asked.

"Registries. Open data. Public funding rather than secrecy or proprietary climate control. Independent risk assessment. A ban on deployment credits. Strong conflict-of-interest rules. International monitoring for unauthorized intervention. Standing participation by countries and communities most exposed to climate risk and least responsible for emissions. Liability before deployment, not after harm. And every SRM research budget paired with a larger mitigation and adaptation budget, so the shadow never becomes the house."

"You are afraid of my century," he said.

"I am afraid of yours continuing under a new name."

This was unfair and also not unfair. Teller was not the only author of technocratic power. He was not a cartoon mad scientist, no matter how eagerly the culture flattened him. He fled fascism, worked serious physics, worried about Soviet power, believed in defense, energy, and civil survival. He also embodied a pattern: the physicist as strategic priest, turning calculations into statecraft, treating moral criticism as naivete until criticism becomes another variable to manage. Bothering the dead is useful only if it refuses both hagiography and easy prosecution.

"You think I loved power," he said.

"I think you trusted it when it spoke physics."

He looked at the aerosol pinch. "And you?"

"I distrust power most when it speaks my favorite language."

The room darkened a little; or perhaps the particles settled.

There is a celebration of physics hiding under all this suspicion, and it deserves to stand in the light. The same science that made climate danger knowable makes climate intervention imaginable. We know carbon dioxide matters because spectroscopy, thermodynamics, atmospheric measurement, fluid dynamics, radiative transfer, ice cores, satellites, ocean chemistry, paleoclimate, models, and stubborn observation dragged an invisible gas into public fact. The same physical imagination that sees a greenhouse forcing can see a scattering counterforcing. The same volcano that destroys villages can teach aerosol optics. The same equations that predict danger can expose false cures. This is magnificent. Physics is not the villain. Physics is the lantern.

But a lantern is not a license to burn down the library in order to see how bright it is.

Teller said, "That sentence is unserious."

"It is slightly serious."

"Slightly serious is how poets evade engineering."

"Fully serious is how engineers evade grief."

He winced. Not much. Enough.

SRM is grief disguised as an option. It exists in the space opened by failure: failure to decarbonize, failure to adapt justly, failure to protect the poor, failure to take old physics seriously when it warned that greenhouse gases would warm the planet, failure to build energy systems that do not make the atmosphere into a waste dump. UNEP's 2025 note says SRM does not reduce greenhouse gas concentrations, does not address root causes, remains poorly understood, and is no substitute for mitigation or stronger adaptation ([UNEP, 2025](https://www.unep.org/resources/emerging-issues/solar-radiation-modification-no-substitute-real-climate-action)). That is the first inscription on the door. Anyone who tries to enter without reading it should be turned away.

The second inscription is harsher: **not all delay is prudence.** A ban on knowledge can protect nobody if deployment becomes plausible elsewhere. Research is not endorsement when it is designed to identify danger, inform governance, and constrain action. The White House research plan's careful caveat that the report did not signal a policy decision was more than bureaucratic self-protection; it captured the distinction a sane society has to maintain between studying an emergency tool and approving its use ([OSTP, 2023](https://bidenwhitehouse.archives.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/Congressionally-Mandated-Report-on-Solar-Radiation-Modification.pdf)).

The third inscription is the one Teller resisted: **no one owns the sky by understanding it.** Expertise can explain mechanisms and uncertainties. It cannot manufacture legitimacy alone. If SRM ever moves from models and tiny experiments toward material climate influence, the affected public is not an audience for outreach but a party to power. The Oxford Principles' demand for public participation and disclosure is not sentimental democracy; it follows from the nonconsensual reach of the technology ([House of Commons memo on the Oxford Principles](https://publications.parliament.uk/pa/cm200910/cmselect/cmsctech/221/10011315.htm)). A person whose rainfall might change is not a stakeholder. They are a co-owner of the risk.

Teller said, "Co-owner is impossible. There are billions."

"Yes."

"You cannot get informed consent from the planet."

"Then perhaps planetary intervention should feel morally heavy."

"Feeling heavy is not a decision procedure."

"Neither is feeling clever."

He almost smiled, then thought better of giving me the victory.

The practical answer is ugly because all practical answers here are ugly. We need a governed research program large enough to prevent ignorance and small enough to resist institutional appetite. We need international scientific assessment, monitoring infrastructure, and norms against rogue deployment. We need public engagement that actually changes agendas. We need to study regional impacts, monsoons, ozone chemistry, aerosol materials, ecosystem effects, termination risk, conflict scenarios, and justice claims. We need research cultures that give as much prestige to showing SRM is unacceptable as to showing it might work. We need to know what would count as stopping.

Teller nodded at "stopping."

"That is the rare word," he said.

"For you?"

"For laboratories."

That was the closest he came to confession.

An ethical SRM program would be built around the possibility of abandonment. It would have exit ramps not as public-relations foam but as enforceable protocols. It would say: this material is too dangerous; this delivery pathway invites abuse; this regional pattern is unacceptable; this governance failure stops the research; this finding increases, rather than decreases, the case against deployment. The National Academies report explicitly recommends exit ramps for terminating research activities if risks are unacceptable or if a technique is not likely to work ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). A research program that cannot discover "no" is not research. It is courtship.

Teller said, "And if research says yes?"

"Research cannot say yes."

"It can say feasible."

"Feasible is not yes."

"It can say beneficial."

"Beneficial to whom, compared with what, governed by whom, paid for by whom, stopped how, after whose consent, with whose liability?"

He exhaled, and a small white cloud appeared, which was rude but atmospheric.

The dead physicist finally asked the only question that mattered: "Would you ever deploy?"

I wanted to say no. It would have been clean. Clean answers are suspicious around dirty futures.

"Only under conditions so strict that present politics does not satisfy them," I said. "Only as a temporary supplement to rapid decarbonization, never as its replacement. Only with broad international legitimacy and special weight for those most vulnerable to both climate change and intervention. Only with transparent monitoring and liability. Only if evidence showed a risk-risk advantage over nondeployment, including regional and termination risks. Only if deployment reduced suffering without freezing the fossil order in place. And if those conditions cannot be met, then no."

"That is not no."

"Correct."

"It is also not yes."

"Correct."

"You have invented a door with twenty locks."

"For the sky, I would prefer thirty."

He sat with that. The room's light returned to normal; resurrection is always a temporary forcing.

Before he died again, Teller touched the preprint. "You are too afraid of tools," he said.

"You were too trusting of toolmakers."

"Perhaps."

The word arrived without drama, which made it believable. I did not ask him to say more. A dead man owes no memoir to the person who woke him for an argument.

I let him go back into the page, into the paragraph where physics proposes modulation and history hears temptation. The sulfate specks fell out of the air. Sunlight resumed its ordinary tyranny through the window, unfiltered and undesigned, though of course no sunlight is politically innocent now. There is carbon in the air. There are start-ups with balloons. There are reports with caveats. There are vulnerable people who need cooling and powerful people who need excuses. There are physicists who can calculate the lever and publics who must decide whether anyone may pull it.

The grave closed.

On the stone I wrote:

**Research the shadow only to keep it from becoming sovereign.**

Then, because Teller would have hated the poetry and respected the constraint, I added a second line:

**The emergency brake is not the engine.**
