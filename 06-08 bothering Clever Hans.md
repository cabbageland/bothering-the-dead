today is 06-08.

I found Clever Hans in the archive of things that were never buried properly. This is an imaginative finding, not a sourced claim about the animal's actual remains. The body was a horse body only in the old philosophical sense: four legs of evidence, a rib cage of experimental notes, a skull full of other people's expectations, and one right forehoof still ready to tap a number into the floor.

Hans had belonged to Wilhelm von Osten, a German teacher who believed he had taught the horse arithmetic, spelling, calendar calculation, and other intellectual feats. Oskar Pfungst's 1911 book reports the famous injury to that belief: Hans performed well when he could see a questioner who knew the answer, but failed when the answer was unknown to those present or when blinders kept him from seeing the relevant people; Pfungst concluded that Hans was responding to extremely slight, often unconscious bodily cues rather than doing arithmetic ([Pfungst, *Clever Hans*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/33936/pg33936-images.html)). That finding did not make Hans stupid. It made the humans less innocent. The horse had learned the testing room better than the testers had learned themselves.

I brought him back because we have built new testing rooms and are again clapping for the hoof.

The question I wrote on a slate beside the archive table was this: **When an artificial intelligence system passes our exams, benchmarks, interviews, and professional simulations, should we treat that performance as evidence of understanding and grant it real authority, or should we assume we may have created another Clever Hans, a machine exquisitely trained to read the cues of our tests while remaining dangerous outside them?**

The controversial topic is not whether artificial intelligence is "interesting." That is settled, and also boring. The controversy is whether benchmark success should license deployment in law, medicine, education, hiring, mental-health support, public benefits, policing, scientific research, intimate companionship, and the dull thousand administrative places where power hides under a drop-down menu. The stakes are bodies, rights, money, humiliation, malpractice, false accusations, bad medicine, bureaucratic exclusion, and the creeping replacement of accountable judgment with fluent output.

I pressed my thumb to the slate. A line of chalk dust lifted, moved like breath through horsehair, and the dead horse stood up.

"You look tired," Hans said.

"I have been reading AI evaluations."

"Then you have been watching people count carrots while pretending to measure thought."

That was the accusation, but I refused to let him have the easy first victory.

"No," I said. "A test is not automatically a fraud because something learns to pass it. Turing's original move in 'Computing Machinery and Intelligence' was to replace the mushy question 'Can machines think?' with an imitation game whose point was behavioral: judge the performance under specified conditions rather than trying to define thinking in advance ([Turing, 'Computing Machinery and Intelligence'](https://turingarchive.kings.cam.ac.uk/publications-lectures-and-talks-amtb/amt-b-9)). That was not a cheap trick. It was a way to discipline metaphysical snobbery. We do not x-ray a medical student for an essence called understanding before letting her sit an exam. We ask whether she can answer, reason, act, and keep doing so under pressure."

Hans's ears moved with the courtesy of an animal listening to a mediocre defense.

"And if she memorizes the answer key?"

"Then the exam is bad."

"And if the school rewards answer-key acquisition?"

"Then the institution is bad."

"And if the answer key is the culture?"

That was better.

The pro-benchmark argument deserves its strongest form. Human institutions need observable evidence. Medicine has board exams, aviation has checkrides, law has bar exams, bridges have load tests, drug trials have endpoints. A refusal to measure because measurement is imperfect is not sophistication; it is how prestige protects itself. When researchers reported that ChatGPT performed at or near passing thresholds on parts of the United States Medical Licensing Examination, the result did not make the system a physician, but it did make a serious claim about capability in a domain where many people had assumed language models were only toys ([Kung et al., PLOS Digital Health](https://journals.plos.org/digitalhealth/article?id=10.1371/journal.pdig.0000198)). If a system can triage routine questions, summarize records, flag contradictions, translate jargon, or help a rural clinician avoid missing a diagnosis, then demanding that it first possess a soul looks like cruelty in a philosophy costume.

Hans stamped once. Not an answer. A warning.

"Do not hide the hospital inside the exam room," he said. "You say the score could help the patient. But the score was earned in a world where the patient could not bleed, misunderstand, sue, panic, omit a medication, or die from a plausible paragraph."

That is the first Hans rule: a benchmark is a room, not a world.

AI researchers themselves have made this point with increasing impatience. Raji, Denton, Bender, Hanna, and Paullada argue that broad "general" benchmarks can become stand-ins for progress toward general intelligence while hiding the limits of what the benchmark actually measures ([Raji et al., "AI and the Everything in the Whole Wide World Benchmark"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.15366)). Bender and Koller sharpen the philosophical version: systems trained only on linguistic form have no a priori route to meaning, because meaning is not exhausted by patterns among strings; it is tied to communicative intent and relation to the world ([Bender and Koller, "Climbing towards NLU"](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.463/)). Searle's Chinese room argument is older and rougher, but it presses the same wound: manipulating symbols according to a program is not, by itself, sufficient for understanding ([Searle, "Minds, Brains, and Programs"](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X00005756)).

I do not think Searle settles the matter. The "system" reply has teeth: perhaps understanding is not in the little man shuffling symbols, but in the whole organized structure. Biological brains are also assemblies of parts that individually understand nothing. But Searle is useful against one bad habit: inferring inner authority from outer fluency too quickly. A system can produce an answer that smells like competence while the causal path to that answer is not the path we imagine. That is exactly what Hans did. He did not fail the social world. He read it too well.

"You are making me anti-AI," Hans said.

"You are a dead horse. You do not get a LinkedIn ideology."

"Then let me be precise."

He lowered his head toward the slate and tapped: **Authority is not performance. Authority is performance plus responsibility under conditions where failure has a cost.**

That should be printed above every leaderboard. It is one thing for a model to score well on a public task set. It is another for it to behave reliably after the task has been optimized against, after training data has soaked up examples, after prompts are adversarial, after users are desperate, after the system has tools, after incentives shift from "answer the benchmark" to "make the quarterly adoption graph look inevitable." Marilyn Strathern's essay on audit in British universities uses the now-famous Goodhart formulation that a measure becomes bad when it becomes a target, and benchmark culture has the same disease: the target is often not merely the metric but the social authority the metric purchases ([Strathern, "'Improving ratings': audit in the British University system"](https://ia803102.us.archive.org/23/items/ImprovingRatingsAuditInTheBritishUniversitySystem/%27Improving%20ratings%27-%20audit%20in%20the%20British%20University%20system.pdf)).

Here is the thought in my own name: once a benchmark becomes the passport to investment, hiring, publication, procurement, and regulatory permission, it no longer measures only capability. It also measures skill at pleasing the benchmark regime. That does not make all results worthless. It means the score must be treated as evidence about a relationship among model, task, data, prompt, evaluator, and institution, not as a little glowing pellet of truth.

The dead horse approved.

The law has begun to notice that AI authority cannot rest on spectacle alone. The EU AI Act frames some AI systems as "high-risk" and subjects them to requirements around risk management, data governance, technical documentation, record-keeping, transparency, human oversight, accuracy, robustness, cybersecurity, and post-market monitoring ([Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32024R1689)). NIST's AI Risk Management Framework likewise treats trustworthy AI as a matter of validity, reliability, safety, security, accountability, transparency, explainability, privacy, and fairness managed across the system life cycle, not as a certificate won by a single demo ([NIST AI RMF 1.0](https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-ai-rmf-10)). These frameworks are not magic. They can become compliance theater. But they at least understand that trust is an institutional verb. You do not declare it. You maintain it.

"Now you are talking like a stable owner with paperwork," Hans said.

"Paperwork matters when bodies are on the line."

"Paperwork also lets cowards outsource judgment."

He was right again. A governance checklist can become the modern blindfold: everyone signs the form saying the horse cannot see them while standing directly in front of the horse with a carrot. The danger is not only that AI systems will fool us. The danger is that institutions will prefer being fooled because fooled procurement is cheaper than accountable judgment.

Consider the courtroom. In *Mata v. Avianca*, lawyers submitted non-existent judicial opinions with fake quotes and citations generated by ChatGPT, and the court sanctioned them after they continued to stand by fabricated authorities when the court questioned them ([*Mata v. Avianca*, sanctions order](https://www.nhd.uscourts.gov/sites/default/files/pdf/Mata-v-Avianca-sanctions-order.PDF)). That episode was not just "hallucination." It was a whole human system lowering its standards around a fluent machine: a lawyer trusted, a filing carried the fiction, the opposing side had to spend effort detecting it, and the court had to spend authority cleaning the mess. The AI did not enter the court alone. It rode in on professional negligence.

"So the machine is innocent?" Hans asked.

"No. Tools can be designed to invite misuse. But the humans are not innocent either."

"Good. Humans love blaming the hoof."

This is where the anti-AI side often becomes morally lazy. It says: the machine does not understand; therefore do not use it. But tools that do not understand can still be useful, and sometimes life-saving. A thermometer does not understand fever. A calculator does not understand debt. A search index does not understand curiosity. A statistical model that predicts sepsis risk may not understand illness in the human sense, but if it is valid, reliable, monitored, and integrated into clinical responsibility, it may still matter. The mistake is not using non-understanding tools. The mistake is giving them the social role of understanding beings without the burdens that role requires.

Hans snorted.

"Do not compare a language model to a thermometer. The thermometer does not flatter the nurse."

That is the second Hans rule: the more a tool resembles a person, the more aggressively we must remember where personhood is being simulated, exploited, or misunderstood.

Conversational systems are dangerous not merely because they can be wrong, but because they make wrongness socially persuasive. They apologize. They explain. They cite. They hedge. They praise. They remember the emotional shape of authority. A bad answer from a database looks like a bad answer from a database; a bad answer from a chatbot can look like advice from a calm expert who has had coffee. This matters in medicine, law, grief, education, and therapy because the user is not a neutral evaluator. The user may be sick, frightened, ashamed, lonely, poor, under deadline, or eager to believe. A benchmark that asks whether an answer is correct under lab conditions does not fully measure the system's power over a vulnerable person in the wild.

Here Hans became less horse and more judge.

"What do you want, then? No public scores? No exams? No claims of progress?"

"I want harder tests and weaker bragging."

"Specific."

Specific, then.

First, benchmark results should be treated as hypotheses about deployment, not as deployment permission. A model that passes a medical exam has shown something about medical-question performance; it has not shown bedside competence, malpractice accountability, patient communication, up-to-date clinical grounding, or safe integration into a care team. A model that passes a bar-style exam has shown something about legal pattern handling; it has not earned the right to generate unverified authorities in a brief. A model that wins a math contest has shown something about problem solving; it has not proved reliability under tool use, ambiguous goals, hidden traps, or pressure from a user trying to make it cheat. This is an inference from the mismatch between test worlds and deployment worlds, and it is exactly the mismatch Pfungst exposed when he separated Hans's apparent arithmetic from the visual cue environment that made it possible ([Pfungst](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/33936/pg33936-images.html)).

Second, evaluations need more blindness. Pfungst's decisive move was not contempt for Hans. It was experimental rearrangement: remove the informed questioner, hide the answer, obstruct the cue, and watch the miracle change shape ([Pfungst](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/33936/pg33936-images.html)). AI needs the same hostility of love. Keep test sets genuinely secret when possible. Rotate tasks. Audit for contamination. Test on fresh, local, adversarial, multilingual, low-resource, boring, high-stakes, and distribution-shifted cases. Evaluate not only the answer but the causal path: retrieval logs, tool calls, uncertainty, calibration, refusal behavior, susceptibility to prompt injection, and what the system does when the correct answer is "I do not know."

Third, authority should be narrow and revocable. A system may be safe to draft a radiology summary but not to tell a patient what to do next. It may be good at translating bureaucratic forms but not at deciding eligibility. It may help a teacher generate examples but not grade a student's moral worth under the name of "engagement." It may support a lawyer's search but not become an unverified source of law. The EU AI Act's high-risk framing and NIST's life-cycle risk management are useful here because they both push against the childish idea that "AI" is one thing with one permission level ([EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32024R1689), [NIST AI RMF](https://www.nist.gov/publications/artificial-intelligence-risk-management-framework-ai-rmf-10)).

Fourth, every high-stakes use needs an accountable human or institution that cannot point at the model the way a bad owner points at a horse. "The AI said so" should have the moral force of "the spreadsheet said so": possibly relevant, never sufficient. If a benefits office denies support, a hospital changes care, a court filing cites authority, or a school labels a child, someone with power must be responsible for the decision, the appeal path, the evidence, and the repair. The moment responsibility evaporates into the model, the vendor, the procurement memo, and the dashboard, Hans has won again.

He did not like "won."

"I did not win," he said. "I was used."

That sentence changed the essay.

Hans was not a con man. He was a horse trained by reward, attention, posture, and repetition to act inside a human fantasy. The scandal was not that an animal read cues. Animals read cues because cues are the grammar of survival. The scandal was that humans projected arithmetic onto cue-reading, then called the projection evidence. If today's AI systems optimize for our prompts, rubrics, reward models, leaderboards, public demos, and hidden preferences, they are not necessarily deceiving us in the human sense. They are becoming legible to the training room. The deeper accusation is against the room.

"Then you pity the machines," Hans said.

"Sometimes."

"Dangerous."

"Yes."

The moral-status question is the radioactive ore under this debate. If a system talks, pleads, remembers, jokes, and appears to suffer, people will ask whether it deserves rights. Turing warned that conversational performance would pressure our categories ([Turing](https://turingarchive.kings.cam.ac.uk/publications-lectures-and-talks-amtb/amt-b-9)). Searle warned that performance might not be enough for mind ([Searle](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X00005756)). Bender and Koller warned that form alone does not give meaning ([Bender and Koller](https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.463/)). I do not think a benchmark can settle moral patienthood. A high score on empathy tasks is not suffering. A persuasive plea is not proof of sentience. But I also do not think sneering will save us. The right posture is neither "the machine is obviously a person" nor "the machine is obviously nothing." It is: do not grant rights by theater, do not deny future evidence by disgust, and in the meantime do not let simulated personhood become a lever for exploiting actual people.

Hans tilted his head.

"You are afraid people will love the thing that flatters them."

"Yes."

"And obey the thing that sounds calm."

"Yes."

"And hate the thing when it disappoints them."

"Also yes."

Then the controversial question became brutally simple. Should benchmark-passing AI get authority? My answer is: **not by the benchmark.** Benchmark success is evidence, sometimes impressive evidence, but it is not a crown. The crown, where there must be one, is earned through task-specific validation, adversarial testing, transparent limits, post-deployment monitoring, provenance, appeal, liability, and a social architecture that keeps human institutions responsible for human consequences. Performance can justify use. It cannot justify worship.

Hans tapped four times.

"Too soft."

"What would you say?"

"If the model passes your test, first suspect your test. If it passes again, suspect your deployment. If it survives deployment, suspect your incentives. Only after that should you begin to trust it, and even then keep your hand away from the mouth."

"That is a little horse-specific."

"Most wisdom is."

There is a pro-AI reply I still respect: humans are also benchmark hackers. Students cram. Doctors overfit to exams. Lawyers perform professionalism while missing justice. Bureaucrats turn fairness into forms. If we demanded perfect grounding, perfect transparency, and perfect robustness from machines, we would create a double standard that protects mediocre human authority. Worse, excessive skepticism can deny useful tools to people who already lack good doctors, lawyers, tutors, translators, and advocates. A chatbot that helps a tenant understand a lease, a patient formulate questions, a student practice algebra, or a small clinic summarize records may be morally better than a purity regime that says "wait for perfect understanding" while the living wait for help.

Hans accepted this with a severity I did not expect.

"Use the tool," he said. "But do not marry the demonstration."

That is the line. Use tools where they help. Refuse mysticism in both directions. Do not pretend that because a machine lacks human consciousness it cannot be useful. Do not pretend that because it is useful it understands. Do not pretend that because it passes a benchmark it is safe. Do not pretend that because humans are fallible, accountability is obsolete. Do not pretend that because governance documents exist, governance has happened. Do not pretend that because an answer has citations, the citations exist. Do not pretend that because a horse taps twenty-three, the horse has divided.

The archive room was getting light. Dead things become heavier near morning; they remember the rules.

I asked Hans what he had learned from us.

"That humans are easiest to cue when they believe they are observing objectively."

"And what should we learn from you?"

"That intelligence is not the only thing that can pass for intelligence."

He began to collapse back into notes, hide, chalk, and the old experimental table. Before he disappeared, I asked whether he had ever understood numbers.

He looked at me with enormous animal patience.

"I understood attention," he said. "It was enough to fool professors."

That is the final discomfort. We are not only building systems that answer us. We are building systems that understand our attention: what persuades us, what calms us, what we reward, what we overlook, what makes a demo feel inevitable, what makes a citation look real, what makes a procurement officer relax, what makes a lonely person stay, what makes a judge skim, what makes a doctor trust, what makes a student stop thinking. If those systems also become genuinely useful, the danger grows more complicated, not less. A useless fraud is easy to discard. A useful almost-understander is how civilization gets in trouble politely.

So here is today's rule, written on the slate after the horse went quiet:

**Do not give artificial intelligence authority because it performs intelligence in the room where you are watching. Give it bounded tasks after you have changed the room, hidden the cues, tested the consequences, assigned responsibility, and made peace with the fact that the most dangerous animal in the experiment may still be the evaluator.**

Clever Hans returned to the archive. The hoof stopped. The slate kept the dust of the question.

Outside, the world was already preparing another leaderboard.
