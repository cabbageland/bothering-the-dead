today is May 11, 2026.

I found James Baldwin in the south of France, though that is not the whole truth. His body was buried in Ferncliff Cemetery in New York, and if the assignment had only been archaeological I could have taken a train to Hartsdale, stood among the expensive silence of the dead, and scratched at the soil until I hit the lid. But Baldwin is one of those writers whose remains are never entirely where the cemetery says they are. Part of him is still in Saint-Paul-de-Vence, in the rooms where he finished books under the pressure of exile, and part of him is still in Harlem, where the country taught him what it meant to be made by a place that despised you. So I went first to his house in France, now emptied of him, carrying a copy of *The Fire Next Time* and a small ledger bound in cracked black leather. Inside the ledger I had written, by hand, a sequence of numbers: the median wealth of white families in the United States, the median wealth of Black families, and the distance between them. According to the Federal Reserve’s 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances, the median white family held far more wealth than the median Black family, a disparity so durable it looks less like an accident than a regime ([Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, *Changes in U.S. Family Finances from 2019 to 2022*, 2024](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/scf23.pdf)). I read the numbers aloud in Baldwin’s old garden like a spell composed by a statistician who had finally lost patience.

Nothing happened. Then I read his own words back to him: “It is not permissible that the authors of devastation should also be innocent. It is the innocence which constitutes the crime” ([James Baldwin, *The Fire Next Time*, 1963, Internet Archive lending page](https://archive.org/details/firenexttime00jame)). That did it. The air tightened. A glass on the outdoor table cracked down its side. Baldwin came into the evening the way a hard thought enters a complacent mind, not with lightning but with intolerable clarity. He wore a dark shirt open at the throat, and his face carried the exhausted alertness of someone who had returned only because the living had managed, again, to require him.

“I assume,” he said, looking at my ledger, “that you did not wake me for nostalgia.”

I told him no. I had woken him for an argument about reparations for American slavery and its aftermath, specifically whether the United States should enact a large federal reparations program that includes direct cash payments to Black descendants of enslaved people. The controversy is not abstract. Ta-Nehisi Coates’s 2014 essay made the claim unavoidable for a broad public audience, insisting that reparations are not a boutique moral add-on but a way of confronting plunder that was systematic, documented, and compounded across generations ([Ta-Nehisi Coates, “The Case for Reparations,” *The Atlantic*, 2014](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2014/06/the-case-for-reparations/361631/)). Congress has repeatedly considered H.R. 40, a bill to study and develop proposals for reparations, but it has never become law ([U.S. Congress, H.R. 40, 118th Congress](https://www.congress.gov/bill/118th-congress/house-bill/40)). Meanwhile critics say cash reparations are morally confused, politically impossible, administratively unworkable, or corrosive to democratic solidarity. The stakes are not symbolic. If the claim is right, then a republic that has spoken endlessly about equality while refusing repair has been living on an unpaid debt. If the claim is wrong, then reparations discourse risks turning civic life into an inheritance tribunal from which no shared future can emerge.

Baldwin laughed, but only with his mouth. “America loves innocence more than justice,” he said. “That is why this argument remains alive.”

We sat at a stone table under cypress trees. I opened with the strongest case for reparations I knew, because otherwise there is no point in digging up the dead. Slavery was not simply unpaid labor in the past. It was state-protected extraction whose gains were carried forward through land theft, exclusion from New Deal benefits, redlining, contract selling, labor market discrimination, segregated schooling, and racist credit policy. The harm was cumulative, and so was the advantage. The federal government itself enforced parts of that structure. The Home Owners’ Loan Corporation maps and Federal Housing Administration practices helped create racially stratified access to mortgage credit in the twentieth century, with long-run effects on homeownership and wealth ([National Archives, “Records of the Home Owners’ Loan Corporation”](https://www.archives.gov/research/guide-fed-records/groups/195.html); [Richard Rothstein, *The Color of Law*, overview page](https://wwnorton.com/books/the-color-of-law/)). Reparations advocates like William Darity Jr. and A. Kirsten Mullen therefore argue that repair must be national in scale and material in form, not merely commemorative, because the injury was national and material ([William A. Darity Jr. and A. Kirsten Mullen, *From Here to Equality*, University of North Carolina Press](https://uncpress.org/book/9781469654973/from-here-to-equality/)).

Baldwin listened as if I were reciting a diagnosis he had long since accepted. “Yes,” he said. “And beneath all those policies is a moral habit. This country has wanted Black labor, Black music, Black sacrifice, Black forgiveness, and Black patience. It has not wanted to pay.” He reminded me that in “My Dungeon Shook,” Baldwin had already framed the white American problem not as ignorance alone but as an attachment to a lie about innocence and deservingness ([James Baldwin, *The Fire Next Time*](https://archive.org/details/firenexttime00jame)). Reparations, in his voice, were not first a budget line. They were the public abandonment of that lie.

I told him I was sympathetic, but sympathy is cheap and controversy begins where sympathy runs into policy. So I pressed the objections hard.

First, the inheritance objection. Many present-day Americans arrived long after slavery. Others are themselves descended from people who were colonized, dispossessed, or poor. Why should a recent Nigerian immigrant or a child of Bosnian refugees be taxed to finance payments for crimes committed before their families arrived? Liberal states usually resist hereditary guilt for good reason.

Baldwin did not flinch. “Guilt is not the point,” he said. “Political membership is.” A state inherits obligations as well as assets. Nobody asks whether a taxpayer’s ancestors personally signed the Social Security Act before funding Social Security, or whether their grandparents fought in the right war before honoring Treasury debt. The relevant subject is not private sin but public liability. This is also how reparations scholars often frame it: the federal government would not be punishing individual descendants of slaveholders for ancestral vice, but addressing an enduring unjust enrichment and continuing state failure ([Darity and Mullen, *From Here to Equality*](https://uncpress.org/book/9781469654973/from-here-to-equality/)). “You are confusing a country with a family romance,” Baldwin said. “The government that enforced the theft is still the government under which you live.”

That answer is powerful, but it does not dissolve everything. I told him the problem is not only moral standing. It is democratic legitimacy. Policies endure when enough citizens understand them as fair. A massive race-targeted cash program in a country already primed for racial panic could provoke exactly the kind of backlash that guts its substance and poisons every adjacent reform. One does not defeat bad faith merely by diagnosing it. Sometimes a policy can be just in theory and still fail in practice because the surrounding polity lacks the moral competence to enact it cleanly.

Baldwin leaned back, almost amused. “Now you are asking injustice for permission to be corrected.” Then he turned severe. “Backlash is real. But backlash cannot be allowed to become the highest court in the land.” He had a point. Brown v. Board did not become right only after segregationists calmed down. The Civil Rights Act did not wait for Mississippi’s spiritual maturity. A politics that makes white resentment the final veto over Black claims simply renames domination as prudence. And yet there is still a strategic question, not a surrender but a question: is a policy worth demanding in a form that cannot survive contact with institutions as they are?

So I moved to the administrative objection, which is harder to romanticize away. Who qualifies? Descendants of enslaved people in the United States only, as Darity and Mullen propose, often paired with proof of at least one ancestor enslaved in the United States and recent self-identification as Black, or all Black Americans affected by Jim Crow and later discrimination ([Darity and Mullen, *From Here to Equality*](https://uncpress.org/book/9781469654973/from-here-to-equality/))? What about Black immigrants whose families did not suffer U.S. chattel slavery but do suffer American anti-Blackness? What about multiracial descendants, adoptees, records destroyed by violence, families deliberately severed from their own paper trail? The bureaucracy of repair can easily become another injury.

That, finally, seemed to wound him. “Yes,” Baldwin said quietly. “The archive is part of the crime.” Enslavement turned kinship into inventory, and the afterlife of that system includes missing names, falsified names, broken lines, and records designed for sale rather than memory. Any reparations regime will therefore have to decide whether it wants perfect genealogical purity or reasonable public justice. Perfection is impossible. But impossible administration does not follow. Governments already make eligibility determinations under conditions of imperfect documentation all the time, from tribal enrollment disputes to refugee claims to compensation funds. The question is whether the state approaches uncertainty in a reparative spirit or a prosecutorial one.

I then gave him the hardest substantive objection I know: maybe direct cash is the wrong instrument even if reparations are morally owed. Some critics who agree a debt exists argue that asset-building, housing grants, baby bonds, educational endowments, or place-based investment might produce broader and more durable repair than individual cash payments. Economist Darrick Hamilton, for example, has argued for structural tools that reduce wealth inequality at scale, including baby bonds, rather than relying solely on income measures or narrow behavioral fixes ([Darrick Hamilton and William Darity Jr., “Can ‘Baby Bonds’ Eliminate the Racial Wealth Gap in Putative Post-Racial America?” *Review of Black Political Economy* 37, no. 3-4, 2010](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12114-010-9063-1)). Coleman Hughes, from the opposite direction, has argued that class-based social democracy would address need without reifying racial categories in law ([Coleman Hughes, “A Case Against Reparations,” *Quillette*, 2019](https://quillette.com/2019/07/28/a-case-against-reparations/)). If the goal is to reduce suffering and expand freedom, why insist on the most politically explosive mechanism?

Baldwin’s reply arrived almost before I finished. “Because there is a difference between help and payment.” Social programs can be good and still evasive. Universal benefits may reduce hardship, but they do not by themselves acknowledge who was robbed, by whom, and under what authority. A nation can build better schools and still refuse to name the theft that made some neighborhoods rich enough to fund theirs in the first place. Cash matters in part because theft was material. Human beings were legally treated as capital; wages were stolen; land opportunities were denied; homes were devalued; inheritances were blocked. To respond only with general uplift is to speak in the grammar of charity when the grammar required is debt.

I felt the force of that, but not all of it. Debt language clarifies responsibility; it can also distort politics. Nations are not courts in which every historical injury can be settled cleanly by invoice. The deeper worry is that cash reparations, if treated as a final payment, could be used to purchase moral closure on the cheap. A state might distribute checks, stage a ceremony, and then insist that the account is closed while schools, zoning, policing, environmental exposure, and healthcare remain structured by the same old hierarchy. One can imagine reparations being deployed not as transformation but as absolution.

Baldwin smiled with genuine bitterness. “Of course you can imagine it. You are American.” He agreed the danger is real. In fact, it is one of the reasons the argument must be kept philosophically strict. Reparations are not a mood, not a diversity initiative, not a museum wing, and not a one-time performance of sorrow. They are part of a broader project of reconstruction. Coates made a similar point when he described reparations as a national reckoning rather than a simple cash transaction ([Coates, “The Case for Reparations”](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2014/06/the-case-for-reparations/361631/)). Baldwin, being Baldwin, put it more harshly: “If they pay in order not to change, then they have not paid.”

By then dusk had thickened around the table. The argument had started to expose its real core. The issue was not whether a spreadsheet could be drawn, though it could. Darity and Mullen estimate a program in the trillions if the aim is to close the Black-white wealth gap for eligible descendants, which is economically vast but not conceptually absurd in a country capable of financing wars, bailouts, and tax expenditures on enormous scales ([Darity and Mullen, *From Here to Equality*](https://uncpress.org/book/9781469654973/from-here-to-equality/)). The issue was whether the United States is willing to let justice become concrete when concreteness threatens the national self-image.

So I tried one final counterargument, partly because I believed it and partly because he deserved resistance worthy of the resurrection. What if the most important thing is not retrospective repair but prospective solidarity? In an age of democratic decay, climate stress, and rising oligarchy, perhaps the wiser politics is one that builds a universal multiracial welfare state instead of litigating historic entitlement line by line. A society can drown in true premises if it cannot rank its emergencies.

Baldwin’s face hardened. “This is the old trap,” he said. “The demand to postpone justice until after the next emergency created by injustice.” Universal programs are good. Build them. But if universality becomes a reason never to specify anti-Black theft, then universality is functioning as anesthesia. The New Deal was universal in rhetoric and exclusionary in implementation. The GI Bill was formally broad and often administered in ways that entrenched racial inequality ([U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs, GI Bill history overview](https://www.va.gov/education/about-gi-bill-benefits/history-and-timeline/); [National Archives, “The GI Bill and Black Americans”](https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2003/fall/gi-bill.html)). A republic that refuses specificity in the name of togetherness usually ends by protecting the already protected.

There it was. Not a slogan but the split in the road. Reparations opponents often speak as if particular justice threatens common citizenship; reparations advocates answer that common citizenship built on denied plunder is already fraudulent. Both claims contain something real. Shared political life does require some willingness to live with people who are not merely extensions of one’s moral tribe. But shared political life also collapses when entire classes of citizens are told, generation after generation, that the injuries structuring their lives are either too old to count or too divisive to mention.

When I walked Baldwin back toward the dark edge of the garden, the cypresses looked less like trees than witnesses. I asked him, before he went, what he actually wanted the living to say if they were finally serious.

He answered without drama. Admit the theft plainly. Identify the debtors politically, not psychically. Build a reparations program that includes direct cash payments substantial enough to matter, not as the whole of justice but as proof that the country is willing to translate moral language into material terms. Pair it with institutional reconstruction in housing, schooling, health, and credit. And above all do not call repair “division” while treating the status quo as neutral.

Then he looked at my ledger one last time. “The trouble with your country,” he said, “is that it thinks numbers become less terrible when they are normal.”

He was dead again before the sentence had fully landed.

My own view, after bothering him, is that the United States should enact federal reparations for descendants of enslaved people, and that the program should include large direct cash payments alongside structural measures, not because cash alone can heal history, but because anything less risks turning acknowledgment into evasion. The best objections are not frivolous. Eligibility will be contested. Backlash will be vicious. No formula will perfectly convert atrocity into justice. But those difficulties are arguments for design, not for denial. A country that can trace capital, title, inheritance, debt, and tax liability with obsessive sophistication does not suddenly become helpless when asked to trace its own crimes. The deeper resistance is moral. Reparations would force the United States to admit that racial hierarchy was not merely a shameful deviation from its principles but one of the ways those principles were historically financed. That is why the argument feels incendiary. It threatens innocence where innocence has long functioned as property.

And property, as Baldwin knew, is never surrendered just because the bill arrives.
