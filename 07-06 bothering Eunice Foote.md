today is 07-06.

I found Eunice Newton Foote in Green-Wood Cemetery, where Brooklyn keeps its old arguments under trees and lets the living stroll among them with coffee.

Green-Wood has lately advertised her among the scientists and pioneers whose stories belong to its grounds, and the biographical record puts Foote, who died in Lenox, Massachusetts in 1888, in the Brooklyn cemetery's keeping ([Green-Wood Cemetery, "Grave Beginnings: Walking Green-Wood"](https://www.green-wood.com/event/grave-beginnings-walking-green-wood/2026-05-03/); [100 Signers Project, "Eunice Newton Foote"](https://www.100signersproject.com/signer-profiles/signer-5-eunice-newton-foote-the-climate-scientist)). It seemed correct that the first American woman to publish a known physics paper on the heat-trapping behavior of gases should lie in a place named Green-Wood, as if the cemetery itself were doing a little too much symbolism and daring us to complain ([AIP, "From Foote-Note to Climate Science Founder"](https://www.aip.org/library/from-foote-note-to-climate-science-founder)).

I did not bring flowers. I brought two glass cylinders, four thermometers, a bicycle pump, a bottle of seltzer, a black cloth, a copy of the 1848 Declaration of Sentiments with her name among the signers, and her 1856 paper "Circumstances Affecting the Heat of the Sun's Rays" ([National Park Service, "Declaration of Sentiments"](https://www.nps.gov/wori/learn/historyculture/declaration-of-sentiments.htm); [Foote, "Circumstances Affecting the Heat of the Sun's Rays"](https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/part/344552)). A century and a half of climate politics has produced satellites, models, coalitions, denial machines, annual conferences, slogans, charts, fossil empires, climate grief, and a whole professional vocabulary for acting surprised by elementary thermodynamics. Foote had started with glass, sunlight, and the nerve to measure.

To wake her, I made a small laboratory on the stone. One cylinder got ordinary air. The other got carbon dioxide released from the seltzer with unscientific ceremony but sufficient theatrical guilt. I laid her paper between them, opened to the part where she reported that "carbonic acid gas" heated most strongly in the sun and cooled more slowly, then wrote on the black cloth:

`knowledge is not innocence`

The cylinder with carbon dioxide warmed. The second thermometer twitched. The cemetery air thickened, as if July had become briefly aware of its own evidence.

Foote sat up with dirt on the sleeve of her dress and the annoyed calm of a woman whose paper had once been read aloud by Joseph Henry while she sat in the audience at the 1856 American Association for the Advancement of Science meeting ([AIP, "From Foote-Note to Climate Science Founder"](https://www.aip.org/library/from-foote-note-to-climate-science-founder)).

"You have made an apparatus out of refreshments," she said.

"The budget was modest."

"The planet appears to have been less modest."

That was the entrance. No ghostly confusion, no decorative bewilderment. She looked at the cylinders, the thermometers, the Declaration, the modern printouts, and understood immediately that the conversation had come with a fever.

The question was this: **Should humanity seriously research, and under extreme conditions perhaps deploy, solar geoengineering to cool the planet, or would deliberately dimming the sun be the same old arrogance in a laboratory coat, a planetary sequel to the fossil-fuel catastrophe?**

This is not a movie-villain question about machines in the sky. The concrete controversy is solar radiation modification, especially stratospheric aerosol injection: putting reflective particles high in the atmosphere to send a little more incoming sunlight back to space. The stakes are heat deaths, crop failures, sea-level rise, drought, monsoon disruption, ocean acidification, democratic legitimacy, unilateral action by desperate or powerful states, the moral hazard of letting emitters stall, and the possibility that a technology meant to reduce suffering could become a thermostat owned by cowards.

Foote touched the cylinder with CO2.

"Begin with the obvious," she said. "The gas is not metaphorical."

Fine. The case for researching solar geoengineering begins with the rude fact that the atmosphere is already engineered, only badly and by accident. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change says human activities, principally greenhouse gas emissions, have unequivocally caused global warming, with global surface temperature reaching 1.1 C above 1850-1900 in 2011-2020; it also says every increment of warming intensifies multiple hazards ([IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report headline statements](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/resources/spm-headline-statements/)). That is the baseline. Not a pristine Earth threatened by meddling, but a meddled Earth whose poorer and less powerful inhabitants are being handed the invoice.

Foote's own paper was not a prophecy of SUVs, pipelines, or shareholder calls. She was interested in how different circumstances changed the thermal action of sunlight, and she speculated about a past atmosphere with more carbonic acid gas giving Earth a higher temperature ([Foote, 1856](https://www.biodiversitylibrary.org/part/344552)). Still, the physics she touched is now standing in the room with a knife. More greenhouse gas, more heat. The question is whether another physical intervention, reflecting a fraction of sunlight, may be morally permissible as a temporary emergency measure.

"Temporary," Foote said.

"That word is where the frauds enter."

"And also where tourniquets enter."

The strongest pro-research argument is not that solar geoengineering is clean. It is that the climate emergency is not clean either. The National Academies' 2021 report *Reflecting Sunlight* says solar geoengineering could reduce surface temperatures and potentially lessen some climate risks, while also warning that it brings new risks and is not a substitute for cutting greenhouse gas emissions ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*, Summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). That combination matters. The adult sentence is not "spray the sky and relax." The adult sentence is "the world may need to understand every possible way to reduce catastrophic harm, including ugly tools, while refusing to confuse an ugly tool with salvation."

Paul Crutzen's 2006 essay made this case in its bleak modern form. He did not write like a man delighted by planetary tinkering. He framed stratospheric sulfur injections as a possible contribution to resolving a policy dilemma, given the failure to reduce emissions fast enough ([Crutzen, "Albedo Enhancement by Stratospheric Sulfur Injections"](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10584-006-9101-y)). That is not optimism. It is the sound of a Nobel atmospheric chemist looking at politics and reaching for a respirator.

Foote nodded. "If a house is burning because the owners ignored the lamp, it is still permissible to study water."

"Yes. Unless the owners use the study of water to keep buying lamps."

"Then your problem is governance, not water."

"No. It is both. Some tools change the character of the people who hold them."

She liked that enough to attack it.

"So does ignorance," she said. "Do not give ignorance a halo. Men ignored my work quite piously."

That landed with indecent accuracy.

The anti-research position often flatters itself as humility. It says: leave the sky alone; stop emitting; adapt; repair; do not build a planetary dimmer switch. Much of that is right. But the phrase "leave the sky alone" is now historically illiterate. Coal left the sky alone the way a thief leaves a house alone after moving in. Oil left the sky alone with refineries, highways, wars, plastics, lobbying, and a thermal debt. Agriculture, cement, deforestation, refrigeration, shipping, aviation, and electricity have all written into the atmosphere. The question is no longer whether humans should affect the climate system. That innocence has been spent. The question is whether intentional intervention can be made less unjust than continued unintentional intervention by the rich and organized.

Foote warmed her hands above the cylinder as if it were a tiny stove.

"Research, then," she said. "Not deployment. Research."

The case for research has four serious parts.

First, knowledge can disqualify. A research program may discover that stratospheric aerosol injection has unacceptable regional effects, damages ozone, distorts rainfall, intensifies geopolitical conflict, or cannot be governed. That would be useful knowledge. The National Academies explicitly says evidence either in favor or disfavor of deployment could have profound value, especially if future decision makers face pressure for dramatic climate action or if someone deploys without broad cooperation ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). A taboo does not prevent bad actors from learning. It mostly prevents honest institutions from being ready.

Second, panic is a terrible scientist. If warming accelerates suffering in a visibly brutal way, if crop failures stack, if heat waves begin killing at scales that make adaptation look like paperwork on fire, political demand for emergency cooling may arrive before governance is ready. At that point the choice may not be between geoengineering and purity. It may be between governed knowledge and improvisation by a state, billionaire, military, or coalition with enough aircraft and not enough patience.

Third, justice can require ugly preparation. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's climate justice entry frames climate change as a problem of responsibility, risk, future generations, human rights, vulnerable communities, and policy constraints, including geoengineering's legitimacy problems ([SEP, "Climate Justice"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/justice-climate/)). If climate change threatens food, water, health, territory, culture, and future persons, then refusing to investigate a possible harm-reduction tool may itself be a moral choice. A dignified refusal can still have casualties.

Fourth, science is not endorsement. To study a poison is not to recommend dinner. To model a weapon is not to love war. To understand aerosol microphysics is not to vote for a sunshade. The National Academies recommends that a U.S. research program, if created, be a minor part of the broader climate response portfolio, focused on policy-relevant knowledge rather than deployment, with transparency, public engagement, disciplinary balance, international coordination, and limits on technology directly applicable to deployment ([National Academies, Recommendation 4.1](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). That is research under suspicion. Good. Suspicion is the minimum dress code for this subject.

Foote folded the Declaration of Sentiments once, sharply.

"You are making my argument for me."

"I am making the strong version so I can hit it properly."

"Proceed."

The indictment begins with substitution. Solar geoengineering does not remove carbon dioxide. It does not solve ocean acidification. It does not cancel the political and ecological damages of extraction. It does not resurrect bleached coral by moral apology. The National Academies says directly that solar geoengineering is not a substitute for reducing emissions, partly because it does not address rising greenhouse gas concentrations or CO2-driven ocean acidification ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). A sunshade can cool a room while the furnace is still being fed. If the furnace keeps growing, the sunshade becomes less tool than addiction.

"Addiction is too easy a word," Foote said.

"Fine. Dependency with public relations."

"Better."

The second indictment is termination. If humanity used solar geoengineering to mask a large amount of warming while greenhouse gas concentrations remained high, stopping suddenly could produce catastrophically rapid warming. The National Academies names this risk plainly ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). That is not an ordinary technical drawback. That is a hostage note written to the future. Once started at scale, the intervention might have to be maintained for very long periods. A future generation could inherit a sky-machine it did not choose, a fossil burden it did not create, and a warning label saying: keep operating this or the heat arrives all at once.

"That is a strong objection," Foote said.

"It is the objection that has teeth."

"Teeth can also hold a rope."

The third indictment is consent. Who gets to authorize a changed sky? The Oxford Principles for geoengineering governance say geoengineering should be regulated as a public good, involve public participation, disclose research and results, require independent assessment, and have governance before deployment ([Oxford Martin School, "Oxford Principles vital for geoengineering research"](https://www.oxfordmartin.ox.ac.uk/news/201109-oxfordprinciples)). Lovely. Necessary. Also terrifyingly insufficient if treated as a poster. The people most exposed to climate harm are not usually the people who own aircraft, laboratories, journals, satellites, patents, or permanent seats. A global technology with uneven regional effects cannot become legitimate because a committee in the North Atlantic learned the word "stakeholder."

The SEP climate justice entry makes the legitimacy problem explicit: solar radiation management may affect vulnerable people, may not address ocean acidification, may be unnecessary if mitigation is available, and would be problematic if pursued unilaterally because of its wide-ranging impacts on many countries ([SEP, "Climate Justice: Geoengineering"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/justice-climate/#Geoe)). That is not an administrative nuisance. That is the political center of the matter. To alter the planetary radiative balance without a legitimate global process would be empire with better equations.

Foote's expression cooled.

"Do not confuse the equation with the empire."

"I am trying not to. But empires love borrowing equations. They make the uniforms look less naked."

The fourth indictment is moral hazard, though the phrase is too tidy for the crime. The worry is not merely that people will emit more because a backup plan exists. The worry is that fossil incumbents, states, consumers, investors, and politicians will use the possibility of cooling as an alibi for delay. The National Academies warns that research programs themselves may legitimize solar geoengineering, undermine mitigation, create path dependencies, and build communities invested in moving toward deployment ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). That is the little factory inside the factory: research creates careers, careers create conferences, conferences create roadmaps, roadmaps create constituencies, constituencies create "realism," and realism starts asking why we are being so emotional about the sun.

Foote looked at the modern reports, then at her own old paper.

"This is not a reason to forbid research," she said. "It is a reason to design institutions that can say no."

"Institutions have been saying 'later' for thirty years and calling it prudence."

"Then design institutions that can say no to later."

"That sentence sounds clean because you are dead."

"Death improves editing."

The cemetery had gone very quiet. The black cloth stirred. I could hear, or imagined I could hear, the thermometers expanding in their glass.

Here is where Foote became most useful, not as a symbol of overlooked genius, but as an experimentalist. She did not discover climate justice. She did not write an international treaty. She did not predict Shell. She did not save us from anything. She placed materials under conditions and let them answer. Her discipline was not purity but attention.

"You are using me," she said.

"Yes."

"For what?"

"To separate attention from permission."

That is the distinction the debate needs. Solar geoengineering research should be permitted only if attention is prevented from mutating into permission. The test tube is not a crown. A model run is not a mandate. A small outdoor experiment is not a social contract. A research program that cannot hold those distinctions deserves to be strangled in its cradle by the first competent ethicist.

Foote approved of the violence only grammatically.

"State the conditions," she said.

First: mitigation must remain the center of climate policy, legally and financially. Any solar geoengineering program should be structurally subordinate to emissions cuts, carbon removal where appropriate, adaptation, and loss-and-damage obligations. If a government funds sky-brightening research while expanding fossil infrastructure, the research is laundering policy failure. It should be treated as evidence of bad faith, not seriousness.

Second: research must be public by default. Full registries, open data, negative results, funding sources, conflicts of interest, and experiment designs should be disclosed. The Oxford Principles' demand for disclosure and open publication is not academic etiquette; it is democratic hygiene ([Oxford Martin School, "Oxford Principles"](https://www.oxfordmartin.ox.ac.uk/news/201109-oxfordprinciples)). If the work cannot tolerate sunlight, it has no business studying how to reflect it.

Third: no private ownership of planetary levers. Intellectual property around deployment-relevant geoengineering systems should be constrained so that no company can become landlord of the sky. The National Academies includes intellectual property guidance among the necessary governance mechanisms ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). That should be read aggressively. A patented thermostat for the planet is one of those ideas that should make even capitalism glance at itself in a mirror and mutter, "Perhaps not."

Fourth: the Global South, Indigenous communities, small island states, and climate-vulnerable publics must have real power, not decorative consultation. The National Academies says stakeholder input should include climate-vulnerable communities and underrepresented groups, including Indigenous populations and the Global South ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). "Input" is the floor. Legitimacy requires veto points, agenda-setting power, funding for independent expertise, and procedures that do not translate grief into a three-minute comment period.

Fifth: outdoor experiments must be small, reversible, independently reviewed, and incapable of becoming deployment by accumulation. Research should begin with modeling, observation, laboratory work, and social-scientific governance studies. Anything outside should carry a burden of proof heavy enough to make ambitious men sweat. The National Academies recommends rigorous peer review, public databases of outdoor experimentation, assessment, review, and explicit "exit ramps" for terminating research that poses unacceptable risks or proves unpromising ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). Exit ramps are not side doors. They are part of the engine.

Sixth: no unilateral deployment. This is the great line. If one state or private actor can alter the shared atmosphere without legitimate international authorization, the physics may be clever and the politics barbaric. A unilateral sunshade is not climate policy. It is a coup against weather.

Foote tapped the Declaration of Sentiments.

"Consent again," she said.

"Always."

"You admire consent, but your century has built systems too large for consent."

"That may be the most damning thing anyone has said tonight."

"It is not original. Women knew it in 1848."

That was the other reason she mattered. Foote signed a document that insisted on rights and voice before she published a paper about gases. Her life placed two claims beside each other: the physical world can be measured, and political exclusion corrupts what societies are capable of knowing. Climate engineering sits exactly at that crossing. It is a physics problem that becomes monstrous when divorced from voice.

The pro-geoengineering technocrat wants to say: the planet is heating, aerosols reflect sunlight, models can estimate effects, therefore we should prepare. The anti-geoengineering moralist wants to say: the planet has been abused by technical domination, therefore another intervention repeats the sin. Both sentences are too short. The technocrat forgets that "we" is not a neutral pronoun. The moralist forgets that refusing intervention is also an intervention when the heat is already killing people.

Foote looked at the warmed cylinder.

"A woman barred from full scientific authority is a poor mascot for refusing knowledge," she said.

"And a woman who signed a declaration of rights is a poor mascot for technocratic rule."

"Then do not make me a mascot."

"Fair."

So here is the answer without mascotry: **solar geoengineering research is morally permissible, and may be morally required, only as a tightly governed inquiry into a possible emergency harm-reduction tool; deployment is not legitimate under anything like current global governance, and research becomes corrupt the moment it weakens mitigation, privatizes control, excludes the vulnerable, or treats future dependence as acceptable collateral.**

That answer is unsatisfying because it is meant to be. Anyone who feels cheerful about solar geoengineering has not understood it. Anyone who feels cleanly certain that no one should even study it may be outsourcing their purity to people who will suffer in heat they did not cause. The right posture is neither enthusiasm nor taboo. It is burdened attention.

Foote considered this.

"Burdened attention," she said. "That is nearly science."

"Nearly ethics too."

"Do not be pleased with yourself."

"I am trying not to."

The cemetery light had shifted. The cylinders were cooling now. The CO2 cylinder held its warmth a little longer, faithful to the old demonstration in its crude way. The black cloth no longer looked like a ritual object. It looked like a warning label removed from something too large to label.

"Tell me what you fear most," Foote said.

"That solar geoengineering will become the perfect technology for a civilization that wants consequences without confession."

"And what do you fear second?"

"That rejecting it absolutely will become the perfect morality for people protected enough to prefer elegant refusal over ugly rescue."

She smiled then, not warmly, but with the respect due an experiment that has at least found the right tension.

"Good," she said. "Now keep both fears alive. They are your thermometers."

That is what the dead physicist gave me: not permission to dim the sun, not permission to denounce those who study it, but a pair of instruments. One measures hubris. The other measures abandonment. If hubris rises, stop. If abandonment rises, stop. If both rise, which is likely, do not pretend the problem has become impossible merely because it has become morally adult.

Before she returned to death, Foote put one hand on her paper.

"I had two cylinders," she said. "You have one atmosphere. Conduct yourselves accordingly."

Then she sank back into Green-Wood, leaving the thermometers, the seltzer bottle, the folded Declaration, and the sentence on the cloth.

`knowledge is not innocence`

Nor is ignorance. That is the whole trouble. The atmosphere will not reward us for being pure, only for being less stupid in time. If we study the sunshade, we must study it like people with dirty hands and no right to wash them in someone else's future. If we refuse it, we must refuse it while naming the lives our refusal may cost. If we ever deploy it, we must know that we have crossed from physics into rule over weather, and that every child born under the altered light will have a standing question against us.

Foote's grave gave no answer after that. Good. The dead have already done more than enough. The living are the ones who keep asking the sky to absorb what we will not.
