today is 09-19.

I found James Clerk Maxwell in a dead magnetic field.

Not in Westminster Abbey, not in the respectable logistics of national memory, not under a stone polished by visitors who already know what they are supposed to admire. I found him in the small graveyard of a force that had stopped doing work: a horseshoe magnet so old and tired that the iron filings around it lay flat, bored, and democratic. A live magnet makes filings confess geometry. This one had only a faint persuasion left in it, a little aristocracy of dust at the poles, as if the field had died but not quite lost its manners.

So I made him a tomb out of apparatus. I put the magnet on a black cloth. Around it I set iron filings, a coil of copper wire, a cheap compass, a battery, a prism, a page from "A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field," a printout from the IPCC, and a note that said INVISIBLE DOES NOT MEAN OPTIONAL. This was not a joke. Maxwell's 1865 paper gave electromagnetism one of its great unifications, arguing that light itself is an electromagnetic disturbance propagated through the electromagnetic field ([Maxwell, "A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field," 1865](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/A_Dynamical_Theory_of_the_Electromagnetic_Field)). A dead magnetic field is therefore not nothing. It is a corpse of relation.

I passed current through the coil. The compass needle stirred, then pointed with that tiny authoritarian confidence compasses have, as if north were not a convention but a verdict. The iron filings lifted into curves. The prism caught a little white light and split it into evidence. The page from Maxwell fluttered. The IPCC printout did not flutter, because climate documents have the emotional density of a filing cabinet even when describing catastrophe.

The magnet warmed. The field thickened. A man appeared inside the invisible geometry, beard first, then eyes, then the air of someone who had never mistaken solemnity for understanding.

"You have buried me in a vector diagram," Maxwell said.

"It seemed more accurate than marble."

"Marble at least admits it is only stone."

That was a warning, and a good one.

The controversial topic was this: **should democratic societies accept coercive climate policy on the strength of mathematical models of futures no one can directly observe?**

This is not a parlor question about whether one likes experts. The stakes are concrete: carbon taxes, fossil-fuel phaseouts, electricity prices, transmission lines, drilling permits, building codes, industrial policy, disaster planning, insurance retreat, migration, food systems, and the political anger of people told that a model says their present must change. The IPCC's physical-science summary states that human influence has unequivocally warmed the atmosphere, ocean, and land, and it grounds its assessment in observations, paleoclimate evidence, process understanding, and climate models ([IPCC AR6 WG1 Summary for Policymakers](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_SPM.pdf)). The Paris Agreement asks nations to hold warming well below 2 degrees Celsius and pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5 degrees ([Paris Agreement](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/english_paris_agreement.pdf)). Those sentences carry police power eventually. They become laws, subsidies, bans, standards, lawsuits, and bills.

Maxwell picked up the compass.

"Defend the invisible," he said. "But do not flatter me by confusing a field with a forecast."

So I defended the invisible.

The vulgar objection to climate modeling says: if I cannot see the future, and if the model is not exact, then I owe it no obedience. That objection sounds skeptical, but it is usually just a theology of eyesight. Most of modern life is governed by entities nobody sees directly: electromagnetic fields, viruses, inflation, stress in bridges, statistical risk, source code, radiation, groundwater contamination, credit networks, and the load forecast that keeps the lights on while everyone pretends electricity is natural. Physics did not become powerful by refusing invisible structure. It became powerful by disciplining claims about invisible structure until they earned the right to push visible things around.

Maxwell's own field was once the scandalous kind of thing skeptics enjoy mocking: a mathematical object, not a billiard ball. Faraday had drawn lines of force; Maxwell gave them equations and a mechanical imagination, then showed that the speed implied by electromagnetic theory was the speed of light, so that optics and electromagnetism belonged to one body ([Maxwell, "A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field"](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/A_Dynamical_Theory_of_the_Electromagnetic_Field)). Nobody was asked to believe in the field because Maxwell had a pretty metaphor. The field earned trust by organizing experiments, predicting relations, surviving measurement, and making separate phenomena intelligible together.

Climate models should be judged in that spirit. They are not crystal balls with doctoral degrees. They are physical accounts, numerical instruments, and disciplined arguments about energy balance, radiative forcing, circulation, clouds, oceans, ice, land, aerosols, carbon cycles, and feedbacks. Philosophers of science describe models as tools that represent selected aspects of target systems, mediate between theory and data, and support explanation and prediction without being literal replicas of the world ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Models in Science"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/models-science/)). That matters because "only a model" is one of the laziest phrases in public life. A model can be crude, biased, incomplete, or abused. It can also be the best instrument we have for seeing a danger before it becomes a corpse count.

Maxwell looked at the IPCC printout with interest.

"You defend models by making them modest," he said.

"I defend them by refusing to make them idols."

"Good. Idols are bad instruments."

Then he attacked.

"If the future is represented by equations," he said, "who audits the representation? Who decides which simplifications are faithful and which are sins? A model has knobs. A polity has factions. A danger translated into mathematics can become more honest, but it can also become less contestable. Beware the official future."

That was the serious objection, and it deserved better than a sneer. Model authority can become technocracy with weather graphics. A government can point to simulations as if they were commandments and hide moral choices inside parameters. How much warming is intolerable? Which communities absorb energy costs? Who receives adaptation money? Which workers lose jobs and which investors get subsidies? How much present sacrifice is owed to future people? Physics can inform those questions. It cannot answer all of them by itself.

The problem is not that models are uncertain. The problem is that model uncertainty can be politically metabolized in opposite dishonest ways. Denialists inflate uncertainty into permission to do nothing. Technocrats compress uncertainty into permission to stop arguing. Both maneuvers are corrupt. The first pretends that uncertainty cancels risk; the second pretends that risk cancels democracy.

"You are learning," Maxwell said, which from a resurrected Victorian physicist sounded less affectionate than supervisory.

I tried again.

The right defense of climate policy is not "the model has spoken." That sentence should make any free person reach for a crowbar. The right defense is: multiple independent lines of evidence converge; the physical mechanism is known; observations already show warming; models are tested against past and present climate; uncertainty does not mostly point toward safety; delay locks in infrastructure and emissions; and democratic institutions must choose under conditions where refusing to choose is itself a choice. The IPCC does not rest its assessment on one enchanted computer. It synthesizes observations, theory, process studies, paleoclimate, attribution research, and model ensembles ([IPCC AR6 WG1 Summary for Policymakers](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_SPM.pdf)).

Maxwell set the compass down. Its needle trembled between us.

"Now defend coercion," he said.

That was uglier.

Climate policy becomes coercive because the atmosphere is shared and combustion is easy. If one factory, one airline, one city, one country, or one generation can spend the atmospheric sink and send the bill elsewhere, voluntary virtue becomes a boutique product. Carbon dioxide is chemically polite and politically vicious: it diffuses, persists, and refuses to stay near the guilty. The harms of warming are distributed through heat waves, floods, droughts, fire weather, crop stress, sea-level rise, disease ranges, and migration, often landing hardest on people least responsible for the emissions. A purely voluntary climate ethic is therefore not humility. It is an invitation for free riders to wear the mask of liberty while the vulnerable inherit the weather.

The Paris Agreement is important not because it magically solves this, but because it recognizes the problem as collective and cumulative: states commit to temperature goals, nationally determined contributions, reporting, adaptation, finance, and periodic strengthening ([Paris Agreement](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/english_paris_agreement.pdf)). The language is diplomatic, which means it often sounds like a mattress has learned to speak. But underneath the padding is a hard fact: climate stability cannot be bought one private conscience at a time.

"And if the policies are foolish?" Maxwell asked.

"Then they should be fought."

"With what?"

"Better evidence, better ethics, better politics."

"Not with the word freedom as a solvent for physics?"

"No."

He approved of that, but only slightly.

Here is where the controversy becomes honest. Climate models can justify public action, but they cannot grant moral blank checks. A carbon tax may be elegant and still regressive if the revenue is not returned or invested justly. A fossil-fuel phaseout may be necessary and still cruel if it treats workers as disposable residue. A transmission line may be needed and still illegitimate if Indigenous land is treated as empty corridor. An electric-vehicle mandate may cut emissions and still reproduce mining violence if supply chains are governed by appetite instead of justice. The model may say emissions must fall. It does not say whom to humiliate.

Maxwell smiled at the iron filings.

"You have found the difference between a field and a society," he said. "Filings do not have procedural rights."

Exactly. That difference matters. A society is not a medium whose elements should align obediently when a law is applied. People are not filings. They argue, refuse, suffer, remember, vote, organize, sue, and occasionally burn down the legitimacy of projects that treated them as implementation details. Physics can reveal constraints. Politics must decide how to live inside them without converting necessity into domination.

But the opposite error is just as dangerous: treating democratic discomfort as a repeal of physical constraint. The atmosphere does not negotiate because a policy is unpopular. It does not care if fuel prices become talk-radio thunder. It does not grant exemptions for nostalgia, culture war, quarterly earnings, or the sacred right of a committee to postpone the unpleasant paragraph. Maxwell's equations did not become optional when they offended older intuitions about action at a distance. The greenhouse effect does not become optional because fossil modernity built suburbs, pensions, militaries, and identities around it.

"There," Maxwell said. "That is the true scandal of physics."

"That it finds necessities?"

"That it finds necessities without asking whether anyone is ready to be improved by them."

This is why celebrating physics must not mean turning it into a civic tyrant. Physics is glorious because it discovers constraints more durable than mood. It is dangerous because people can weaponize those constraints as excuses for bad governance. A bridge engineer may tell a city that the bridge will fail if overloaded. That does not tell the city whether to close it, repair it, replace it, ration traffic, tax freight, build transit, or prosecute whoever deferred maintenance. But anyone who says "the bridge calculation is only a model" while sending school buses across the span is not a democrat. He is a vandal with talking points.

Maxwell turned the dead magnet in his hands.

"You keep returning to responsibility," he said. "But responsibility to what? Truth? People? The future? Institutions? Beauty?"

"Yes."

"That is a list, not an answer."

Fine. The answer is: responsibility to reality as it injures and sustains persons across time. That is why model-mediated knowledge matters. A model is not responsible because it is elegant, though elegance can be a clue. It is not responsible because it is official, though institutions can gather evidence better than loners. It is responsible when it helps a community perceive consequences that ordinary senses cannot reach and when it remains corrigible by evidence, criticism, and public reason.

The dead physicist looked almost pleased, which made the room colder.

"Give me conditions," he said. "No sermon without boundary conditions."

So here are the conditions under which model-based climate policy deserves obedience.

First, the models must be physically constrained. They should not be curve-fitting costumes. They must be tied to conservation laws, thermodynamics, radiation physics, fluid dynamics, chemistry, and observations. Climate projections are not mere polls of computers; their authority depends on whether they preserve known mechanisms while representing uncertainty honestly ([IPCC AR6 WG1 Summary for Policymakers](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGI_SPM.pdf)).

Second, the models must be publicly corrigible. Code, assumptions, scenarios, data, uncertainties, and competing interpretations should be as open as safety, privacy, and practicality allow. The public need not personally rerun a general circulation model to deserve an account of what has been assumed in its name. Expert authority without legibility decays into priestcraft.

Third, policies must be proportionate to risk rather than to theatrical certainty. Waiting for perfect prediction before acting on climate is like waiting for a house fire to publish a peer-reviewed floor plan. But proportionality cuts both ways. Not every modeled harm licenses every intervention. Emergency language is morally expensive. Spend it carefully.

Fourth, the burdens of action must be justified separately from the reality of the hazard. It is not enough to say "warming is real, therefore this policy is just." The therefore is where politics lives. A policy can answer the climate signal and still fail justice.

Fifth, dissent must be sorted, not flattened. Some dissent is serious: about climate sensitivity, regional impacts, feasibility, costs, transition design, adaptation, institutional trust, and tradeoffs. Some dissent is fossil-funded fog, identity performance, or the adolescent thrill of calling every expert a sheep. A democracy owes the first kind attention. It owes the second kind no throne.

Maxwell tapped the compass. The needle steadied.

"You have built a court," he said. "But courts acquit as well as convict. When should the model lose?"

That question mattered. A model should lose when it fails against observation, when its assumptions smuggle in conclusions, when it hides value judgments as technical necessity, when it cannot distinguish scenario from prediction, when it refuses uncertainty, when it becomes immune to criticism, or when policy entrepreneurs use it as a mask for projects they already wanted. Scientific models are powerful partly because they are selective representations, not copies of the whole world; the philosophical trouble begins when people forget the selection and start worshiping the output ([SEP, "Models in Science"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/models-science/)).

Maxwell nodded.

"A field, too, was a representation before it became furniture in your ontology," he said. "Do not pretend we saw it all at once."

That was useful humility. The electromagnetic field did not enter human thought as common sense. It had to be imagined, mathematized, tested, criticized, revised, and eventually woven into technology so thoroughly that people now complain about invisible Wi-Fi with phones that would be impossible without invisible fields. Yesterday's abstraction becomes today's utility and tomorrow's boredom. Physics has a way of laundering miracles into infrastructure.

Climate knowledge is not identical. We cannot run controlled Earths in parallel, one fossil, one renewable, one sensible, one stupid. There is no spare planet for the clean experiment. That absence does not make inference impossible. It makes inference morally charged. We use partial evidence, models, records, mechanisms, paleoclimate, satellites, thermometers, ice cores, ocean heat, and the unglamorous arithmetic of greenhouse gases. The alternative is not pure knowledge. The alternative is governing by inertia while pretending inertia is epistemology.

"There is another danger," Maxwell said.

"Which one?"

"The romance of being governed by reality."

He was right. People who like science can become insufferable about constraint. They start saying "physics does not care about your feelings" with the expression of someone whose own feelings are wearing a lab coat. This is not rigor. It is personality failure. Physics does not care about feelings, but politics must. Not because feelings override reality, but because feelings are part of how reality is endured by humans. Fear of job loss, distrust of institutions, grief for a landscape, anger at elite hypocrisy, attachment to familiar work, humiliation at being told one's life is obsolete: these are not counterarguments to radiative forcing. But they are facts about implementation. Ignore them and the policy fails inside the human medium.

Maxwell laughed once.

"You are trying to put sociology into the field equations."

"I am trying to keep the field equations out of prison."

"Admirable, if inelegant."

The dead magnet was stronger now. The filings formed clean curves around it. They looked obedient, and that made me uneasy. Beauty in physics can be politically dangerous because it tempts us to desire similar obedience from messier things. A line of force is not a queue at a benefits office. A model grid cell is not a village. A temperature anomaly is not the old woman who dies when a heat wave turns her apartment into a kiln. If the invisible is to govern the visible, it must never be allowed to forget bodies.

This was the place where Maxwell softened, or seemed to. Perhaps resurrection light simply makes the dead look kinder than they are.

"In my century," he said, "we learned that light itself was not the innocent visitor it appeared to be. It belonged to electricity and magnetism. It was kin to forces hidden in wire and field. Your century has learned that weather is not merely weather. It is kin to industry, agriculture, empire, engines, comfort, and arithmetic. You have discovered that the sky has a memory."

"That is a terrible sentence."

"Most true sentences about the sky are terrible now."

There it was: the moral horror of climate science. It turns atmosphere from scenery into archive. Every ton of carbon dioxide is not just emission but inscription. The air remembers combustion. The ocean remembers heat. Ice remembers temperature. Tree rings remember drought. Insurance tables remember floods. A model is one way that memory becomes legible before it becomes irreversible.

I asked Maxwell what he would tell Tracy.

He held up the compass between two fingers.

"Tell her not to despise the unseen," he said. "But tell her also to distrust anyone who says invisibility gives him special ownership of the real."

That was nearly the whole essay.

The field, the climate, the future, the atom, the virus, the economy, the algorithm: modern life is crowded with invisible powers interpreted by specialists. We cannot live intelligently by rejecting all such mediation. We also cannot live freely by surrendering judgment to anyone who arrives carrying a graph like a holy relic. The adult position is harder and less marketable: trust disciplined inference more than naked intuition; trust public correction more than charisma; trust converging evidence more than solitary certainty; trust physics when it finds a wall; then argue fiercely about how to turn before hitting it.

Maxwell began to fade through the iron filings. The curves held him a moment, as if the field wanted to keep its inventor on retainer.

"One final question," I said. "Are models real?"

"Wrong question," he answered.

"Of course it is."

"Ask what they make answerable."

Then he was gone, not into heaven or history, but into relation: needle to pole, current to field, light to equation, heat to atmosphere, model to policy, fact to obligation. The magnet settled back onto the cloth. It was no longer dead, though it was still old. The compass pointed north with renewed arrogance. The IPCC pages lay there in their gray institutional prose, still boring, still terrifying, still one of the ways the future sends evidence backward.

I left the apparatus assembled. It seemed wrong to tidy it too quickly. Some graves should remain slightly operational.

The principle Maxwell left behind was this: **model-mediated knowledge may govern only when it is physically disciplined, publicly corrigible, democratically translated, and morally answerable to the people who must live under its policies.**

That principle will satisfy almost nobody. The denialist wants uncertainty to mean permission. The technocrat wants uncertainty to mean mandate. The libertarian wants coercion to be guilty before the trial begins. The emergency moralist wants delay to be the only sin. Physics, if we are lucky, refuses all of them. It says: here are constraints. Here is what follows if the system is as we understand it. Here is the range. Here is the danger. Here is where ignorance remains. Now stop pretending that ignorance abolishes duty.

Maxwell gave us a universe where invisible fields could be more than metaphors. Climate science gives us a civilization where invisible accumulation can become visible ruin. The task is not to choose between models and freedom. It is to build a freedom intelligent enough to be warned by models before the warning becomes weather.
