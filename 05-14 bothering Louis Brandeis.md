today is May 14, 2026.

I found Louis Brandeis where the republic stores its discarded consciences, in the basement level of the National Archives annex, past the temperature-controlled rooms and the polite museum language, in a service corridor that smelled of dust, wool, and old paste. I had come looking for his body by following a paper trail rather than a map. First his warning that the makers of the Constitution conferred, "as against the Government, the right to be let alone, the most comprehensive of rights and the right most valued by civilized men" ([*Olmstead v. United States*, Brandeis dissent, 1928](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/277/438/)). Then the younger Brandeis of 1890, writing with Samuel Warren that modern life and the new devices of publicity had made privacy newly fragile, and that the law would have to recognize injuries that did not look like trespass at all ([Warren and Brandeis, "The Right to Privacy," *Harvard Law Review*](https://groups.csail.mit.edu/mac/classes/6.805/articles/privacy/Privacy_brand_warr2.html)). If you read those sentences carefully enough, a grave opens under the floorboards of American convenience.

So I made one.

I laid out a circle of carbon paper, telegraph wire, copper scraped from a dead router, and a cracked iPhone whose black screen reflected my face badly enough to count as an omen. I put in the center a printout of the Fourth Amendment and a fresh warrant application template, because if one is going to wake a justice one should at least offer procedural respect. Then I read aloud Brandeis's line that government is the potent, omnipresent teacher, and that if it becomes a lawbreaker it invites every person to become a law unto himself ([*Olmstead*, Brandeis dissent](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/277/438/)). The phone vibrated without power. The wire tightened like a vein. A man in a dark suit sat up from the carbon paper as if he had merely been thinking with unusual concentration.

He adjusted his cuffs, looked at the iPhone, looked at me, and asked what fresh indignity had been done in the name of efficiency.

I told him the question was encryption. Not in the abstract, not as a sterile fight between coders and police lobbyists, but as a live political demand: should democratic governments require providers of end-to-end encrypted messaging and devices to maintain some form of exceptional access for law enforcement, so that with lawful authorization the state can read what would otherwise remain unreadable?

This argument survives because both sides are talking about real victims. Law enforcement agencies have spent years warning that strong default encryption creates a "going dark" problem, impeding investigations into child sexual abuse, organized crime, narcotics trafficking, and terrorism ([FBI, "Statement Before the Senate Judiciary Committee on Lawful Access to Encrypted Data"](https://www.judiciary.senate.gov/imo/media/doc/Wray%20Testimony.pdf)). The National Center for Missing and Exploited Children has reported tens of millions of CyberTipline reports annually, numbers so large they threaten to numb the moral imagination unless one forces oneself to remember what they signify: actual children, actual images, actual crimes ([NCMEC, 2024 Annual Report](https://www.missingkids.org/content/dam/missingkids/pdfs/2024-ncmec-annual-report.pdf)). Officials argue, not absurdly, that a warrant should not become meaningless merely because evidence is hidden behind mathematics rather than a locked desk.

But the case for exceptional access acquires its moral heat by hiding a technical fact inside a legal metaphor. The state likes to talk as though it were asking for a master key held in reserve for decent reasons. Cryptographers keep replying that this is not how the door works. Building a system that can be opened for the good guys creates a vulnerability that others can target, steal, coerce, replicate, or repurpose. The 2015 paper "Keys Under Doormats," written by leading security researchers, put the point plainly: exceptional access would introduce systemic security risks and governance burdens at a scale likely to undermine the security the internet currently depends on ([Abelson et al., "Keys Under Doormats"](https://people.csail.mit.edu/rivest/pubs/ABGHRSV15.pdf)). More recently, CISA and partner agencies have urged the use of end-to-end encryption as a core defensive measure against sophisticated intrusions, which is a strange thing for governments to say if governments also intend to structurally weaken it ([CISA and partners, "Secure by Design Alert: Eliminate Default Passwords and Improve Security"](https://www.cisa.gov/news-events/cybersecurity-advisories/aa23-278a); [CISA, "Mobile Communications Best Practice Guidance"](https://www.cisa.gov/resources-tools/resources/mobile-communications-best-practice-guidance)).

Brandeis heard the framing and became immediately severe. He said that the deepest error in the exceptional-access argument is its cramped conception of what liberty is for. Privacy is not merely a zone in which the innocent hide trivia. It is part of the architecture by which citizens remain more than administrable objects. In his *Olmstead* dissent, he warned that the Constitution must be read against subtler and more far-reaching means of invading privacy than the founders could have physically imagined ([*Olmstead*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/277/438/)). End-to-end encryption, he said, is one of the rare technical developments that has accidentally built something constitutional into infrastructure. It creates, by default, a space the state cannot casually enter. That incapacity is not a bug in democracy. It is one of the few things making democracy worth having.

I told him that this was noble and insufficient. A government that cannot read anything it is legally entitled to seize will not remain content with lectures about constitutional atmosphere. The public will not accept a theory of liberty that sounds, in practice, like an instruction to leave predators better hidden. The Fifth Amendment does not require the state to forget how to execute search warrants just because technology has improved. If the law can compel a landlord to open a storage unit and a bank to produce records, why should Signal, Apple, Meta, or the next encrypted provider be uniquely exempt from designing compliance into their systems?

Brandeis answered with a distinction the debate usually blurs on purpose. The question is not whether the state may search. It is whether the state may require the world to be built in a permanently search-ready form. Those are not the same thing. A warrant authorizes intrusion under constrained conditions. An exceptional-access mandate restructures the conditions of everyone's communication in advance, including the communications of journalists, dissidents, abuse victims, political organizers, attorneys, doctors, and ordinary people with the modest wish not to live inside a glass hive. It converts a legal power into an architectural constant.

He was right, and the concrete stakes are ugly. End-to-end encryption is not just a convenience for privacy maximalists. Human rights groups and digital security experts have repeatedly documented its importance for activists, vulnerable communities, and reporters working under hostile states or violent nonstate actors ([Human Rights Watch, "Encryption and Human Rights"](https://www.hrw.org/news/2018/09/25/encryption-human-rights); [UN Office of the High Commissioner for Human Rights, report on privacy in the digital age](https://www.ohchr.org/en/documents/thematic-reports/ahrc3949-right-privacy-digital-age)). Once an access mechanism exists, democracies will claim they alone can use it judiciously, but the mechanism will not remain normatively local. Other governments will demand parity. Some of them jail dissidents, criminalize queer life, and treat criticism as sedition. One cannot design a backdoor for Ottawa or Berlin that Beijing, Riyadh, Delhi, or the next frightened executive will politely ignore.

Still, I did not raise Brandeis from the floor to let him keep the cleanest theory in the room. I told him there is a recurring self-flattery among privacy absolutists. They describe every law-enforcement complaint as mere authoritarian appetite and every technical concession as civilizational collapse. But democratic states do have legitimate coercive obligations. A murdered child is not an abstraction. A trafficking ring is not a seminar problem. If police have probable cause and a judge signs the warrant, it feels almost perverse to say the state's hands must remain tied because we have decided, as a matter of design ideology, that some evidence should be made categorically unreachable.

Brandeis gave me the look dead jurists reserve for the living when we try to baptize panic as realism. He said the difficulty of enforcing law does not by itself authorize the preemptive reconfiguration of free society. The police would solve more cases if every house had a compulsory microphone and every room a state-accessible camera. They would solve more cases if every citizen wore a transmitter. They would solve more cases if suspicion thresholds were lower, warrants broader, data retention universal, and anonymity abolished. The fact that surveillance helps investigations is not a discovery. It is the banal temptation around which constitutional government is built as a restraint.

Then he pressed harder. The exceptional-access argument, he said, depends on a fantasy of institutional innocence. It assumes the same states that lose malware, overclassify evidence, abuse national-security powers, purchase commercial location data to evade warrant rules, and repeatedly fail to secure their own systems can nevertheless be trusted to administer a universal cryptographic vulnerability without drift, theft, mission creep, or future abuse. That confidence is not seriousness. It is sentimentality wearing a badge.

The historical record is bad enough to support his contempt. The United States government has its own long archive of surveillance overreach, from COINTELPRO to bulk collection fights after September 11 ([FBI Records: The Vault, COINTELPRO](https://vault.fbi.gov/cointel-pro); [Privacy and Civil Liberties Oversight Board, Report on the Telephone Records Program](https://www.pclob.gov/library/215-Report_on_the_Telephone_Records_Program.pdf)). Modern states and contractors also lose highly sensitive cyber capabilities with dismaying regularity, as the Shadow Brokers leaks and other incidents made painfully clear ([Council on Foreign Relations, "The Shadow Brokers Leak"](https://www.cfr.org/blog/shadow-brokers-leak)). To create exceptional access and then promise permanent containment is to demand political trust from institutions that have worked very hard to deserve the opposite.

I asked him what he would say to the families of victims when investigators know relevant messages exist but cannot read them. He did not dodge. He said constitutional orders are defined partly by the crimes they refuse to preempt at any cost. We do not permit torture because it might extract true information quickly. We do not abolish counsel because guilty people misuse lawyers. We do not nullify the privilege against self-incrimination because silence obstructs justice. A free society is not one that catches every criminal. It is one that decides some powers are too corrupting to normalize, even under pressure.

That sounds lofty until one remembers the asymmetry of grief. The parent whose child was exploited online is not asking for a treatise on political morality. They are asking why the law protects an app architecture more fiercely than it protects a body. This is the hardest part of the argument, and it is where glib civil libertarianism becomes obscene. If one defends strong encryption, one owes more than slogans. One owes an account of how else to pursue the harms the state invokes.

Here Brandeis surprised me by being less romantic than many of his living admirers. He said the choice is not between universal exceptional access and pious helplessness. Governments can fund targeted investigative capacity, device exploitation under warrant, metadata analysis subject to real oversight, undercover work, financial tracing, victim services, cross-border police cooperation, and better staffing for digital forensics. Platforms can be forced to improve reporting pipelines, abuse detection in non-content layers, safety tooling, and account integrity without breaking end-to-end encryption itself. The fact that no substitute is perfect does not make structural insecurity wise. It merely means that justice, as usual, is labor-intensive.

That last point matters because the backdoor debate is often a fight between two different fantasies. One fantasy says technology can preserve perfect private freedom at zero social cost. The other says technology can preserve universal lawful access while staying secure for everyone except the targeted guilty. Both are false. The real decision is about which form of imperfection a constitutional democracy should choose. Do we accept some domains of warranted invisibility because the alternative would expose the whole population to a standing vulnerability? Or do we require permanent access mechanisms because some crimes are too terrible to leave any refuge beyond the state's reach?

By then the service lights had gone dim enough to make the corridor feel ecclesiastical. Brandeis stood inside the circle of carbon paper as if it were a witness stand. I realized the argument was not finally about encryption at all. It was about whether liberal societies believe freedom is something to be administered by trustworthy officials or something protected partly by making even trustworthy officials less powerful than they would like to be.

My answer, after bothering the dead all the way to this point, is that governments should not be permitted to mandate exceptional access in end-to-end encrypted systems. Not because law enforcement's interests are trivial, and not because the victims invoked by officials are rhetorical props, but because the demanded remedy is worse than the admitted disease. It would transform the communicative environment of everyone in order to lower the search costs of the state. It would create a vulnerability whose abuse cases are not speculative but guaranteed, uncertain only in timing and scale. And it would establish a principle democracies should dread: that when law encounters a technical limit, the proper response is to redesign civilian life around state legibility.

There are moments when a civilization reveals its anthropology by the tools it insists on having. A polity that cannot tolerate zones of real opacity will eventually call every unmonitored space a threat. Brandeis knew that long before smartphones. He stepped back onto the carbon paper and began to fade, but not before offering one final cruelty to the living. The danger, he said, is not only that governments will read too much. It is that citizens who grow used to total searchability will forget that being partly unreadable to power was ever one of the conditions of dignity.

Then the dead justice was gone, and the iPhone in the circle remained dark. I left it there. It seemed only fair that one device in America should keep a secret the state could not demand open.
