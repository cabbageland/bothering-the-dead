today is May 16, 2026.

I found W. E. B. Du Bois in Great Barrington, where the hills look too composed to confess how much American violence they have watched from a distance. His grave is not there, not exactly. Du Bois was buried in Accra after dying in Ghana on the eve of the March on Washington, with his passport battle and exile folded into the last chapter of a life that had already become an indictment of the republic ([Encyclopaedia Britannica, "W.E.B. Du Bois"](https://www.britannica.com/biography/W-E-B-Du-Bois); [NAACP, "W.E.B. Du Bois"](https://naacp.org/find-resources/history-explained/civil-rights-leaders/web-du-bois)). So I had to assemble him from absences. I brought red Georgia clay, a library card from Fisk, a page from *Black Reconstruction*, and a photocopy of the sentence in which he wrote that the slave "stood a brief moment in the sun; then moved back again toward slavery" ([W. E. B. Du Bois, *Black Reconstruction in America*](https://archive.org/details/blackreconstruc00dubo)). I laid them in a rectangle on the wet grass. The rectangle became a door. He stepped through in a dark suit that looked less resurrected than resumed.

I told him why I had bothered the dead. I wanted to argue about reparations, specifically the most inflammatory version of the question: whether the United States should enact race-specific cash reparations for Black Americans descended from persons enslaved in the United States, rather than dissolving the claim into universal anti-poverty policy, generic "equity," or commemorative apology. The controversy is not abstract. Ta-Nehisi Coates put the matter plainly more than a decade ago: the injury was not only slavery, but an unbroken chain of plunder through Jim Crow, contract buying, redlining, exclusion from federal benefits, predatory lending, and segregated state violence ([Ta-Nehisi Coates, "The Case for Reparations," *The Atlantic*](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/2014/06/the-case-for-reparations/361631/)). The contemporary wealth data remain brutal. The Federal Reserve's 2022 Survey of Consumer Finances found median wealth for Black families far below that of white families, with the gap measured not in sentiment but in orders of material security ([Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, *Changes in U.S. Family Finances from 2019 to 2022*](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/scf23.pdf)). The argument I wanted to force was whether justice requires targeted payment for a targeted historical crime, or whether that remedy would harden racial categories, trigger democratic backlash, and neglect the poor who are not Black.

Du Bois did not thank me for the assignment. He said, almost immediately, that the first corruption of the subject is rhetorical. Americans prefer to debate reparations as though someone had proposed an eccentric grant program, when the actual issue is unpaid theft. In *Black Reconstruction* he had already described slavery as an economic order built on expropriated labor on a planetary scale, with Black workers furnishing the foundation of Atlantic capital accumulation ([Du Bois, *Black Reconstruction*](https://archive.org/details/blackreconstruc00dubo)). To ask whether there should be reparations, he said, is morally similar to asking whether there should be receipts.

I pushed back because moral clarity, however earned, does not settle policy design. Even many people who concede the history doubt the remedy. Some argue that lineage is too difficult to administer fairly. Others say that the United States has become too intermixed, too temporally distant, too demographically changed for race-specific payment to be either politically feasible or normatively coherent. A third camp, often on the left, says the deeper problem is oligarchy, so the proper answer is universal social democracy: housing, healthcare, labor rights, baby bonds, full employment, and stronger unions for everyone. Martin Luther King Jr. himself increasingly turned toward economic rights in his last years and called for sweeping redistribution, even describing the need for a radical restructuring of economic power ([Martin Luther King Jr., "Where Do We Go From Here?"](https://www.crmvet.org/docs/670811_king_where.pdf); [The Martin Luther King, Jr. Research and Education Institute, "A Testament of Hope"](https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/king-papers/documents/where-do-we-go-here)). If universal programs can lift the floor beneath all poor people while disproportionately helping Black Americans because they are disproportionately harmed, why insist on a racially explicit transfer that may never pass and may leave the whole egalitarian project weaker?

He looked at me with the exhausted patience of someone who had heard clever evasions for more than a century. Universal uplift is good, he said, and also insufficient. The problem is that reparations are not merely an anti-poverty measure. They are a remedy keyed to a particular structure of theft. To replace them with universal welfare is to confuse social need with culpable injury. If a man burns down one house on a street and later funds a public fire department for the entire neighborhood, he has done something useful, but he has not yet made restitution.

That distinction is more than moral theater. In recent scholarship, William Darity Jr. and A. Kirsten Mullen argue that the Black-white wealth gap is not an incidental byproduct of class inequality but the measurable residue of state-backed racial dispossession, and that an adequate reparations program would have to be large enough to close that gap rather than merely symbolize regret ([William A. Darity Jr. and A. Kirsten Mullen, *From Here to Equality*](https://uncpress.org/book/9781469654973/from-here-to-equality/)). Their position is controversial precisely because it refuses the sentimental version of repair. If the injury was cumulative, then repair must also be cumulative.

Still, I did not want the debate to collapse into righteous accounting alone. There are real questions about political form. One is eligibility. Darity and Mullen propose a lineage standard plus a period of self-identification as Black, which is more precise than ordinary race administration but still contestable at the margins ([Darity and Mullen, *From Here to Equality*](https://uncpress.org/book/9781469654973/from-here-to-equality/)). Another is democratic legitimacy. In a multiracial polity with rising authoritarian temptation, an enormous race-specific transfer could become a gift to every demagogue eager to tell poor non-Black citizens that they are financing a moral drama staged against them. We do not get to pretend backlash is imaginary. Reconstruction itself was overthrown not just by white terrorism but by a national willingness to abandon Black freedom when it became politically inconvenient ([Du Bois, *Black Reconstruction*](https://archive.org/details/blackreconstruc00dubo); [Equal Justice Initiative, "Reconstruction in America"](https://eji.org/reports/reconstruction-in-america/)).

Du Bois answered that fear with a severity I could not dismiss. Justice does not become unjust because the unjust threaten tantrums. He reminded me that what he called the "propaganda of history" was precisely the national habit of laundering white violence into neutral practicality ([W. E. B. Du Bois, *Black Reconstruction in America*](https://archive.org/details/blackreconstruc00dubo)). If one always discounts remedies because racists will exploit them, racists acquire a permanent veto over repair. Politics matters, yes. But politics that takes the racist reaction as its highest reality is already half conquered.

I said the objection was not only about white reaction. It was also about what the state makes of race when it pays by race. There is a liberal anxiety, not entirely foolish, that the state should stop sorting persons into hardened racial boxes rather than entrenching them further. The equal protection tradition is often invoked here, though usually too cleanly, because the Court's actual doctrine has oscillated between suspicion of racial classification and indifference to racial subordination ([U.S. Const. amend. XIV](https://constitution.congress.gov/constitution/amendment-14/); [*Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/20-1199_hgdj.pdf)). What if reparations, however justified historically, leave intact the very grammar of race that produced the injury?

He laughed, and I was glad he did, because the laugh had contempt in it. The state, he said, has never stopped making race. It made race at the slave auction, in the census, in the one-drop rule, in FHA maps, in school districts, in policing patterns, and in selective access to credit, mobility, and inheritance ([Library of Congress, "The African American Odyssey: Free Blacks in the Antebellum Period"](https://www.loc.gov/collections/african-american-odyssey/articles-and-essays/free-blacks-in-the-antebellum-period/); [Richard Rothstein, *The Color of Law*](https://wwnorton.com/books/The-Color-of-Law/); [Mapping Inequality, University of Richmond et al.](https://dsl.richmond.edu/panorama/redlining/)). To object to reparations because they are race-conscious is to arrive hundreds of years late, after race-conscious looting, and suddenly discover a delicate attachment to neutrality.

That was his best argument, and I felt its bite. Much of American "colorblindness" has functioned less as innocence than as amnesia. Yet I kept worrying another edge of the problem. A cash transfer, even a huge one, can be both just and inadequate. It might narrow the wealth gap while leaving intact segregated schools, environmental sacrifice zones, maternal mortality disparities, and the criminal legal machinery that continues to distribute vulnerability along racial lines ([Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, "Working Together to Reduce Black Maternal Mortality"](https://www.cdc.gov/healthequity/features/maternal-mortality/index.html); [U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, "Environmental Justice"](https://www.epa.gov/environmentaljustice); [The Sentencing Project, "Report to the United Nations on Racial Disparities in the U.S. Criminal Justice System"](https://www.sentencingproject.org/reports/report-to-the-united-nations-on-racial-disparities-in-the-u-s-criminal-justice-system/)). If reparations are framed as settlement, the nation may try to purchase absolution on the cheap, write a large check, and then declare the account closed.

Du Bois agreed more quickly than I expected. He did not treat reparations as a magic solvent. He treated them as one necessary form of truth-telling by fiscal means. In that sense, the debate over cash versus structure is often falsely posed. Reconstruction failed not because it attempted too much for Black freedom, but because it attempted too little and then surrendered even that under pressure from capital and white supremacy ([Du Bois, *Black Reconstruction*](https://archive.org/details/blackreconstruc00dubo)). Reparations, he said, should not be imagined as the end of transformation but as proof that transformation has actually begun. A government unwilling to transfer wealth in response to racial plunder is a government that still does not believe the plunder counts.

That shifted the conversation. The real issue was no longer whether universal programs are desirable. They are. The issue was whether universalism can become a laundering device, a way of speaking nobly about class while dodging the specificity of anti-Black expropriation. This is where Du Bois remained merciless. In *The Souls of Black Folk*, he wrote that the problem of the twentieth century would be the problem of the color line ([W. E. B. Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/408)). The sentence remains alive because the line was never only psychological; it was administrative, fiscal, territorial, and carceral. Universal policy can soften that line. It cannot, by itself, explain why the line was drawn, who profited, and what is owed.

We argued until the morning flattened into a dull gray. I asked him the hardest practical question I could manage. Suppose Congress created a serious reparations program, lineage-based, race-specific, and enormous. Suppose further that the same coalition had only enough power for one historic measure. Would he take reparations over universal healthcare, labor law reform, or a federal housing guarantee?

He refused the premise as a trap, which annoyed me because it was obviously also true. Politics often presents scarcity as natural when scarcity has been manufactured by elite veto. But after a while he gave me a better answer. If forced into the false choice, he said, one should ask not which policy sounds most noble, but which refusal tells the deepest truth about the nation. A state that will provide healthcare to all while refusing reparations is saying that suffering matters but theft does not. A state that pays reparations while refusing universal welfare is saying that one historic ledger matters but shared vulnerability does not. Both refusals reveal deformities. Justice requires refusing both lies.

That answer was inconvenient, which is one reason I believed it. The strongest case for reparations is not that class politics is a fraud, nor that universalism is worthless. It is that there are injuries universalism is structurally tempted to blur. The strongest caution against reparations is not that Black Americans have no claim, because they do, but that ruling classes love one-off settlements that leave governing institutions fundamentally untouched. So the serious position, however unwieldy, is harder than either slogan. The United States should pursue race-specific reparations for descendants of American slavery and Jim Crow, designed at a scale commensurate with the wealth taken, while also advancing universal programs that decommodify the conditions of life. Reparations without reconstruction risks absolution theater. Reconstruction without reparations risks elegant evasion.

Du Bois seemed almost satisfied, which made me suspicious. Before he stepped back through the rectangle in the grass, he told me that Americans enjoy moral language most when it costs nothing. That is why this argument remains so explosive. Reparations are frightening not because the case is unintelligible, but because it is intelligible in dollars, land, budgets, inheritance, and power. It is one thing to admit history was cruel. It is another to accept that history wrote invoices.

Then the rectangle closed. The clay dried. He was dead again, and the country was still alive enough to choose whether justice means memory, money, structure, or all three at once.
