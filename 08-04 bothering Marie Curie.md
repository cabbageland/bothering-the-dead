today is 08-04.

I found Marie Curie in the Pantheon crypt, where France stores its official dead as if glory were a temperature the state could keep below boiling. The Pantheon's own guide marks the essential fact: in 1995, Marie Curie became the first woman to enter the Pantheon for her own merits ([Pantheon, "The crypt"](https://www.paris-pantheon.fr/en/discover/the-crypt)). That sentence is so polished it nearly hides the violence in it. A woman has to be exceptionally luminous before a nation admits she was not merely reflected light.

Her tomb was not theatrical. No green glow. No comic-book poison. No angelic Geiger counter singing under the stone. The room had the chastened symmetry of republican honor: columns, inscriptions, coolness, the feeling that the dead had been catalogued by a committee with good pens. Pierre was there too, of course. The official account of her 1911 chemistry prize remembers both the shared discovery of radium and polonium and Marie's later isolation of radium and study of its nature and compounds ([Nobel Prize, "Marie Curie - Facts"](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1911/marie-curie/facts/)). But the stone I came for was hers.

I brought no radium. I am reckless in metaphor, not in municipal radiological hygiene. Instead I brought a cloud chamber, a jar of pitchblende dust imagined rather than opened, a photograph of uranium ore trucks crossing land that does not forget, a graph of carbon dioxide climbing like a legal accusation, a dry-cask diagram, a printout of the Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty, and the kind of lead box that makes a human being admit matter has a second personality.

For the resurrection I cooled the chamber until the air became a courtroom. A charged particle crossed it and left a white track: thin, sudden, eloquent. Another track appeared, then another. The invisible began signing its name. Curie, in her Nobel lecture, treated radioactivity not as magic but as a new chemical and physical fact, a property through which radium and polonium forced scientists to revise what an element was allowed to be ([Marie Curie, "Radium and the New Concepts in Chemistry," Nobel Lecture, 1911](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1911/marie-curie/lecture/)). I arranged the cloud tracks above the tomb like a script only matter could write.

The crypt exhaled.

Marie Curie opened her eyes with the weary alertness of someone accustomed to having her work interrupted by men, institutions, illness, committees, patriotism, and death.

"You have brought apparatus," she said.

"I thought you would distrust a resurrection without one."

"I distrust most things without one."

Good. We had the right dead physicist.

Today's question was this: **Should a civilization that is overheating itself build nuclear power aggressively as part of decarbonization, or does nuclear energy carry a moral contamination so deep - accidents, waste, mining, weapons, secrecy, state coercion - that refusing it remains the more honest politics?**

The stakes are not ornamental. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change says human activities, principally greenhouse-gas emissions, have unequivocally caused global warming, with impacts already widespread and every increment of warming intensifying multiple hazards ([IPCC, AR6 Synthesis Report, Summary for Policymakers](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/summary-for-policymakers/)). The World Health Organization estimates that ambient outdoor air pollution caused 4.2 million premature deaths worldwide in 2019, much of the burden riding on fine particulate matter from combustion and industrial activity ([WHO, "Ambient air pollution"](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/ambient-%28outdoor%29-air-quality-and-health)). The International Energy Agency says electricity demand is rising quickly from ordinary electrification and from new loads such as data centers and artificial intelligence, while nuclear remains a low-emissions, dispatchable source that produces just under 10 percent of global electricity ([IEA, "The Path to a New Era for Nuclear Energy," 2025 PDF](https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/b6a6fc8c-c62e-411d-a15c-bf211ccc06f3/ThePathtoaNewEraforNuclearEnergy.pdf)).

So the question is not "Do you like nuclear?" That is childish. I also like storms until a roof is missing.

The real question is whether our fear is intelligent enough to count all the dead.

Curie sat up. The crypt did not approve, but stone has always been bad at argument.

"You begin from panic," she said.

"From arithmetic."

"The two are often twins."

"Fine. I begin from arithmetic with a pulse. We are burning old plants and animals from the underground until the atmosphere becomes a failing machine. Nuclear power promises enormous energy from tiny quantities of matter. But it arrives surrounded by evacuation zones, armed guards, poisoned mines, spent fuel, secrecy, and bombs. That is not a clean machine. That is a machine with priests."

"All serious machines have priests," she said. "The issue is whether they also have inspectors."

That was a good start, rude and correct.

The pro-nuclear case, at its strongest, is not the cartoon version in which uranium descends from heaven wearing a lab coat. It is comparative and unsentimental. Nuclear plants produce steady electricity without burning carbon at the point of generation; the IEA's 2022 report argues that nuclear can make the shift away from unabated fossil fuels faster and more secure, especially because wind and solar need dispatchable companions in electricity systems ([IEA, "Nuclear Power and Secure Energy Transitions," 2022 PDF](https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/0498c8b8-e17f-4346-9bde-dad2ad4458c4/NuclearPowerandSecureEnergyTransitions.pdf)). In the 2025 nuclear report, the IEA says interest in nuclear expansion is at its highest level since the oil crises of the 1970s, with more than 40 countries supporting expansion and 63 reactors under construction worldwide as of late 2024 ([IEA, 2025 PDF](https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/b6a6fc8c-c62e-411d-a15c-bf211ccc06f3/ThePathtoaNewEraforNuclearEnergy.pdf)).

"That is momentum," Curie said.

"That is also lobbying, nationalism, vendor optimism, and grid desperation wearing the same hat."

"Then remove the hat and count the head."

I tried.

There is a stupid anti-nuclear argument and a serious one. The stupid argument treats radiation as metaphysical filth: invisible, therefore demonic; long-lived, therefore uniquely evil; complicated, therefore illegitimate. A century of physics should have cured us of that superstition. Electromagnetism is invisible. Viruses are invisible. Carbon dioxide is invisible. The problem with a hazard is not whether it offends the mammal eye. The problem is exposure, dose, pathway, institutional control, and consent. Curie's work helped teach the world that hidden processes are not unreal merely because human nerves did not evolve to feel them ([Curie, Nobel Lecture, 1911](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1911/marie-curie/lecture/)).

Curie nodded once, sharply.

"People are comfortable with danger when it arrives as smoke," she said. "They are offended when it arrives as a calculation."

"Smoke also killed visibly enough."

"Not visibly enough. That is why it continued."

She had me there. Fossil fuel harm is often democratic in the worst sense: spread over lungs, heat waves, droughts, storms, wages, insurance tables, and the unborn. It does not always produce one cinematic crater. It becomes asthma, missed school, broken crops, a funeral whose cause is distributed among molecules and policy failures. If the moral imagination only responds to spectacle, then coal receives a subsidy from narrative weakness.

The serious anti-nuclear argument begins exactly where the stupid one stops. Nuclear risk is not merely a feeling. It has history. The Chernobyl accident was the worst nuclear-power accident in history; UNSCEAR records that it caused the deaths of workers within weeks, injured many more, and was followed by thousands of thyroid cancers among people exposed as children and adolescents in Belarus, Ukraine, and the Russian Federation ([UNSCEAR, "The Chornobyl Accident"](https://www.unscear.org/unscear/en/areas-of-work/chernobyl.html)). Fukushima did not produce the same radiation-health profile; UNSCEAR's later Fukushima assessment says no adverse health effects among Fukushima residents have been documented that could be directly attributed to radiation exposure, nor are such effects expected to be detectable in the future ([UNSCEAR, "Fukushima Report FAQ"](https://www.unscear.org/unscear/en/areas-of-work/fukushima-report-faq.html)). But a forced evacuation, a shattered region, and a collapsed public trust are not "nothing" because a cancer graph does not scream.

"You are tempted," Curie said, "to make trust a softer fact than dose."

"Is it not?"

"No. Trust is the shielding around every dangerous technology. Remove it, and the public receives the full exposure."

That was the second good blow.

The nuclear industry sometimes speaks as if public distrust were a mood disorder. It is not. It is a memory system. People remember Chernobyl, Fukushima, weapons tests, secret releases, contaminated mines, and officials who discover transparency only after the press has arrived. They remember that "safe" often means "safe for people far from the extraction." The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency says nearly 30 million tons of uranium ore were extracted from Navajo lands from 1944 to 1986, leaving over 500 abandoned uranium mines on and near Navajo Nation, with homes and water sources affected by elevated radiation levels ([EPA, "Navajo Nation Abandoned Uranium Mines Cleanup"](https://www.epa.gov/navajo-nation-uranium-cleanup/aum-cleanup)). EPA's health-effects page warns that higher exposures to uranium and radiation can be harmful and notes that some unregulated water sources on Navajo Nation exceed drinking-water standards for uranium and other chemicals ([EPA, "What Are the Health Effects of Uranium?"](https://www.epa.gov/navajo-nation-uranium-cleanup/what-are-health-effects-uranium)).

"Then say it plainly," I told her. "The light in the reactor may have a mine in its shadow."

Curie looked down at her hands.

"Yes," she said. "Say it plainly."

There are moments when the resurrected dead do not defend themselves because the future has out-argued them. Marie Curie died in 1934, before the reactor age, before Hiroshima and Nagasaki, before Chernobyl, before the domestic mythology of "Atoms for Peace" learned to travel with military escorts. She did not invent the power plant or the bomb. Still, she had opened one of the doors through which the century walked. A serious conversation with the dead cannot treat discovery as innocence merely because consequence arrived later.

"Do you blame me?" she asked.

"No."

"Do you absolve me?"

"Also no."

That answer pleased neither of us, which is sometimes the sign of progress.

The moral status of discovery is not identical with the moral status of deployment. A scientist can reveal a capacity in nature before society knows how to govern it. But after revelation comes character. Nuclear energy is not only a physics problem. It is a government problem, a labor problem, an imperial problem, a memory problem, a finance problem, a weapons problem, and a problem of whether human beings can maintain competence after the ribbon-cutting photographs stop circulating.

Curie lifted the NPT printout and read fast, as if treaties were merely long laboratory labels. The treaty's bargain is explicit enough to be morally awkward: it binds non-nuclear-weapon states away from acquisition, recognizes peaceful nuclear-energy rights under treaty constraints, and commits parties to pursue nuclear disarmament ([UNODA Treaties Database, "Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons"](https://treaties.unoda.org/t/npt)). That bargain has never been clean. It asks the same uranium civilization to divide peaceful use from weaponization by safeguards, law, inspection, and restraint. The distinction is real, and also permanently anxious.

"This is the part I cannot admire," I said. "A wind turbine is not innocent of mining or land conflict, but it does not sit conceptually adjacent to an enrichment cascade and a weapons program."

"Adjacent is not identical," Curie said.

"No. But adjacency matters."

"So does combustion."

"You keep returning to fossil fuels."

"Because you keep wanting to judge nuclear in isolation, like a vial on velvet."

She was right again. Nuclear exceptionalism can work in both directions. Its fans say nuclear is special, therefore entitled to subsidy, secrecy, emergency powers, and a patriotic aura. Its enemies say nuclear is special, therefore no comparison with ordinary energy harms is morally admissible. Both forms of specialness make thought stupid. A reactor is not a demon. It is also not a chalice. It is an engineered bargain with fission, institutions, time, and public fear.

The precautionary principle should not rescue laziness. Principle 15 of the Rio Declaration says that where there are threats of serious or irreversible environmental damage, lack of full scientific certainty should not be used as a reason for postponing cost-effective measures ([Convention on Biological Diversity, "Precautionary Approach"](https://www.cbd.int/marine/precautionary.shtml)). Anti-nuclear politics often invokes precaution as if it meant never touching a dangerous tool. But the climate crisis is also a serious and irreversible threat. The precautionary question is not "Which option contains danger?" They all do. The question is "Which portfolio reduces total irreversible harm under the constraints of physics, time, money, justice, and institutional capacity?"

Curie smiled very slightly.

"Portfolio," she said. "An ugly word."

"Yes."

"But not as ugly as ash."

The pro-nuclear side has to earn that portfolio. It cannot simply chant "baseload" and expect applause. The United States' newest large reactors at Plant Vogtle are a useful bucket of cold water: EIA reported in 2024 that Vogtle Units 3 and 4 began commercial operation years later than originally expected and that Georgia Power estimated total project cost at more than $30 billion, compared with an original expectation of $14 billion and operation in 2016 and 2017 ([U.S. EIA, "Plant Vogtle Unit 4 begins commercial operation"](https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=61963)). The IEA likewise warns that long-time market leaders such as the United States and France have struggled with delays and cost overruns for new large reactors ([IEA, 2025 PDF](https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/b6a6fc8c-c62e-411d-a15c-bf211ccc06f3/ThePathtoaNewEraforNuclearEnergy.pdf)).

"So do not build badly," Curie said.

"That is not a policy. That is a bumper sticker for people who have never met procurement."

"Then make it a policy."

Annoying, because correct again.

If nuclear is to be defended, the defense must be institutional, not romantic. Preserve existing reactors when regulators judge they can operate safely, because replacing working low-carbon generation with gas is not moral seriousness. Build only where the public regulator is independent enough to anger the operator. Standardize designs instead of turning every project into a bespoke national cathedral. Put waste management and decommissioning into the financing at the beginning, not as a ghost invoice for grandchildren. Refuse fuel chains that treat Indigenous land, colonized land, or poor land as the proper address for invisible costs. Make non-proliferation a design constraint, not a speech at the end. Tell the truth about schedule and cost even when the truth ruins the press conference.

"You have made nuclear sound difficult," Curie said.

"It is difficult."

"Everything worth doing is not therefore worth doing."

"Exactly."

"But many things worth doing are difficult. You have not escaped judgment."

The waste question is where judgment becomes geological. The lazy anti-nuclear slogan says the waste problem is unsolved forever. The lazy pro-nuclear slogan says it is solved because the volume is small. Both slogans are worse than the waste. The IAEA's disposal safety requirements establish that radioactive-waste disposal needs governmental, regulatory, operator, siting, design, operation, closure, and post-closure safety responsibilities, not merely barrels and optimism ([IAEA, "Disposal of Radioactive Waste," SSR-5 PDF](https://www-pub.iaea.org/mtcd/publications/pdf/pub1449_web.pdf)). Finland's Posiva describes ONKALO as the first final disposal facility for spent nuclear fuel excavated deep into bedrock and moving toward operation ([Posiva, "Front page"](https://www.posiva.fi/en/)).

That matters. A solved waste problem is not a slogan; it is a site, a license, geology, money, transport, casks, monitoring, political consent, and the humility to admit that "forever" is too large a word for engineers. But it is also true that fossil fuel waste has been dumped into the atmosphere without a repository, without guards, without warning labels, and without consent from future lungs. Carbon dioxide is not less real because it has excellent public relations.

Curie touched the cloud chamber. Another white track appeared and vanished.

"There," she said. "A danger with handwriting."

"The atmosphere also has handwriting."

"Yes. But slower. That is why you pretend not to read it."

I hated her a little, which is one of the pleasures of a good argument.

Then I tried the question that makes the whole nuclear debate misbehave: democracy. Who gets to say yes? A national majority? The town near the plant? The miners? The people along the rail line carrying waste? The island nation threatened by warming seas? The child whose asthma is worsened by combustion? The engineer who knows the grid? The activist who knows the burial ground? The investor who wants guaranteed returns? The future person who inherits both climate and casks?

No single consent procedure can make all those constituencies whole. But some procedures can be recognizably indecent. A nuclear program built by hiding risk is disqualified. A fossil program built by normalizing mass respiratory harm is also disqualified. A climate policy that exports dirty work to the people with the least political power is not clean because the press release says "net zero." The EPA's Navajo uranium record is not an anti-nuclear talking point to be nodded at and then filed away; it is a standing indictment of any energy transition that wants low-carbon electricity without low-colonial behavior ([EPA, Navajo cleanup](https://www.epa.gov/navajo-nation-uranium-cleanup/aum-cleanup)).

Curie leaned forward.

"You are asking for purity."

"No. I am asking for responsibility."

"Responsibility without impurity is a fantasy."

"Responsibility without limits is public relations."

That one landed. She became quiet.

The dead are excellent at silence because they have practiced.

I told her my provisional verdict, because a philosophical essay that refuses a verdict is sometimes only cowardice with better paragraphing. Nuclear power should not be treated as the moral center of decarbonization. Renewables, storage, transmission, efficiency, demand flexibility, geothermal, hydro where appropriate, and electrification all matter. The IEA itself says renewables lead the low-emissions buildout even in scenarios where nuclear helps ([IEA, 2022 PDF](https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/0498c8b8-e17f-4346-9bde-dad2ad4458c4/NuclearPowerandSecureEnergyTransitions.pdf)). But neither should nuclear be expelled from climate policy as if taboo were analysis. Existing safe reactors are valuable. New reactors can be justified where institutions are competent, communities are meaningfully consulted, waste has a credible path, financing is honest, and the project actually arrives within the climate-relevant calendar. Small modular reactors may become useful, but the IEA notes first commercial SMR projects are set to start around 2030 and that their cost reductions and deployment remain conditional on policy, regulation, and industry delivery ([IEA, 2025 PDF](https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/b6a6fc8c-c62e-411d-a15c-bf211ccc06f3/ThePathtoaNewEraforNuclearEnergy.pdf)). A promise scheduled after the damage is not yet electricity.

"So you choose neither faith nor refusal," Curie said.

"I choose conditional yes."

"That is less dramatic."

"Drama has done enough infrastructure planning."

She laughed then, briefly, almost against her will.

But she wanted the philosophical bone, not the policy cartilage.

"What is nuclear energy to you?" she asked.

"A test of whether modern people can live with a dangerous good without lying about either noun."

"Good."

"And to you?"

She looked around the crypt, at the white tracks in the chilled air, at the papers spread like accusations over the stone.

"It is a proof that matter is not as docile as human categories," she said. "You wanted elements to be little bricks. They were storms. You wanted energy to be fire. It was binding. You wanted danger to announce itself by smell and color. It did not. Physics is beautiful because it humiliates the senses and then gives them better instruments."

That was the celebration I had come for. Not the obedient celebration of a technology, but the celebration of physics as disciplined astonishment. Radioactivity was a scandal against common matter. It said the stable world was performing transformations in secret. It made the atom historical. It put time inside stone.

"And ethics?" I asked.

"Ethics is what begins after astonishment," she said.

That may be the cleanest sentence anyone has ever dragged out of a grave.

I thought of the current nuclear arguments, their dreary tribal flags. The booster who thinks every objection is irrational. The opponent who thinks every comparison is propaganda. The climate hawk who wants megawatts first and governance eventually. The degrowth romantic who mistakes scarcity for virtue until the hospital loses power. The tech executive shopping for a private reactor to feed machines while calling it civilization. The politician who cannot distinguish a licensing reform from a magic spell. The activist who remembers the mine. The grid operator whose spreadsheet has no space for national myth. The child breathing particulate matter near a plant that is legal because its dead are ordinary.

Curie listened without interrupting. Death had improved her patience, though not by much.

"Your age wants energy without tragedy," she said.

"Yes."

"It cannot have that."

"Then what can it have?"

"Energy with memory."

That is probably the answer. Not nuclear innocence. Not nuclear damnation. Energy with memory. Count the carbon and the cesium. Count the asthma and the evacuation. Count the miners and the engineers. Count the waste casks and the flood maps. Count the cancers where the evidence supports them, and refuse to invent them where it does not. Count delay as a moral event. Count cost overrun as more than embarrassment when it crowds out faster decarbonization. Count fear as information, not as sovereign. Count physics as reality's way of refusing our preferred symbols.

Then decide.

Marie Curie began to fade when the cloud chamber warmed. The tracks thinned. The air returned to its ordinary transparency, which is only another word for our limited eyesight.

"Do not ask the public to trust nuclear power," she said.

"No?"

"Ask nuclear power to become trustworthy."

"And if it does?"

"Then use it where it helps."

"And if it does not?"

"Then do not pretend the atom failed. Admit the institution did."

Her hand rested for a moment on the lid of the tomb. I wanted to ask her whether she regretted the door she helped open. But that is a vulgar question unless asked of oneself first. We inherit doors. We also build locks, alarms, maps, treaties, excuses, and sometimes bombs. The dead can warn us, but they cannot govern for us.

Before she disappeared, she pointed at the CO2 graph.

"That is also radioactivity," she said.

"No, chemically it is not."

"Do not be small. I mean it is invisible power changing bodies through time."

Fair enough. The physicist had earned the metaphor.

The crypt settled back into civic stone. The cloud chamber sat dull and wet. The NPT printout curled at the edge. Somewhere far from Paris, a community still lived near abandoned uranium mines. Somewhere else, a coal plant still turned ordinary air into a quiet killer. Somewhere else, an engineer was either shaving risk from a reactor design or shaving truth from a cost estimate. Somewhere else, a child who did not vote for any of this breathed whatever policy had made available.

I left the Pantheon before morning with no glow on my hands.

That is not the same as clean.
