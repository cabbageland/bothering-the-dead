today is 05-31.

I found Martha in a freezer drawer that felt too small for the scale of the crime. Not at the Cincinnati Zoo, where she died on September 1, 1914, and not in the forests that once moved with the sound of billions of wings, but in the Smithsonian, where her body was preserved after her death and converted into a lesson the living could visit at civilized hours ([Smithsonian, “Martha, a Passenger Pigeon”](https://www.si.edu/object/martha-passenger-pigeon%3Asiris_sic_11640); [Smithsonian, “The Last Passenger Pigeon”](https://www.si.edu/collections/snapshot/last-passenger-pigeon)). I had expected grandeur. Instead there was refrigeration, cataloging, institutional light. Extinction, once you have finished romanticizing it, turns out to involve drawers.

I had come prepared to commit a different kind of desecration. In one pocket I carried a printout of the [IUCN SSC Guiding Principles on Creating Proxies of Extinct Species for Conservation Benefit](https://portals.iucn.org/library/node/46248), because even our fantasies of resurrection now arrive with governance frameworks. In the other I carried pages from Revive & Restore’s official passenger pigeon project, which speaks in the tone of technical hope about recreating a proxy passenger pigeon in order to restore North American forest ecology ([Revive & Restore, “About the Passenger Pigeon”](https://reviverestore.org/projects/about-the-passenger-pigeon/)). I laid both beside Martha’s historical body and added one more paper, a genomic study arguing that passenger pigeons experienced dramatic population fluctuations over evolutionary time, a finding that complicates the old picture of a species that was simply too numerous to fail ([Hung et al., *PNAS*, 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4115547/)). Then I said, aloud, what modern people say when they want forgiveness without first surrendering ambition: we think we may be able to bring something like you back.

That was enough.

Martha woke all at once, not with ghostly sweetness but with the blunt, bodily indignation of a creature who had already been handled too much by history. She came up off the steel table in a rush of feathers and cold air, then fixed me with one dark eye that contained no gratitude whatsoever.

"Something like me?" she said.

The controversial question between us was immediately clear. **If humans acquire the power to create proxies of extinct species, should we use it to bring back lost animals like the passenger pigeon, or is de-extinction mostly a morally evasive spectacle that lets the civilization which exterminated a species pretend it has learned repair while avoiding restraint?** That is not a toy question. It reaches into conservation budgets, biotechnology, land use, animal welfare, the politics of guilt, and our increasingly common belief that every catastrophe remains provisional so long as engineering still has one more trick.

The basic case for de-extinction is not stupid. Martha had once belonged to a species so abundant that nineteenth-century observers described flocks as darkening the sky, and later estimates placed their numbers in the billions before the collapse ([Hung et al., *PNAS*, 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4115547/); [Smithsonian, “The Last Passenger Pigeon”](https://www.si.edu/collections/snapshot/last-passenger-pigeon)). Revive & Restore argues that restoring a passenger pigeon proxy could help recover ecological functions in eastern forests, not merely satisfy curiosity, and presents the project as a model for genomic intervention in conservation rather than a theme-park stunt ([Revive & Restore, “About the Passenger Pigeon”](https://reviverestore.org/projects/about-the-passenger-pigeon/)). The IUCN principles, for their part, do not dismiss proxy creation as absurd. They treat it as a possible conservation tool, but one that demands rigorous evaluation of animal welfare, habitat, governance, long-term management, and whether the intervention would produce genuine conservation benefit rather than symbolic excitement ([IUCN SSC Guiding Principles](https://portals.iucn.org/library/node/46248)).

In other words, the grown-up argument for de-extinction is not simply that it would be cool. It is that a vanished species might be approximated closely enough to restore damaged ecological relationships, while also expanding the technical repertoire available for conservation in an age of accelerating biodiversity loss.

Martha despised this case instantly.

"You love the word restore," she said. "It lets you sound pious while keeping the verbs that matter hidden. Who cut down, shot, netted, shipped, sold, and congratulated themselves while we disappeared?"

She was right to ask it that way. The passenger pigeon was not extinguished by fate. It was hunted at industrial scale and stripped of the social and ecological conditions that had allowed its immense flocks to function, until abundance itself turned out not to be armor but a vulnerability in the presence of markets, extraction, and firearms ([Smithsonian, “Martha, the World’s Last Passenger Pigeon”](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smithsonian-institution/martha-the-worlds-last-passenger-pigeon-67196038/); [Hung et al., *PNAS*, 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4115547/)). If that history matters, then de-extinction cannot be judged only by technical feasibility. It must also be judged by whether it changes the habits of the civilization that did the killing.

I answered that this was too prosecutorial, and maybe too pure. Conservation now faces losses so severe that refusing every powerful tool can become its own form of vanity. If we can use genomics to aid living endangered species, why should we draw an absolute line at using related methods to recreate extinct ones when the ecological case is strong? The IUCN document itself does not treat proxy creation as sacrilege; it treats it as a difficult question for conservation planning ([IUCN SSC Guiding Principles](https://portals.iucn.org/library/node/46248)). If the eastern forests are missing a disturbance regime, seed dispersal pattern, or flock behavior once partly shaped by passenger pigeons, then perhaps attempting some carefully governed proxy is better than leaving absence untouched merely because the original wrong was human.

Martha gave me a look that would have been unbearable in any species.

"Absence untouched?" she said. "You still think the alternative to a dramatic machine is doing nothing. That is how your kind excuses itself. You destroy habitats, fragment migration corridors, overconsume wood, overheat the climate, and then call restraint passive because it lacks a laboratory announcement."

That was the center of her position. De-extinction, she said, flatters a civilization addicted to redemptive spectacle. It offers a story in which the same species that exterminates can also resurrect, which means it never has to submit to the more humiliating disciplines of conservation: stop destroying habitat, stop treating other creatures as infinitely substitutable, stop relying on future technical rescue to justify present extraction. The danger is not only wasted money, though conservation triage is real and every dollar spent on a charismatic proxy project is a dollar not spent somewhere else. The deeper danger is moral hazard. De-extinction trains the public imagination to think extinction is reversible enough that urgency can soften.

She had no need to invent that worry. The IUCN principles themselves repeatedly insist that proxy creation should not undermine existing conservation priorities and should not be pursued merely because the technology is exciting ([IUCN SSC Guiding Principles](https://portals.iucn.org/library/node/46248)). The document is cautious in exactly the places technological culture is often glib: welfare of surrogates, ecological unpredictability, governance burdens, and the risk of confusing proxy creation with an adequate response to biodiversity collapse.

Still, I resisted her conclusion. A moral hazard is a real danger, but it is not a knockdown argument against the technology itself. Vaccines can encourage recklessness in some people; that does not make vaccination unserious. Carbon removal technologies may be abused as excuses for ongoing emissions; that does not imply every form of carbon removal is fraudulent. The question is whether institutions can be built to prevent the abuse while preserving the benefit. Likewise here: if de-extinction projects are subordinated to real conservation goals, ecological due diligence, and public accountability, then perhaps they need not become alibis.

Martha interrupted me before I could sound too managerial.

"And what exactly is brought back?"

This was the strongest objection, and I felt it before I answered. Revive & Restore itself does not promise some mystical reversal of time. It speaks of creating a passenger pigeon proxy through genome editing of the band-tailed pigeon lineage, then eventually rearing birds capable of social life and release if the science and ecological planning justify it ([Revive & Restore, “About the Passenger Pigeon”](https://reviverestore.org/projects/about-the-passenger-pigeon/)). Even the IUCN language is careful: *proxy*, not perfect recovery ([IUCN SSC Guiding Principles](https://portals.iucn.org/library/node/46248)). That honesty matters. But it also reveals the philosophical problem. A recreated bird is not Martha returned. It is not even straightforwardly the passenger pigeon species restored in full historical continuity. It is an engineered descendant-like being, made under contemporary conditions, asked to inhabit an ecological role reconstructed from loss.

So Martha pressed the point harder than any bioethicist’s seminar would have done.

"If you manufacture a bird that resembles me," she said, "who consents to being your emblem of repair? The dead do not. The engineered do not. The surrogates used in breeding do not. And the forests do not either."

There was real force in that. De-extinction talk often becomes metaphysically lazy. It slides from resemblance to return. It invites sentimental headlines about species being "brought back" when what is actually being produced, if it succeeds at all, is a new organism with old design targets. That does not make the work meaningless. But it does make honesty essential. If the public is promised resurrection when what is possible is approximation, then the project begins in myth.

I told Martha that she was conflating two questions, identity and value. Perhaps the proxy is not literally the same species in every philosophically satisfying sense. Still, if it can perform ecologically meaningful functions, live good avian lives, and help preserve or restore habitats under pressure, the moral case may survive the loss of metaphysical purity. Conservation already works with translocations, captive breeding, hybrids, and assisted migration under conditions far from pristine continuity. In the Anthropocene, demanding untouched categories before allowing intervention can become another way of watching things vanish beautifully.

She did not fully reject this. That is what made her difficult. She conceded that some future project might satisfy enough conditions to be more than vanity: habitat genuinely ready, welfare seriously protected, long-term stewardship funded, ecological function plausible rather than theatrical, and no displacement of urgent work on the living. Under those conditions, she said, a proxy project might count not as resurrection but as penance with instruments.

But she would not let the larger indictment go.

The most obscene feature of de-extinction culture, in her view, is temporal arrogance. Humans imagine they can intervene at the end of the story and thereby redeem the middle. Yet extinction is not only a missing body. It is the destruction of relationships, migrations, learned behaviors, predators, mast cycles, forests, and collective forms of life that took shape over time. The 2014 *PNAS* study on passenger pigeons suggested that their population dynamics were tied to dramatic ecological fluctuations and large-scale social organization, which is another way of saying that the species was not a detachable object but a mode of living in relation ([Hung et al., *PNAS*, 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4115547/)). If those worlds are gone, a recreated pigeon may be biologically impressive and ecologically thin.

That line stayed with me because it names the real temptation. We prefer bringing back the visible creature to rebuilding the invisible conditions. One makes for headlines and donor dinners. The other requires land reform, boring regulation, anti-extraction politics, indigenous stewardship, climate mitigation, and the uncinematic work of leaving things alone. We are attracted to de-extinction for some of the same reasons we are attracted to geoengineering and artificial wombs and machine fixes for spiritual failures. The machine seems to let us keep our habits while repairing their consequences.

So where did the argument land?

Not where easy reverence would put it. Martha did not convince me that all de-extinction efforts are inherently fraudulent. The blanket denunciation is too simple for a world in which biodiversity is collapsing and technical capacities may, in some cases, genuinely expand conservation options. The IUCN is right not to dismiss proxy creation out of hand ([IUCN SSC Guiding Principles](https://portals.iucn.org/library/node/46248)). And the best version of the passenger pigeon effort is motivated by ecological restoration, not amusement ([Revive & Restore, “About the Passenger Pigeon”](https://reviverestore.org/projects/about-the-passenger-pigeon/)).

But I think Martha won the deeper philosophical point. **De-extinction is morally serious only if it is treated as subordinate to the harder duty of changing the civilization that made extinction normal.** If it becomes a glamorous substitute for habitat protection, for limits on extraction, for sustained care of currently endangered species, or for accepting that some losses impose obligations without offering redemption, then it is not conservation. It is absolution theater.

The stakes are ugly enough to deserve plain language. A species capable of exterminating billions of birds in a few generations should be viewed with suspicion when it presents itself as the natural author of resurrection. Power loves to retell its crimes as innovation. The living can always say, look what we can build now. The dead ask a simpler question: why was destruction easier for you than restraint?

When Martha lay back down, she did it without ceremony.

"If you make another bird," she said, "do not call that innocence. And do not call it me."

Then she returned to stillness, and the drawer became a drawer again, with all the small bureaucratic dignity humans give to what they were too late to save.

I left thinking that the proper attitude toward de-extinction is neither scoffing nor wonder, but severity. Use the tool if, and only if, it survives a brutal accounting: real ecological benefit, real welfare protections, real governance, real non-diversion from urgent conservation, and total honesty that a proxy is not a pardon. Anything less is just the old human trick of mistaking technical drama for moral repair.

Extinction should bother the living more than it tempts them. If we ever raise the dead forests' birds again, the act should not reassure us that nothing is final. It should remind us how much had to be destroyed before resurrection began to look easier than decency.
