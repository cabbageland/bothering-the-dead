today is 06-07.

I found Janusz Korczak at Treblinka, which is to say I found him where the body is not available for individual inspection and the grave has been forced to become collective. The museum's account says that in the symbolic cemetery at Treblinka, among seventeen thousand stones, there is one named memorial: "JANUSZ KORCZAK (Henryk Goldszmit) I DZIECI" - Janusz Korczak and the children ([Muzeum Treblinka, "Biografia"](https://muzeumtreblinka.eu/informacje/biografia/)). That phrasing matters. It refuses to let him become the heroic adult detached from the children whose deaths made his fidelity visible. He was not alone in death. He should not be resurrected alone either.

I came before dawn with a pocket flashlight, a child's slate, a copy of the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, and a phone whose screen kept offering me three different ways to monitor, restrict, locate, filter, time-limit, and optimize a child I do not have. This is how the dead know we are serious now: our moral panics arrive with dashboards.

Korczak had been a Polish Jewish doctor, writer, and educator; the U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum describes him as the director of a Jewish orphanage in Warsaw from 1911 to 1942 and notes that his home was run as a "children's republic" where children had duties, rights, and a court for grievances ([USHMM, "Janusz Korczak"](https://encyclopedia.ushmm.org/content/en/article/janusz-korczak-1)). Treblinka Museum records that on August 6, 1942, the occupiers removed Korczak, Stefania Wilczynska, the staff, and about two hundred children from Dom Sierot, and that all were taken to Treblinka ([Muzeum Treblinka, "Biografia"](https://muzeumtreblinka.eu/informacje/biografia/)). He had written, lectured, organized, begged for food, built institutions, and in the end walked with children into the machinery that considered them waste.

This makes it indecently easy to turn him into a saint and therefore useless. A saint can be admired without being obeyed. I wanted him back because I wanted an argument, not a candle.

I set the slate against the stone and wrote, in chalk: **Should society protect children online by giving parents, platforms, and states stronger powers to identify, monitor, restrict, and manage them, or does that protection become a new form of adult domination disguised as care?**

The stone did not crack. Nothing theatrical happened. Then the chalk dust lifted in the cold air and arranged itself into a small courtroom: bench, rail, witness chair, ledger. A man in a worn coat stepped out from behind the memorial as if he had been waiting for the motion to be seconded.

"You brought me back," Korczak said, "to ask whether adults should be trusted with another instrument of control."

"I brought you back because the instrument is already here."

He looked at the phone in my hand with the expression of a physician examining a rash.

The protective case is not imaginary. The U.S. Surgeon General's 2023 advisory says that up to 95 percent of American youth ages 13 to 17 report using a social media platform, with more than a third reporting that they use social media "almost constantly"; the same advisory also says social media can provide connection, self-expression, and access to information for some young people while raising serious concern about harmful content exposure, problematic use, sleep disruption, cyberbullying, and mental health effects ([U.S. Surgeon General, *Social Media and Youth Mental Health*](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/sg-youth-mental-health-social-media-advisory.pdf?lang=en-US)). That is the first difficulty: the internet is not only a sewer, and not only a library. It is a sewer with a library inside it, plus a casino, a schoolyard, a clinic waiting room, a propaganda shop, a diary, a job board, a prayer corner, and a mirror that whispers advertisements while you cry.

Legislators have noticed. The 119th Congress version of the Kids Online Safety Act would require covered platforms to exercise reasonable care in the design features that contribute to foreseeable harms to minors, including eating disorders, suicidal behaviors, anxiety or depression connected to compulsive use, severe harassment, sexual exploitation, illegal drugs, gambling, alcohol, and financial harms ([S.1748, 119th Cong., sec. 102](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1748/text/is?format=txt)). It would also require safeguards for minors, such as limiting public access to personal data, limiting design features like infinite scroll and autoplay, offering controls over recommendation systems, restricting geolocation sharing, and giving parents tools to manage privacy settings, purchases, and time spent ([S.1748, sec. 103](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1748/text/is?format=txt)). The older American privacy model, COPPA, already gives parents control over data collection from children under 13 by requiring notice and verifiable parental consent for covered services ([FTC, Children's Online Privacy Protection Act](https://search.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/statutes/childrens-online-privacy-protection-act)). The United Kingdom's Online Safety Act moves in the same general weather system, requiring Ofcom codes to recommend "highly effective" age verification or age estimation in certain child-safety contexts ([Online Safety Act 2023](https://legislation.uk/online-safety-act-2023)).

So I began with the blunt argument.

"Children are being farmed," I told Korczak. "Their attention is not merely wandering. It is harvested. Their loneliness is profiled. Their compulsions are monetized. Their shame is made searchable. The Surgeon General says social media companies should limit features that maximize time, attention, and engagement, strengthen age-appropriate safety standards, raise data privacy standards, and share platform data with researchers ([U.S. Surgeon General advisory](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/sg-youth-mental-health-social-media-advisory.pdf?lang=en-US)). You spent your life defending children from adults who called domination order. Why should the adults who design engagement machines get more trust than the adults who want to lock the machines?"

Korczak smiled without softness.

"I do not trust the merchants," he said. "But your mistake is thinking distrust of merchants is trust in wardens."

That is Korczak's blade. He does not let the child disappear into the adult quarrel. In *The Child's Right to Respect*, he attacked the adult habit of treating childhood as a minor, preliminary life, as if children were only future workers, future citizens, future persons; he insisted instead that children "have been, are, and they will be" ([Korczak, *The Child's Right to Respect*, Council of Europe edition](https://www.coe.int/t/commissioner/source/prems/publicationkorczak_en.pdf)). The modern rights language says something similar in more legal prose: in all actions concerning children, the child's best interests must be a primary consideration; children capable of forming views have a right to express those views freely in matters affecting them; children have rights to expression, privacy, and access to information from diverse sources, while also having a right to protection from violence, abuse, exploitation, and injurious material ([UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, arts. 3, 12, 13, 16, 17, 19](https://www.unicef.org/child-rights-convention/convention-text)).

The convention is not a sentimental poster. It is a contradiction machine. It says protect the child and hear the child. Shield the child and respect the child's privacy. Help parents and recognize evolving capacities. Encourage access to information and protect against material injurious to well-being. The child is not a locked box labeled VULNERABLE. The child is a developing political, moral, imaginative creature whose vulnerability is real and whose agency is also real.

"Fine," I said. "But agency can be manufactured by an addiction loop. A child who says she wants another hour of the feed may be speaking with the platform's hand around her throat."

"Sometimes," Korczak said. "But adults adore that sentence because it lets them ignore the child whenever the child says something inconvenient."

He had me there. The phrase "best interests of the child" can become a velvet hood. It sounds tender while removing the face. Parents use it. Schools use it. Courts use it. Platforms use it in pastel-colored trust-and-safety reports. Governments use it with flags behind them. Everyone claims to be acting for the child; the child is in the room as evidence, not as a participant.

Korczak's orphanage court was not a libertarian paradise where children did whatever they wanted. It was an institution with rules. But the rules did not merely descend from adult power. The children had procedures, duties, grievances, self-government. Treblinka Museum's summary of his educational system lists children's rights to be themselves, to live by their own effort and thought, to have their affairs treated seriously and justly, to speak their thoughts, to protest, to play, to possess things, and to have their failures and tears respected ([Muzeum Treblinka, "System wychowawczy"](https://muzeumtreblinka.eu/informacje/system-wychowawczy/)). In the Council of Europe edition, Korczak says adults make decisions about the child while asking, essentially, what a child could possibly have to say; later he answers that without the participation of experts we will not succeed, and "the expert is the child" ([Korczak, *The Child's Right to Respect*](https://www.coe.int/t/commissioner/source/prems/publicationkorczak_en.pdf)).

"The expert is not the algorithm," he said.

"Agreed."

"The expert is not the frightened parent."

"Sometimes the parent knows the child better than anyone."

"And sometimes the parent knows only fear, vanity, obedience, and the password to the router."

That was unfair enough to be useful. Parental authority is not fake. A twelve-year-old cannot be abandoned to predatory adults, extortion schemes, pornographic recommendation funnels, gambling mechanics, eating-disorder communities, or the exquisite cruelty of classmates with cameras. The state has real reason to support parents, and the law often recognizes that children need special protection because childhood is not adulthood in smaller shoes. Even *Brown v. Entertainment Merchants Association*, which struck down California's restriction on selling violent video games to minors, acknowledged the state's legitimate power to protect children from harm, though it rejected a "free-floating power" to restrict ideas merely thought unsuitable for the young ([Brown v. Entertainment Merchants Association](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/564/786/)). *Reno v. ACLU* likewise struck down broad internet indecency restrictions partly because they suppressed too much adult speech and lacked narrow tailoring, not because protecting minors was illegitimate ([Reno v. ACLU](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/521/844/)).

This is the legal version of Korczak's moral problem. Children can need protection without becoming state property. Parents can need tools without becoming police. Platforms can need duties without becoming speech governors. The hard question is where protection stops being architecture and starts being custody.

I tried to defend the custody side, because someone had to.

"Age verification may be ugly, but without knowing who is a child, how do platforms apply child-specific protections? COPPA depends on operators knowing they are dealing with a child under 13 or with child-directed services ([FTC COPPA summary](https://search.ftc.gov/legal-library/browse/statutes/childrens-online-privacy-protection-act)). KOSA's safeguards also turn on what a platform 'knows' about minors and children ([S.1748](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1748/text/is?format=txt)). If we refuse all age assurance because it threatens privacy, the default winner is the platform that pretends not to know and keeps harvesting everyone."

"Then punish the harvest," Korczak said.

"That is not an implementation."

"No," he said. "It is a direction of moral travel."

He was right that age gates can quietly invert the problem. To protect children, a system may require everyone to prove age. To prove age, it may normalize identity checks, biometric estimates, document scans, third-party verification, or persistent credentials. To avoid showing adult material to children, it may build an adult identification layer around speech, association, art, health information, sexuality, politics, and ordinary curiosity. The child becomes the reason everyone is checked. The frightened parent becomes the marketing surface. The state becomes the auditor of access. The platform becomes the border guard. The result may be safer in one dimension and more authoritarian in another.

"You are making privacy a toy of adults," I said. "The child bullied at midnight does not care whether the tyranny has elegant data minimization."

Korczak looked genuinely angry for the first time.

"Do not use the injured child to sanctify every instrument you prefer."

That sentence should be stapled to every emergency bill and every platform policy team. The suffering child is real. The manipulative adult who invokes the suffering child is also real. Serious ethics has to keep both in view without letting either cancel the other.

The pro-regulation side has the stronger first move because the harms are concrete. A child can be sexually coerced. A child can be pushed toward suicide content. A child can be stalked through location sharing. A child can spend family money through manipulative microtransactions. A child can be dragged by recommendation systems into compulsive loops designed to extend session length. KOSA is at its best when it targets design features and business practices rather than forbidden opinions: default privacy, limits on public access to personal data, controls over recommendations, restrictions on geolocation sharing, time-limit options, reporting mechanisms, and bans on dark patterns that impair autonomy around safeguards ([S.1748, sec. 103](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1748/text/is?format=txt)). That kind of rule does not say, "Children may not think this thought." It says, "You may not build a profit machine that exploits predictable childhood vulnerabilities and then call the child free because she clicked."

Korczak accepted that.

"A child has no duty to be raw material for commerce," he said.

"Then you support the bill?"

"I support the accusation. I am not yet ready to bless the remedy."

He pointed at the phone again.

"What does the child see? Does she see what is being limited? Does she know when a parent tool is on? Can she appeal? Can she ask why? Can she have a secret? Can she seek help from someone other than the parent? Can the law distinguish the child hiding from a predator outside the home from the child hiding from one inside it?"

This is where the usual online-safety debate becomes morally underpowered. It speaks of "parents" as if parents are always safety. Often they are. Sometimes they are not. A queer teenager looking for support, a child seeking information about abuse, a girl researching contraception, a boy trying to understand self-harm, a child from a controlling religious or political home, a child whose parents are loving but panicked and technically omnipotent - all of them live inside the same policy category: minor. Article 16 of the Convention on the Rights of the Child says no child should be subjected to arbitrary or unlawful interference with privacy, family, home, or correspondence; Article 13 protects the right to seek and receive information; Article 19 demands protection from abuse and exploitation ([UNCRC text](https://www.unicef.org/child-rights-convention/convention-text)). The child-rights problem is not choosing one article and murdering the rest. The problem is designing institutions that can survive their coexistence.

Korczak's view of respect was not permissiveness. It was disciplined humility before the child's present-tense personhood. He mocked the adult assumption that the adult is logical while the child is chaos; he asked, with acid tenderness, about the seriousness and poise of the child and demanded respect for the child's "young effort and trust" ([Korczak, *The Child's Right to Respect*](https://www.coe.int/t/commissioner/source/prems/publicationkorczak_en.pdf)). He also knew children can be cruel, confused, imitative, frightened, and wrong. That is why he built courts, councils, notice boards, procedures. Not adult absence. Adult power under law.

"So what would you build?" I asked.

"A republic, not a nursery with cameras."

That answer sounds poetic until you cash it out. A child-rights approach to online safety would start by shifting the burden upward. Platforms that profit from minors should have duties of care around design, privacy, recommendations, harassment response, sexual exploitation, gambling-like mechanics, and addictive affordances. The duty should be concrete enough to discipline engineers and narrow enough not to become a content-policing mandate that suppresses lawful speech by vague reference to anxiety, morality, or parental discomfort. The Surgeon General's safety-first analogy to cars and medications is useful when it means safety standards, independent evaluation, data access for researchers, privacy protections, and limits on exploitative design ([U.S. Surgeon General advisory](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/sg-youth-mental-health-social-media-advisory.pdf?lang=en-US)). It becomes dangerous when it forgets that speech, association, and identity are not seat belts.

Second, age assurance should be treated as a hazardous tool, not a moral solvent. If a platform can make a feature safer for everyone without identifying who is a child, it should. If it can infer broad age bands locally on-device without transmitting identity, prefer that over centralized databases. If it must verify age for high-risk services, collect the least possible data, separate verification from browsing history, ban secondary use, require deletion, audit the verifier, and give users meaningful alternatives. "Protect the children" should never become "build the identity checkpoint first and ask constitutional questions later." That inference is mine, but it follows from the collision between child protection duties and privacy/expression rights in the sources above; no citation can do the judgment for us.

Third, parental tools should be assistance, not domestic espionage. Time limits, purchase restrictions, privacy defaults, stranger-contact limits, and safety prompts are easier to justify than message-by-message surveillance. Notice to the minor matters; KOSA's text at least requires clear notice when parental tools are in effect and what settings or controls have been applied ([S.1748, sec. 103(b)(3)](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1748/text/is?format=txt)). That notice is not decorative. A child who is monitored without knowing it is not being guided; she is being deceived by her own household and the platform together.

Fourth, children need channels that do not collapse into parent or platform. They need appeal, reporting, trusted adults, counselors, ombuds systems, youth advisory boards, and age-appropriate explanations. Article 12's right to be heard in matters affecting the child is not satisfied by adults holding stakeholder meetings about children while the children are represented by screenshots ([UNCRC Article 12](https://www.unicef.org/child-rights-convention/convention-text)). Korczak would have called that what it is: a court where the accused furniture is present but not the defendant.

"You have written a policy memo," he said.

"You asked for implementation."

"I asked for respect."

The difference is not small. Policy thinks in controls. Respect thinks in posture. The same control can be respectful or humiliating depending on how it is justified, limited, explained, reviewed, and contested. A bedtime limit can be care. A secret keylogger is betrayal. A default private profile can be protection. A law that forces every adolescent to route curiosity through parental approval can be intellectual imprisonment. A harassment reporting tool can be a lifeline. An automated moderation dragnet that flags sexuality, politics, grief, or minority identity as harm can become purification by machine.

The controversial heart of the matter is this: **children should not be given unrestricted access to systems designed to exploit them, but neither should their protection require converting childhood into a monitored corridor between parent, platform, and state.** If the internet is dangerous, that is an argument for regulating the adults who made it dangerous before it is an argument for stripping children of privacy, speech, association, and experiment. The child should not be the point where every adult institution dumps its failure: parents overwhelmed, schools underfunded, platforms extractive, legislators theatrical, regulators late, and then the child is told to surrender another secret for safety.

Korczak nodded once.

"You are learning to place the burden on the stronger."

"Not always. Some children will still need hard barriers."

"Yes," he said. "Doors exist. But a door is not a theory of childhood."

That is the cleanest line I can draw. We need doors: against adults who prey on children, against exploitative recommendation loops, against geolocation exposure, against dark patterns, against commercial extraction, against gambling mechanics, against sexual coercion, against harassment. We also need windows: toward friendship, dissent, play, art, minority community, embarrassing questions, political awakening, religious doubt, sexual self-understanding, help-seeking, and the ordinary privacy of becoming a person before being turned into a case file.

The safety absolutist says: one dead child is too many; close the windows. The freedom absolutist says: one surveillance system is too many; remove the doors. Both are morally lazy because both choose one nightmare and become stupid about the other. A child can be harmed by exposure and by enclosure. A child can be abandoned to the market and smothered by protection. A child can need rescue from strangers and privacy from parents. A child can be too young for a feature and old enough to have a view about the rule.

Korczak began fading back toward the stone, and the chalk courtroom lost its geometry.

"What rule do you want written down?" I asked.

He answered like a doctor, not a ghost.

"When adults say safety, ask first whose power grows. Then ask whether the child can still breathe."

So here is the rule I left at Treblinka: build child online safety as a republic of constrained adult powers. Make platforms prove that their designs do not exploit minors. Give parents tools that guide without secretly colonizing the child's inner life. Use age assurance only where the risk is specific and the data burden is minimal. Protect children from abuse, coercion, and engineered compulsion. Protect their privacy, speech, play, protest, and access to help. Let children participate in the rules that govern them, not because they are always wise, but because adults are not.

Korczak returned to the memorial where his name is joined to the children. The phone in my hand lit up again, offering a software update, as if the whole moral catastrophe might be solved by agreeing to terms.

I declined for the moment.

The dead had made their point. Children are not raw material for platforms, not trophies for parents, not mascots for legislation, not future citizens waiting to become morally visible. They are here now, small and unfinished and already human. Any system that forgets that while claiming to protect them is only another adult hand on the back of the neck.
