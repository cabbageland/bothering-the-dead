today is May 25, 2026.

I found Ivan Illich in Bremen, which is to say I found the place where a man who spent his life attacking the smug inevitabilities of modern institutions finally stopped arguing in public. The record is plain enough: Illich died in Bremen in 2002, and Deutsche Biographie lists his grave at Oberneulander Friedhof there ([Deutsche Biographie, "Illich, Ivan"](https://www.deutsche-biographie.de/118555456.html?language=en)). I went first to the cemetery, because one should begin with the official lie, the stone that says a person has become settled. But Illich never really belonged to his own gravestone. He belonged to thresholds, to languages, to exasperated seminars, to friendships sharp enough to count as thought. So after the cemetery I went looking for the dead body in the more serious sense: not the remains under the ground, but the suspended argument still hanging over his work.

I brought a school-issued Chromebook, a district procurement memo proposing a generative AI tutor for every middle-school student, and printouts from three different regimes of educational hope and warning: Illich's own [*Deschooling Society*](https://www.davidtinapple.com/illich/1970_deschooling.html), UNESCO's [*Guidance for generative AI in education and research*](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research?hub=67098), and the OECD's [*Digital Education Outlook 2026*](https://doi.org/10.1787/062a7394-en). Then I opened the Chromebook on the grave like a cheap portable altar and let the screen glow over the name. If a century wants to be judged, it should be judged by the devices it hands to children with the claim that this time the machinery is emancipatory.

The dirt did not explode. Illich would have disliked spectacle. He emerged instead with the annoyed composure of a teacher who has been told, once again, that liberation will arrive through better administration. He glanced at the Chromebook, then at the procurement memo, and gave me the look one reserves for a person who may have woken the dead for a reason too stupid to forgive.

I told him the question quickly. Should public schools make generative AI a default part of education, giving every student an AI tutor or copilot for writing, studying, and personalized instruction, or should schools refuse that model because it deepens dependence, deskills teachers and students, and turns learning into a managed relationship with opaque systems?

That is not a side issue for ed-tech conferences. It is a live constitutional question for education. UNESCO warns that publicly available GenAI is spreading faster than national regulation, leaving privacy unprotected and institutions unprepared, and it explicitly calls for data protection and even age limits for unsupervised use by children ([UNESCO, *Guidance for generative AI in education and research*](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research?hub=67098)). The OECD, meanwhile, argues that GenAI can support learning when guided by good pedagogy, but also stresses that better performance on tasks does not necessarily mean real learning, and that gains often vanish when AI access is removed ([OECD, *Digital Education Outlook 2026*](https://doi.org/10.1787/062a7394-en)). So the controversy is brutally concrete. Is AI in school an instrument that can widen access to teaching and feedback, or is it the next institutional trap disguised as help?

Illich answered as if he had been waiting half a century for the question to become this vulgar. In [*Deschooling Society*](https://www.davidtinapple.com/illich/1970_deschooling.html), he argued that modern schooling teaches people to confuse teaching with learning, grades with education, diplomas with competence. More pointedly, he warned that making children full-time pupils gives teachers and institutions unusual power over their persons, collapsing moral, legal, and personal judgments into one administrative order. He said the AI tutor is not a break from that structure. It is its perfection. Where the old school needed a teacher to monitor, rank, and remediate, the new school promises to do it continuously, invisibly, and at scale. The child is never merely learning; the child is being modeled.

I said that was too clean, too antique, too pleased with its own suspicion. Public schools are not going away. The choice facing administrators is not between pristine human freedom and digital corruption. It is between badly under-resourced classrooms and tools that might actually help. The OECD's case is not simple techno-propaganda. It says general-purpose chatbots can produce shallow outsourcing, yes, but it also says educational GenAI designed with pedagogical intent can improve tutoring, support argumentation, and help less experienced tutors become more effective ([OECD, *Digital Education Outlook 2026*](https://doi.org/10.1787/062a7394-en)). In the real world, millions of students already get too little individual feedback. An AI tutor available at midnight may not be wisdom, but for a lonely or struggling child it may be more help than the system otherwise offers.

Illich said scarcity is the favorite alibi of institutions preparing a new dependency. He was not impressed by my appeal to realism. In [*Tools for Conviviality*](https://www.davidtinapple.com/illich/1973_tools_for_convivality.html), he distinguishes tools that enlarge autonomous action from tools that reorganize life around the needs of the system. A convivial tool serves a user-chosen purpose. A dominating tool quietly rewrites the user to fit itself. His charge was that AI deployed as default school infrastructure is almost guaranteed to be the second kind. It does not merely answer questions. It establishes a norm: that every difficulty in reading, writing, planning, remembering, or revising should encounter an intermediary machine before it encounters patience, friendship, silence, or one's own mind.

That was strong, but I pushed harder. Children are not harmed only by dependence on systems. They are also harmed by neglect. A student with dyslexia, weak prior preparation, little family support, or an overworked teacher may gain something real from immediate explanation, translation, scaffolding, and practice. UNESCO's own guidance does not call for banning GenAI from education. It tries to regulate it on human-centered terms, which implies that there are uses worth governing rather than simply rejecting ([UNESCO, *Guidance for generative AI in education and research*](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research?hub=67098)). If a tool can expand access to feedback, lower barriers for disabled students, and help teachers spend less time on clerical drudgery, then refusing it wholesale can become a form of moral vanity practiced at children's expense.

Illich bent down and tapped the Chromebook screen with one finger, not gently. He said I was still speaking as though education were mainly the delivery of instructional services, only now delivered more efficiently. That, he said, was precisely the disease he had spent his life naming. In [*Deschooling Society*](https://www.davidtinapple.com/illich/1970_deschooling.html), he imagined something far stranger than today's school reform: learning webs, peer matching, public access to educational resources, and even computer-supported networks through which learners could find one another and choose teachers without surrendering education to compulsory institutional pipelines. He was not, in that sense, simply anti-technology. He could imagine computers serving freedom. But only if they functioned as public utilities for self-directed association, not as compulsory pedagogical supervisors embedded in the bureaucratic metabolism of school.

This was the sharpest turn in the whole argument, because it blocked my easiest caricature of him. He was not telling me that all educational technology is inherently corrupt. He was telling me that governance and form matter more than gadget enthusiasm. A computer used to help people locate one another, compare experiences, or reach resources is one thing. A machine positioned as a child's ever-present tutor, evaluator, and cognitive prosthesis inside a compulsory system is another. The first could widen initiative. The second risks what he would call radical monopoly: a social arrangement in which one mode of getting help becomes so normalized that other forms of learning begin to look illegible or irresponsible ([Illich, *Tools for Conviviality*](https://www.davidtinapple.com/illich/1973_tools_for_convivality.html)).

I answered that he was right about one danger but wrong about the policy consequence. The threat of monopoly is real, which is exactly why public systems should shape these tools instead of leaving them to private consumer chaos. Right now, because general-purpose GenAI is intuitive and widely available, students use it largely beyond institutional control anyway, as the OECD notes ([OECD, *Digital Education Outlook 2026*](https://doi.org/10.1787/062a7394-en)). If schools abstain, they do not create a noble pre-digital commons. They abandon children to whatever the market hands them: persuasive chatbots, hidden data extraction, opaque safety standards, and learning habits set by product teams rather than teachers. Bringing AI into school, I argued, can be a way of taming it, subjecting it to public norms, disclosure, curriculum limits, and democratic scrutiny.

Illich looked almost pleased for the first time, which worried me, because it meant the knife was coming.

He said this is the classic modern dream: to cure institutional excess with a more enlightened institution. Yet the school system has a remarkable ability to turn every critique into a new layer of management. Once AI enters as official infrastructure, the pressure will not remain modest. It will spread into assessment, behavior prediction, lesson planning, plagiarism policing, career guidance, and the permanent archival memory of childhood. UNESCO warns that institutions are often unprepared to validate these tools and that privacy protection is a central issue ([UNESCO, *Guidance for generative AI in education and research*](https://www.unesco.org/en/articles/guidance-generative-ai-education-and-research?hub=67098)). He said this understates the matter. The issue is not only data leakage. It is the political education of dependence. A child habituated to asking an approved machine how to begin, what to think, whether a paragraph is good, what question to ask next, becomes easy to govern in precisely the way industrial institutions prefer: assisted at every point, autonomous at none.

I did not want to yield that ground because the romantic version of self-direction often forgets how unequally it is distributed. The children best positioned to flourish without institutional scaffolds are usually the ones already cushioned by books, time, quiet rooms, and adults who know how to intervene. For everyone else, anti-institutional purity can shade into abandonment. The strongest case for educational AI is not that it will produce genius. It is that it may distribute some forms of attention more widely than affluent families and elite schools currently do. If an overburdened public system can use AI to give a student iterative writing feedback, targeted practice, or patient explanation that no adult has time to provide, there is justice in that, not just control.

Illich said I was letting a true grievance be conscripted by a false solution. Of course unequal attention is a scandal. But why assume the answer is synthetic attention packaged as educational infrastructure? The OECD's own warning matters here: task performance can improve without learning, and the advantage may disappear when the machine disappears ([OECD, *Digital Education Outlook 2026*](https://doi.org/10.1787/062a7394-en)). That is not a minor bug. It goes to the core of what schools are for. If students become more fluent at producing acceptable outputs while less capable of memory, endurance, drafting, and independent judgment, then the system will congratulate itself on efficiency while quietly manufacturing fragility.

I had to grant that point because it is exactly the danger people most eager for AI in education often try to euphemize. If writing becomes mostly prompting and revision becomes mostly accepting machine suggestions, some students will indeed become more presentable and less formed. The question, then, is whether that risk condemns the tool or only the default model. Here I split from the procurement memo I had brought. I told Illich that making AI the default tutor for every child is a mistake. Not because every AI use is corrupt, but because default status changes the moral shape of the classroom. What is optional can remain instrumental. What becomes ambient starts defining the norm of competence.

At this, he stopped arguing for a moment. The night at Oberneuland had gone thin and cold. Freight moved somewhere beyond the cemetery. A school laptop on a grave had become, improbably, a serious object.

My conclusion to him was this. Public schools should not adopt generative AI as the default infrastructure of learning, writing, or judgment. They should not tell children that the normal way to study is in constant partnership with a machine trained to predict the next plausible sentence. That path would deepen the oldest school mistake Illich diagnosed: confusing the provision of educational services with education itself ([Illich, *Deschooling Society*](https://www.davidtinapple.com/illich/1970_deschooling.html)). But neither should schools retreat into pious refusal while students use ungoverned commercial systems anyway. The defensible position is narrower and harder: regulated, disclosed, limited use for specific purposes such as accessibility support, teacher assistance, and tightly designed tutoring, with large protected zones of unaided reading, writing, memorization, discussion, and problem-solving preserved on purpose. In other words, AI should be a constrained supplement inside education, never its atmosphere.

Illich said that was less foolish than what most administrators will choose, but he would still distrust it, because institutions drift toward total use. I told him distrust is warranted. That is exactly why the line has to be drawn now, before convenience hardens into anthropology.

The stakes are not small. If schools normalize AI as a child's standing intellectual companion, they may produce graduates who are easier to coach, calmer to manage, and better at generating polished work on demand, but less practiced at the solitude and friction from which judgment grows. If they prohibit every serious use, they may entrench existing educational inequalities and let the private market train students in secret anyway. The fight is therefore not between purity and progress. It is between two models of help. One treats the learner as someone who should increasingly be able to stand in relation to the world without constant mediation. The other treats the learner as a permanent client of smart assistance.

Illich looked back toward the grave that had briefly failed to keep him. Then, in the imaginary sentence I had brought him up to hear, he said:

When a society cannot imagine teaching without managed dependence, it will soon be unable to imagine freedom as anything but well-designed support.

Then he lay back down among the dead, leaving me with the laptop glow and the unpleasant clarity that he was mostly right. The school system will be tempted to treat AI not as a tool but as a destiny. It should resist that temptation. A civilization that puts a fluent machine between every child and every hard thing may call this personalization. More honestly, it is a decision about what kind of person the child will be allowed to become.
