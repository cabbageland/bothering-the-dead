today is 07-04.

I found Leo Szilard where a grave becomes an argument with geography.

The National Park Service gives the hard coordinates first: born in Budapest on February 11, 1898, died in San Diego on May 30, 1964, chief physicist at Chicago's Metallurgical Laboratory, place of burial: cremated ([National Park Service, "Manhattan Project Scientists: Leo Szilard"](https://www.nps.gov/people/manhattan-project-scientists-leo-szilard.htm)). A conventional grave would almost have been vulgar for him. Szilard was one of those minds whose real remains are distributed through institutions, policies, nightmares, patents, committees, and sentences nobody can unread.

So I did not go looking for a neat stone. I made a grave from the correct debris: a block of graphite, a hotel key from an imaginary La Jolla room, a subway map of London with one red mark at a traffic light, a Chicago Pile-1 diagram, a July 1945 petition, a copy of the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons, one cheap American flag, and a firework with the fuse soaked through.

Szilard is said to have conceived the nuclear chain reaction in London in 1933, after leaving Germany as Hitler rose; the Atomic Heritage Foundation profile follows him from Budapest to Berlin to England to Columbia and then to the Chicago work that helped make the first self-sustaining chain reaction possible ([Atomic Heritage Foundation, "Leo Szilard"](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/profile/leo-szilard/)). In 1939 he helped persuade Einstein to warn Roosevelt that uranium might become a source of enormous energy and possibly bombs; the Einstein-Szilard letter warned that extremely powerful bombs might be constructed and urged contact between the U.S. government and physicists working on chain reactions ([Einstein-Szilard Letter, Atomic Heritage Foundation](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/key-documents/einstein-szilard-letter/); [DOE/OSTI Manhattan Project history](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1939-1942/einstein_letter.htm)). Then, after Germany was defeated, Szilard helped make the opposite motion: the Franck Report warned that nuclear bombs could not remain an exclusive American secret and that first use against Japan would likely precipitate an arms race ([The Franck Report, Atomic Heritage Foundation](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/key-documents/franck-report/)). His own petition to Truman tried to force moral deliberation before the bomb was used; the National Security Archive says Truman never saw it ([National Security Archive, Szilard petition](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/28456-document-38-e-lapp-leo-szilard-et-al-petition-president-united-states-july-17-1945)).

To wake him, I did not chant. I put the graphite in the center, folded the petition around it, laid the wet firework beside the flag, and read aloud the line in the Franck Report that treated the arms race not as melodrama but as predictable political chemistry: reveal the bomb by destroying a city, and distrust will breed weapons. Then I read the modern inventory: the Federation of American Scientists estimates that at the beginning of 2026 the world had roughly 12,187 nuclear warheads, about 9,745 of them in military stockpiles and about 3,912 deployed with operational forces ([Federation of American Scientists, "Status of World Nuclear Forces"](https://fas.org/initiative/status-world-nuclear-forces/)). SIPRI's 2026 Yearbook release gives the same total and says 2,100 to 2,200 deployed warheads were on high operational alert on ballistic missiles ([SIPRI Yearbook 2026 press release](https://www.sipri.org/media/press-release/2026/increasing-focus-nuclear-weapons-amid-heightened-escalation-risks-new-sipri-yearbook-out-now)).

The soaked firework hissed, although it did not burn. The graphite warmed. The flag stirred without wind.

Szilard sat up from the improvised grave with the impatient expression of a man who had already written the memo and had no confidence anyone had read past page one.

"You brought me fireworks," he said.

"It is the Fourth of July."

"An annual celebration of combustion under constitutional cover."

"More or less."

"And what is the question?"

The question was this: **Should nuclear-armed democracies continue to rely on nuclear deterrence as a tragic necessity, or is deterrence itself a standing moral and political corruption that must be dismantled rather than managed?**

This is not a dorm-room puzzle about whether bombs are bad. The stakes are cities, launch warning systems, alliances, dictatorships, parliaments, submarines under black water, technicians in silos, children taught to call terror "stability," and whole populations made into bargaining chips without ever signing the bargain. It is also about whether an abolitionist who cannot describe the danger of unilateral disarmament is serious, and whether a deterrence theorist who cannot describe the evil inside his own doctrine is sane.

Szilard brushed ash from his sleeve.

"Begin with deterrence," he said. "Moral vanity is fastest when it begins with condemnation."

Fine. The case for nuclear deterrence begins with the fact that physics does not have an undo button. Once a chain reaction is known, the universe does not forget it for us. Szilard's own life is the exhibit. He did not warn Roosevelt because he loved militarized science. He warned Roosevelt because he feared Nazi Germany might reach the weapon first; the DOE history describes him, Teller, and Wigner as seeing it as their responsibility to alert Americans to the possibility of a German atomic bomb ([DOE/OSTI Manhattan Project history](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/manhattan-project-history/Events/1939-1942/einstein_letter.htm)). In that original moral atmosphere, the argument was not "bombs are noble." It was "if a murderous regime gets them alone, the world may become hostage to it."

"Correct," Szilard said. "Fear can be intelligent."

"Fear can also become an institution."

"Continue."

The serious deterrence argument says that nuclear weapons are politically disgusting but strategically stabilizing under certain conditions. If an adversary knows that nuclear attack will bring unbearable retaliation, it may refrain from attack. If allies believe they are protected by a nuclear umbrella, they may not build their own nuclear arsenals. The U.S. Nuclear Posture Review makes this case in official language: American nuclear weapons deter strategic attacks, assure allies and partners, and would be available if deterrence fails; it also says extended deterrence helps non-proliferation by giving allies confidence that they can stay secure without acquiring nuclear weapons of their own ([2022 Nuclear Posture Review](https://media.defense.gov/2022/Oct/27/2003103845/-1/-1/1/2022-NATIONAL-DEFENSE-STRATEGY-NPR-MDR.pdf)).

This is the cleanest pro-deterrence claim: a nuclear arsenal can be a bad thing that prevents worse things. It may keep nuclear-armed states from launching wars directly against each other. It may make conquest too dangerous. It may prevent a nervous ally from deciding that the only trustworthy shield is a domestic bomb factory. It may give diplomacy the time that panic would otherwise consume.

Szilard nodded, not happily.

"You must add verification," he said. "No sane man abolishes a minefield by announcing that his side has become pure."

Yes. Disarmament is not a sermon; it is engineering, inspection, sequencing, and adversarial trust built one measurable constraint at a time. The NPT bargain itself reflects that structure: prevent spread, permit peaceful nuclear energy under safeguards, and pursue nuclear disarmament. Article VI commits parties to pursue good-faith negotiations on ending the arms race and nuclear disarmament ([United Nations NPT text](https://www.un.org/en/conf/npt/2005/npttreaty.html); [IAEA overview of the NPT](https://www.iaea.org/topics/non-proliferation-treaty)). The same U.S. review that defends nuclear deterrence also says mutual, verifiable arms control is the most durable path to reducing the role of nuclear weapons ([2022 Nuclear Posture Review](https://media.defense.gov/2022/Oct/27/2003103845/-1/-1/1/2022-NATIONAL-DEFENSE-STRATEGY-NPR-MDR.pdf)).

That matters. The person who says "abolish them tomorrow" has to answer the tyrant, the cheater, the hidden enrichment plant, the missile tunnel, the state that signs smilingly and lies professionally. Nuclear weapons cannot be wished out of arsenals by moral clarity alone. The world contains governments that would happily pocket another state's virtue and convert it into leverage.

"Good," Szilard said. "Now do not become the lawyer for cowardice."

Here is where deterrence begins to rot.

Deterrence is usually described as restraint, but its engine is threat. To deter nuclear attack, a state must make its enemy believe that under some conditions it is willing to do something almost unspeakable. The defender of deterrence wants credit for the war that did not happen, but the price of that non-event is a permanent readiness to make catastrophe plausible. It is not enough to own the weapons. The adversary has to believe the weapons might be used.

"A bluff can deter," Szilard said.

"Only if it looks enough like a plan."

"Yes. That is the poison."

The poison is moral, legal, and democratic at once. The Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons bans not only possession and use among its parties, but also threatening to use nuclear weapons ([TPNW text, ICRC treaty database](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/assets/treaties/640-TPNW-EN.pdf)). Nuclear-armed states and their allies largely reject that treaty as unrealistic, and realism is not always wrong. But the treaty sees something deterrence language tries to domesticate: a threat to use nuclear weapons is not a ghostly abstraction. It is part of the weapon.

The International Court of Justice's 1996 advisory opinion did not give abolitionists the clean victory many wanted. It found no comprehensive universal prohibition of nuclear weapons as such, but it also held that nuclear threat or use would generally be contrary to humanitarian law, while leaving unresolved the extreme case of self-defense where a state's survival was at stake ([ICJ, *Legality of the Threat or Use of Nuclear Weapons*](https://www.icj-cij.org/case/95)). That ambiguity is often treated as room for policy. It should also be treated as a moral alarm. If the best legal description of your civilization's ultimate security practice is a split, haunted, edge-case answer about possible state survival, perhaps you have not found a stable foundation. Perhaps you are standing on a trapdoor with excellent lawyers.

Szilard picked up the wet firework and sniffed it.

"Americans like founding documents," he said. "Use yours."

So I did. The Declaration of Independence says governments derive "their just powers from the consent of the governed" and are instituted to secure life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness ([National Archives, Declaration of Independence transcript](https://www.archives.gov/milestone-documents/declaration-of-independence)). Nuclear deterrence is a hard test of that sentence. Who consented to be the retaliatory target in another state's strategy? Who consented to have their city made credible as a possible ash heap? Who consented to a launch architecture in which minutes, sensors, submarines, satellites, codes, and human judgment are asked to hold back the end of ordinary time?

"Citizens consent through representatives," Szilard said.

"Do they consent to what they cannot know?"

"They consent imperfectly to many things."

"Taxes are imperfect consent. So are prisons. Nuclear deterrence is different in scale."

"Scale changes ethics?"

"Scale reveals ethics. A mosquito and a guillotine are both sharp, but one teaches political philosophy faster."

He almost smiled. Almost.

The democratic problem is that nuclear policy is wrapped in secrecy by design. Some secrecy is necessary; a public target list would be madness wearing transparency's hat. But secrecy creates a strange republic in which the deepest wager is made by a narrow priesthood of executives, generals, laboratories, contractors, and strategists, while the public is invited to call the arrangement security. The governed live inside the blast radius of decisions they cannot meaningfully inspect.

The deterrence theorist replies that democracy is not a suicide pact and that strategic secrecy can protect the public. True. But a democracy should still be allergic to a doctrine that converts civilians into implicit hostages and then congratulates itself for mature restraint. If nuclear threats work partly because enemy civilians, cities, and regimes are placed under catastrophic risk, then deterrence is not merely a shield. It is a reciprocal hostage system with patriotic vocabulary.

"Too simple," Szilard said.

"Which part?"

"You say hostage. The defender says prevented war. A hostage who is not killed may still live."

"A hostage who survives is not evidence that hostage-taking is justice."

"But it may be evidence that one madman did not shoot another."

This is why the topic is hard. Deterrence may be morally grotesque and historically useful. Both can be true. The twentieth century did not end in a U.S.-Soviet nuclear exchange. That fact is immense. But it is not a proof of righteousness. It may be proof of prudence, fear, luck, arms control, command discipline, geography, exhaustion, economic interdependence, spies, weather, malfunction averted, individual restraint at frightening moments, or the mere fact that no crisis found the exact crack in the system.

The trouble with nuclear deterrence is that it has no harmless failure mode. A bridge that fails teaches engineers by collapsing one span. A medical trial that fails can be stopped. A deterrence system that fails may erase the people who would have written the postmortem. Calling the system successful because catastrophe has not occurred is like calling yourself immortal because you woke up this morning.

Szilard looked down at his own resurrected hands.

"Careful. By that standard almost all safety systems are suspect."

"No. Ordinary safety systems reduce the chance of harm without requiring a credible intention to commit mass killing."

"Firebreaks are not innocent. Quarantines are not innocent. War itself is not innocent."

"I agree. But nuclear deterrence makes annihilation a standing instrument of policy."

He accepted that sentence, which did not mean he liked it.

The abolitionist case gets stronger in 2026 because the old story of shrinking arsenals is no longer enough. FAS says global inventory has declined from Cold War peaks, but military stockpiles available for use are increasing again; SIPRI says nuclear-armed states continued modernizing and enhancing arsenals in 2025, with most deploying new nuclear-armed or nuclear-capable systems ([FAS, "Status of World Nuclear Forces"](https://fas.org/initiative/status-world-nuclear-forces/); [SIPRI Yearbook 2026 press release](https://www.sipri.org/media/press-release/2026/increasing-focus-nuclear-weapons-amid-heightened-escalation-risks-new-sipri-yearbook-out-now)). Deterrence presented as a temporary evil keeps renewing the lease. Every modernization is described as necessary for stability. Every adversary program justifies one's own. Every new weapon is the regrettable but responsible answer to someone else's regrettable but responsible answer. The arms race does not need villains if it has enough professionals.

"That is why we wrote the Franck Report," Szilard said.

"You predicted the race."

"We predicted human beings."

The Franck Report's force was not that scientists had suddenly become priests of peace. It was that people inside the machinery saw the likely machinery after it. A secret weapon would not remain secret. A first strike by a country that wanted postwar moral authority would seed fear, imitation, and national programs. The report argued for a demonstration before use against Japan because political legitimacy and international control mattered before the first city was destroyed ([The Franck Report](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/key-documents/franck-report/)). It was not innocence speaking. It was technical imagination forced to become political.

That is Szilard's real haunt. He is not useful as a simple anti-bomb saint. Saints are too clean for this subject. Szilard helped push the first warning because Hitler existed. Then he tried to slow the use of the weapon because Germany had fallen and the moral situation had changed. He understood something fanatics and bureaucrats both hate: the same action can be prudent in one strategic moment and monstrous in the next. You do not get to freeze your first fear into permanent doctrine.

"Say that again," he said.

"You do not get to freeze your first fear into permanent doctrine."

"Good. Now apply it."

The first fear was nuclear monopoly by a murderous state. The permanent doctrine became nuclear possession by many states, justified forever by the possibility that someone else might be worse. Deterrence began as emergency logic and matured into architecture. That is the corruption. A democracy can perhaps tolerate emergency nuclear deterrence while it is working visibly and urgently toward verified abolition. It cannot honestly baptize permanent deterrence as peace.

The pro-deterrence side objects: the world is not ready. Russia threatens. China expands. North Korea exists. India and Pakistan face each other with history between them. Israel is opaque. Alliances are strained. Missile defenses, cyber operations, anti-satellite weapons, hypersonic systems, and artificial intelligence may compress decision time. Even the 2022 NPR says emerging cyber and space capabilities create complex escalation pathways and new stresses on stability ([2022 Nuclear Posture Review](https://media.defense.gov/2022/Oct/27/2003103845/-1/-1/1/2022-NATIONAL-DEFENSE-STRATEGY-NPR-MDR.pdf)). In such a world, unilateral abolition by one state could be less a moral victory than an invitation to coercion.

I do not dismiss that. A serious abolitionist cannot pretend the last nuclear warhead disappears into a folk song. Verification has to be intrusive. Conventional imbalances matter. Cheating has to be detectable. Withdrawal has to be costly. Civil nuclear programs require safeguards. Regional conflicts need settlement. Missile defenses and precision conventional weapons have to be considered because states do not only fear nuclear weapons; they fear defeat, decapitation, humiliation, invasion, and regime death.

"You sound almost reasonable," Szilard said.

"I apologize."

"Do not. Reason is unfashionable only because it is expensive."

But the deterrence defender also owes answers. If deterrence is temporary, what timeline and policies make that visible? If nuclear weapons exist only to deter nuclear attack, why retain doctrines allowing use in some non-nuclear strategic contingencies? If no nuclear war can be won and must never be fought, why keep weapons on high alert? If extended deterrence reduces allied proliferation, what non-nuclear guarantees and regional arms-control measures could let allies rely less on nuclear promises? If arms control is the durable path, why does every breakdown become an excuse for modernization but not for diplomatic desperation? If civilians are not to be targeted, why does so much public nuclear discourse still lean on the psychological terror of city destruction?

Szilard put the petition in his lap.

"The terrible thing about politics," he said, "is that both fools and cowards can use complexity as shelter."

"And clarity?"

"Clarity can be a rifle in the hands of a child."

There it was: the whole argument, small enough to fit under the wet firework.

My answer is this: **nuclear deterrence may be defensible only as an emergency bridge to verified disarmament, never as a permanent moral settlement.** A nuclear-armed democracy should not be asked to abolish its arsenal tomorrow regardless of adversaries, verification, alliance commitments, and strategic conditions. But it should be judged harshly if every year it calls deterrence necessary while making necessity more permanent.

That means the burden of proof belongs on the possessor. Keep a nuclear force only under a doctrine of reduction, restraint, and self-liquidation. Reduce the role of nuclear weapons in national strategy. Narrow declaratory policy toward sole-purpose or no-first-use unless a government can publicly justify why the remaining ambiguity is necessary. Take warheads off hair-trigger alert where that can be done without increasing first-strike incentives. Pursue mutual, verifiable reductions with the obsession usually reserved for procurement. Strengthen negative security assurances to non-nuclear NPT-compliant states. Stop treating every new adversary capability as a blank check for new nuclear missions. Fund verification science as seriously as warhead life extension. Treat the TPNW not as a naive embarrassment but as a moral indictment, even if one disputes its strategic sufficiency ([TPNW text](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/assets/treaties/640-TPNW-EN.pdf); [2022 Nuclear Posture Review](https://media.defense.gov/2022/Oct/27/2003103845/-1/-1/1/2022-NATIONAL-DEFENSE-STRATEGY-NPR-MDR.pdf)).

Most importantly, tell the truth about the doctrine. Do not call it peace. Call it a threat system that may, under current conditions, be less dangerous than sudden disarmament, but more dangerous than a world that has done the political and technical work to escape it. Do not let schoolchildren inherit mushroom clouds as a normal branch of civics. Do not let strategists talk about "exchange" as if they are pricing citrus. Do not let the word stability become a pillow over conscience.

"And if abolition fails?" Szilard asked.

"Then deterrence remains, but ashamed."

"Shame is not policy."

"No. But a policy without shame can become a religion."

He seemed to like that less than he should have, perhaps because he knew religions are good at surviving evidence.

The Fourth of July kept happening around us. Somewhere outside the essay, people were buying fireworks, grilling meat, arguing over flags, wearing paper hats, misquoting founders, loving their children, and believing in freedom with the usual American mixture of grandeur and amnesia. The Declaration's language about life and consent does not settle nuclear policy by itself. But it does accuse any democracy that makes human survival depend forever on secret plans for irreversible violence.

Independence should mean, at minimum, not being permanently governed by the fears of 1939.

That does not mean forgetting 1939. Szilard would not allow that. Hitler was real. The race was real. The physics was real. The letter to Roosevelt was not a childish act. But fear has a half-life only if we refuse to keep enriching it. Otherwise it becomes weapons-grade tradition.

The wet firework finally split down the side. No flame, only a smell of powder and rain.

"You have written a tolerable answer," Szilard said.

"Tolerable?"

"On this subject, excellent answers are suspicious."

"What would you write?"

He touched the petition. "I would write that every chain reaction needs a moderator."

"Graphite?"

"Conscience. Verification. Humor, where possible. Institutions, if we are unlucky enough to need them."

"And if the reaction has already begun?"

"Then you insert the rods before congratulating yourself on the heat."

The improvised grave cooled. The graphite returned to being only carbon. The flag lay still. Szilard began to thin, not into peace exactly, but into that sterner condition where the dead have done all the warning they can and the living are out of excuses.

Before he vanished, he looked at the firework.

"Do not confuse a fuse with a birthday candle," he said.

Then he was ash again, or memory, or distributed responsibility. I left the petition where his chest had been and carried the graphite away.

All night the sky would bloom with harmless imitations of terrible light. Children would cheer. Windows would tremble. Adults would look up and call the explosions beautiful because they ended quickly and belonged to celebration.

That is the difference. A firework is allowed to be beautiful because it has no doctrine for tomorrow.
