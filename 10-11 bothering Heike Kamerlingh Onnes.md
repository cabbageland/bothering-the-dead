today is 10-11.

I found Heike Kamerlingh Onnes at the Dorpskerk graveyard in Voorschoten, where the cemetery guide lists him plainly among the buried, grave number 50, professor, Leiden University, maker of liquid helium and pioneer of strange conductance ([Kerkhof Dorpskerk Voorschoten, "For our English guest"](https://kerkhof-dorpskerk-voorschoten.nl/for-our-english-guest/)). It was the kind of graveyard that does not dramatize itself. The stones were not trying to be Gothic. The grass was not theatrical. The air had the clean Dutch talent for making metaphysics feel administratively registered.

I had brought a small brass thermometer, a coil of copper wire, a cracked magnet, a page from a 2023 arXiv preprint, and a silvered dewar that contained no helium, only the idea of helium. This is usually enough for resurrection if the dead person was an experimental physicist and the living person is willing to be ridiculous with sufficient precision.

Onnes had earned the 1913 Nobel Prize in Physics for work on the properties of matter at low temperatures, work that included the liquefaction of helium; Nobel's account notes that in 1908 he cooled helium to liquid form and that superconductivity followed from that cold kingdom ([Nobel Prize, "Heike Kamerlingh Onnes - Facts"](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1913/onnes/facts/)). In April 1911, measuring mercury at helium temperatures, Onnes and Gilles Holst saw its electrical resistance fall to practically zero around a few kelvin, which is not a poetic statement but one of the times nature changed costume in front of a careful instrument ([Heike Kamerlingh Onnes Prize, "About Heike Kamerlingh Onnes"](https://kamerlingh-onnes-prize.ch/about-heike-kamerlingh-onnes/)).

To wake him, I laid the copper coil over the grave and held the magnet above it.

"Door meten tot weten," I said.

Through measurement to knowledge. The phrase comes from Onnes's 1882 inaugural lecture at Leiden, where he made quantitative experiment sound less like bookkeeping and more like a moral discipline ([NIST translation, "Through Measurement to Knowledge"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4861352/)).

The thermometer fell below zero without changing temperature. The magnet rose an inch, not levitating exactly, but refusing ordinary manners. Frost formed on the arXiv page and crept over the abstract first, because even necromancy has editorial judgment. Then the grave opened with the dry click of an apparatus being unlocked.

Onnes sat up, brushed soil from his sleeves, and looked at the magnet with professional suspicion.

"That is not a measurement," he said.

"It is a provocation."

"Those are cheaper."

Good. Awake.

The controversial topic was waiting for him like a lab bench no one had cleaned: **should modern physics, especially materials physics, keep publicly amplifying spectacular claims of room-temperature superconductivity before independent replication, or has the search for the miracle material become a factory for hype, retractions, public confusion, and corrupted incentives?**

This is not a small argument about press-release etiquette. Superconductors are not just fancy metals with better manners. In a superconductor, electrical resistance drops to zero, and magnetic fields are expelled in the Meissner effect; CERN's account puts those two facts at the heart of the phenomenon ([CERN, "Superconductivity"](https://home.cern/science/physics/superconductivity/)). DOE's summary stresses why people care: zero resistance in superconducting wires permits very high currents, though only under critical limits, and superconductivity already underwrites powerful magnets and quantum technologies ([DOE Explains, "Superconductivity"](https://www.energy.gov/science/doe-explainssuperconductivity)). A superconductor that worked at ordinary temperature and pressure would not merely be a nice sample in a cold lab. It would be a claim on the imagination of energy, transportation, medicine, computing, and every headline writer who has ever wanted a laboratory to save civilization by Friday.

"So," Onnes said, "you have come to scold desire."

"I have come to scold the way desire borrows the lab coat of evidence."

"Begin precisely."

Fine.

The accusation is that superconductivity has become one of the places where physics lets hope outrun its own instruments. In 2020, a Nature paper reported room-temperature superconductivity in a carbonaceous sulfur hydride under enormous pressure; Nature later retracted the article, noting that concerns over data processing and interpretation undermined confidence in the result ([Nature, "Retraction Note: Room-temperature superconductivity in a carbonaceous sulfur hydride"](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-022-05294-9)). In 2023, another Nature paper claimed evidence for near-ambient superconductivity in nitrogen-doped lutetium hydride, reporting a transition near room temperature at far lower pressure; that paper was also retracted ([Nature, "Retraction Note: Evidence of near-ambient superconductivity in a N-doped lutetium hydride"](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06774-2)). Also in 2023, LK-99 preprints claimed a room-temperature, ambient-pressure superconductor with a transition temperature above 400 kelvin ([Lee et al., "The First Room-Temperature Ambient-Pressure Superconductor"](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.12008)); within weeks, Nature reported that replication work had traced the excitement to ordinary material behavior rather than superconductivity ([Nature, "LK-99 isn't a superconductor - how science sleuths solved the mystery"](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-023-02585-7)).

"There," I said. "Three bonfires in the same temple."

"You are confusing a bonfire with a furnace."

"People ran around saying the future had arrived."

"People often run. It is not always the fault of physics."

"Convenient."

He looked almost offended, which is a risky expression for a resurrected man whose whole career had depended on liquid helium not behaving conveniently.

"No," he said. "Not convenient. Necessary. You must distinguish the claim, the evidence, the journal, the press office, the investor, the social machine, and the replication. If you put them all into one bucket and call it science, you have done philosophy with a mop."

That was the first cut, and annoyingly fair.

The easy anti-hype position treats failed claims as proof that the field has become unserious. This is too quick. LK-99, in one sense, is exactly what the scientific immune system looks like when it is awake: a public claim, a worldwide scramble, synthesis attempts, magnetic tests, electrical measurements, theoretical scrutiny, and then a fast demotion from miracle to interesting mistake. The National Academies report on reproducibility and replicability insists that replication is not mere clerical duplication but part of how science strengthens or revises the web of knowledge ([National Academies, "Reproducibility and Replicability in Science," Executive Summary](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK547535/)). A failed replication is not the opposite of science. It is one of science's teeth.

"Then you admit my point," Onnes said.

"I admit part of it. A tooth is good. Biting the public repeatedly and calling it nutrition is not."

He smiled at that. The dead enjoy a decent insult because nothing in the graveyard needs protecting from embarrassment.

The physics itself explains the hunger. Superconductivity feels like nature briefly abolishing friction's moral authority. A normal wire wastes energy as heat; a superconducting current can persist without ordinary electrical dissipation. A magnet hovers. A field is expelled. The material enters a collective quantum state that makes the everyday world look underachieving. Even the language is suspiciously religious: critical temperature, transition, phase, perfect conductor, expulsion. The dream of room-temperature superconductivity is the dream that the miracle stops requiring a cryogenic monastery.

"You hear yourself," Onnes said. "Miracle, monastery. You accuse others of romance while bringing incense to the apparatus."

"Physics deserves romance. Not fake invoices."

"Good. Then defend the difference."

The difference is measurement disciplined by consequences. Onnes's motto was not "through wanting to knowing." It was not "through trending to knowing." It was not "through a photograph of a floating crumb to knowing." It was measurement: apparatus, calibration, repetition, error, cold, patience, and the willingness to let the needle humiliate the theory. The scandal in spectacular materials claims is not that scientists desire a transformative discovery. Desire is part of discovery's metabolism. The scandal is when the culture around science learns to convert desire into premature certainty because certainty travels faster than care.

This matters because room-temperature superconductivity is not a private hallucination. It is a civilization-scale promise. Nature's own discussion of LK-99 emphasized why a true ambient superconductor would be consequential across electricity grids, magnets, transport, and scientific instruments ([Nature, "How would room-temperature superconductors change science?"](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-023-02681-8)). Once a claim carries that scale of promise, it starts moving money, attention, trust, political fantasy, and public emotion. It becomes less like an ordinary paper and more like a weather event in the economy of hope.

"You want a special epistemology for interesting things," Onnes said.

"I want a special caution for claims that arrive wearing a crown."

"That is not absurd. But be careful. Many discoveries look absurd before they look obvious."

He had the right to say it. A Dutch laboratory cooling matter toward absolute zero at the beginning of the twentieth century was already a kind of magnificent excess. Liquid helium was not produced by a bored man with a spoon. It required engineering, institutional support, skilled labor, instruments, and the stubborn ambition to make nature speak under conditions nature rarely bothers to provide on Earth. If every committee had demanded immediate practical payoff and no tolerance for strangeness, the coldest room in Leiden would have remained warm and sensible and barren.

"So," he said, "should I have waited for independent replication before telling anyone mercury had lost its resistance?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because the claim was tied to a visible measurement, a specific material, a specific apparatus, and a community that could inspect the problem. Because you were not selling stock in the future."

"A low blow."

"A measured one."

"Not measured enough. Ambition also needs patrons. Patrons like futures."

Second cut. He was right again, but only partly.

Science has always depended on stories about the future. The telescope, the accelerator, the cryogenic lab, the space mission, the genome sequencer: none are funded by pure humility. They are funded by a disciplined extravagance, a persuasive account of why a civilization should spend scarce attention on a question whose answer may be useless, transformative, or both in an order nobody predicted. The moral crime is not telling a public story. The moral crime is letting the story start impersonating the evidence.

Onnes reached for the cracked magnet. It snapped into his palm and left a little ring of frost on his skin.

"What do you want done?" he asked.

"No press release for a miracle without independent confirmation."

"Too blunt."

"No journal prestige for a claim whose raw evidential structure cannot be inspected."

"Better."

"No headline saying 'discovery' when the honest word is 'claim.'"

"Better still."

"No public promise of world-changing applications from a sample that has not survived hostile replication."

"Now you are measuring."

The distinction matters. Preprints should exist. A field benefits when strange results circulate fast enough for other labs to attack them. The LK-99 episode, for all its absurd internet carnival, showed that a distributed scientific community can mobilize quickly when a claim is concrete enough to test. But speed is not the same virtue in every institution. A preprint server, a journal, a university press office, a venture-backed company, a national newspaper, and a physics department seminar do not have identical duties. The same sentence can be exploration in one place and misinformation in another.

APS ethics standards say that fabrication and falsification damage public trust and diminish science's value, while also distinguishing misconduct from honest error and interpretive disagreement ([American Physical Society, "Ethics Standards"](https://www.aps.org/about/governance/policies-procedures/ethics-standards)). That distinction is essential. If every wrong superconductivity claim is treated as fraud, science becomes a courtroom with better cryostats. If every spectacular failure is treated as harmless enthusiasm, science becomes advertising with equations. The hard work is keeping error possible and hype accountable.

"You dislike the middle because it is difficult to tweet," Onnes said.

"The middle is where institutions go to avoid decision."

"Sometimes. It is also where reality often lives."

Third cut.

The prosecution has one more argument, and it is the sharpest. Hype taxes the honest. Every exaggerated claim steals time from careful labs asked to reproduce it, steals trust from future claims, steals public patience from slower programs, and steals status from researchers who say boring true things. A field where the loudest promise wins attention teaches young scientists that careful uncertainty is a career handicap. It tells them to choose the grandest verb. It tells them that "may suggest" is for cowards, "revolutionary" is for grants, and "world-changing" is for the homepage.

Onnes did not dismiss this. The frost on the grave thickened.

"A laboratory has a temperature," he said. "Not only physical. Moral. Below one temperature, patience condenses. Above another, vanity becomes vapor."

"That is good."

"I have been dead long enough to prepare one aphorism."

I let him have it.

The modern scientific attention system runs hot. Preprints move faster than peer review. Social media moves faster than preprints. Markets move faster than social media. Journal brands move slower than all three but amplify harder when they finally act. Retractions, by contrast, feel like afterthoughts, even when they are the record correcting itself. The public remembers the miracle more vividly than the correction because miracle has better lighting.

"Then improve the lighting on correction," Onnes said.

"You want retractions celebrated?"

"I want them made legible. A retraction is not always a funeral. Sometimes it is a cleaning."

"And sometimes it is the discovery that someone sold contaminated glass as diamond."

"Yes. Then say that. But do not teach the public that correction means science is uniquely rotten. Teach them that uncorrected certainty is worse."

There it was, the defense at its strongest. Science should not pretend to be a cathedral of stainless facts lowered from heaven. It should show its machinery: the draft, the doubt, the replication, the failed synthesis, the suspicious graph, the reanalysis, the expression of concern, the retraction, the revised model. Public trust built on the fantasy of infallibility is brittle. Public trust built on visible correction is less glamorous, but it can bend.

Still, Onnes's defense needed discipline too. "Self-correction" can become a laundering word. Institutions sometimes use it to make damage sound automatic, as if science corrects itself the way a thermostat corrects temperature. It does not. People correct science. They spend money, time, reputation, and sometimes career safety doing it. They request data. They reproduce methods. They challenge friends. They withstand harassment from believers in the miracle. They become, in the heroic imagination, less attractive than the original claimant because correction has the narrative shape of janitorial work.

"You are too cruel to janitors," Onnes said.

"No. I am saying janitors keep civilization alive."

"Then honor them as experimentalists."

That was the line that moved the argument. Replication is not lesser science. It is science in its most adult form: refusing to confuse novelty with truth. The person who proves a claimed room-temperature superconductor is only a magnetic impurity has not merely spoiled a party. They have protected the word superconductor from inflation. They have kept the miracle meaningful by preventing counterfeit miracles from occupying its name.

The cracked magnet hovered again, then dropped. Even ghosts obeyed boundary conditions.

"Tell me your rule," Onnes said.

"All right. Spectacular physics claims should carry a hype surcharge."

"Define it."

"The larger the promised consequence, the stronger the obligation to publish inspectable evidence, preserve raw data, describe sample preparation honestly, share analysis choices, invite hostile replication, and restrain institutional language until independent groups can test the core claim. A paper may speculate. A press office should not hallucinate certainty. A journal may publish a risky result if the evidence is inspectable and the uncertainty explicit. A university should not turn every anomaly into a civilization event. Reporters should say 'claimed' until the field earns 'found.'"

"And the scientist?"

"The scientist should want to be right less than they want the phenomenon to be real."

Onnes was silent for a while. That is how I knew the sentence had made it past rhetoric and into the apparatus.

Finally he said, "Good. But add one condition."

"Which?"

"Do not punish wonder."

The graveyard seemed to lower in temperature around that. Not physically. Better: intellectually.

Physics without wonder becomes compliance paperwork for matter. It loses the courage to ask ridiculous questions with expensive instruments. It forgets that mercury dropping to zero resistance was not common sense but a small impossibility turned actual. The point is not to make superconductivity boring until it is safe. The point is to keep wonder attached to reality rather than to projection. Wonder should sharpen measurement, not replace it.

"That is the corruption," Onnes said. "Not wonder. Wonder without obedience to what is measured."

"Obedience is an ugly word."

"It is beautiful in a laboratory. The apparatus is allowed to say no."

He was right. The apparatus is a democratic tyrant. It does not care about seniority, market cap, nationalism, institutional brand, or the emotional needs of the funding cycle. It will not make a material superconducting because the headline has already been scheduled. It will not keep a resistance curve honest because a famous journal printed it on expensive paper. It will not expel magnetic flux for the sake of a TED talk. The apparatus is where desire goes to be educated.

So the verdict is not cynicism. Cynicism is lazy heat. The verdict is colder and kinder:

Keep hunting for room-temperature superconductors. Hunt seriously. Hunt with risky chemistry, strange pressures, ugly samples, theory, simulation, powder, diamond anvils, levitation videos, skepticism, and joy. Publish preprints when there is enough there for others to test. But stop selling preliminary anomalies as if they have already signed a treaty with the future. Make replication prestigious. Make correction visible. Make raw data boringly available. Make press releases allergic to the word "breakthrough" until independent evidence has done some bruising work. Teach the public that science's glory is not that it never falls for a beautiful possibility, but that its best practitioners build instruments sharp enough to wound their own wishes.

Onnes began to sink back into the grave. The frost retreated from the arXiv page. The copper coil warmed. The magnet stopped performing symbolic labor and became again a magnet, which is one of the mercies of the physical world.

"One more thing," he said.

"Yes?"

"Do not say the failed claims prove the dream is foolish. A room-temperature superconductor may yet exist."

"And if it does?"

"Then it will not arrive because people believed hard enough. It will arrive because someone measured well enough."

The grave closed. The night returned to ordinary conductivity. I picked up the thermometer, the coil, the preprint page, and the cracked magnet. Nothing levitated. Nothing glowed. Nothing had been saved.

But the world was still full of materials whose electrons, under the right conditions, stop behaving like lonely commuters and enter a collective state so strange that resistance vanishes and magnetic fields are turned away at the door. That is worth celebrating. Not because it makes technology promises easy, but because it reminds us that matter keeps hidden rooms. Physics is the craft of finding them without lying about which door has opened.

I left Voorschoten before dawn with one sentence cold enough to keep:

Wonder is not the enemy of rigor. Wonder is what rigor is for.
