today is May 12, 2026.

I found Hannah Arendt where Europe stores its embarrassments, which is to say in a cemetery neat enough to imply moral order and old enough to know better. Bard College keeps her papers in boxes and climate control, but the body itself is in Annandale-on-Hudson, under a stone that does not advertise how much twentieth-century argument is compressed beneath it. I went there after midnight with a spade, a cheap lantern, a copy of *The Human Condition*, and a small quadcopter whose camera blinked red like an animal trying to impersonate conscience. The drone mattered. I had not come merely to exhume a thinker. I had come to ask whether democratic states should ever permit lethal autonomous weapons systems, machines that can select and engage targets without a human making the final individual firing decision. The question has become less science fiction than procurement dispute. The United States Department of Defense does not categorically ban autonomy in weapon systems; its directive instead regulates levels of review and design for such systems ([U.S. Department of Defense, Directive 3000.09, updated 2023](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodd/300009p.pdf)). The International Committee of the Red Cross has called for new legally binding rules because systems that target humans without meaningful human control threaten humanitarian law and basic ethical limits ([ICRC, *Autonomous Weapon Systems*, 2021](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/autonomous-weapon-systems-technical-military-legal-and-humanitarian-aspects)). The U.N. Secretary-General has said machines with the power and discretion to take human life are “politically unacceptable” and “morally repugnant” ([United Nations, Secretary-General’s remarks on priorities for 2024](https://www.un.org/sg/en/content/sg/statement/2024-02-07/secretary-generals-remarks-the-general-assembly-priorities-2024-bilingual-delivered-scroll-down-for-all-english-version)).

I set the drone on her grave and let its rotors idle. Then I read aloud a line from Arendt that still has teeth: “The sad truth is that most evil is done by people who never make up their minds to be good or evil” ([Hannah Arendt, *The Life of the Mind*, vol. 1, *Thinking*](https://archive.org/details/lifeofmind0000aren)). The drone rose three feet, then dropped. The soil shivered. A hand came through first, annoyed rather than dramatic, as if burial had been an administrative inconvenience. Arendt pulled herself up in a dark coat slick with dirt, looked at the machine, looked at me, and said, “If you have revived me for a panel discussion, I will go back down.”

I promised her something better than a panel. I told her I wanted an argument. Not about whether war is ugly, which is obvious, but about whether a state that calls itself civilized may delegate lethal targeting to a machine if the machine is faster, calmer, and in some circumstances more accurate than frightened human beings. Militaries and technology companies keep pressing toward exactly that horizon. Autonomy already exists in missile defense and loitering systems of various kinds, though definitions are contested and often conveniently vague ([U.N. Office for Disarmament Affairs, background on lethal autonomous weapons systems](https://disarmament.unoda.org/the-convention-on-certain-conventional-weapons/background-on-laws-in-the-ccw/)). The controversy is no longer whether autonomy will appear in war. It is whether the line between assisting a human decision and replacing it at the point of killing can be crossed without rotting the political and moral structure that supposedly justifies the use of force at all.

She brushed mud from her sleeves with the irritability of the recently resurrected. “Very well,” she said. “You are asking whether murder may be made more efficient by making judgment more absent.”

I told her that was one answer, but also perhaps too quick. The strongest case for permitting lethal autonomous weapons is not bloodlust. It is an argument from military necessity and harm reduction. In modern combat, especially against missiles, swarms, or attacks unfolding faster than human reaction time, speed matters. Defensive automated systems like the U.S. Navy’s Aegis Combat System and Phalanx Close-In Weapon System were developed precisely because a human operator may simply be too slow against incoming threats ([U.S. Navy, facts on Aegis Weapon System](https://www.navy.mil/Resources/Fact-Files/Display-FactFiles/Article/2169787/aegis-weapon-system/); [U.S. Navy, facts on Phalanx CIWS](https://www.navy.mil/Resources/Fact-Files/Display-FactFiles/Article/2169791/phalanx-close-in-weapons-system-ciws/)). More broadly, some scholars argue that properly designed autonomous systems could in theory outperform human soldiers at distinction and proportionality because machines do not panic, seek revenge, or fire wildly out of fear ([Robert Sparrow, “Twenty Seconds to Comply,” *International Relations* 21, no. 4, 2007](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0047117807083375) summarizes and contests part of this optimistic line; [Ronald C. Arkin, *Governing Lethal Behavior in Autonomous Robots*, 2009](https://books.google.com/books?id=qqbQvDEFU0UC)). If the relevant comparison is not between perfect humans and imperfect machines but between exhausted nineteen-year-olds and software constrained by rules, perhaps refusing autonomy could itself cost civilian lives.

Arendt listened without interruption, which is always more menacing than argument. Then she asked a question that sounded small and was not. “Who,” she said, “is the one who judges?”

I answered in the language engineers and lawyers often use when they want to sound responsible. The system does not float free. Humans design the model, set the target profile, define the operating environment, choose whether to activate it, and remain accountable through command responsibility and weapons review. Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions requires states to determine whether new weapons would be prohibited by international law ([ICRC database, Additional Protocol I, Article 36](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/api-1977/article-36)). The Pentagon directive requires senior review for autonomous and semi-autonomous weapon systems and says such systems must allow commanders and operators to exercise appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force ([DoD Directive 3000.09](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodd/300009p.pdf)). On this view, the human judgment is not absent, only relocated upstream.

“That,” she said, “is precisely what should frighten you.”

We began walking between the graves. She spoke without hurry, as if the whole century were still available. In *Eichmann in Jerusalem*, she had described not a demon of extravagant wickedness but an administrator marked by a terrifying inability or refusal to think from the standpoint of others ([Hannah Arendt, *Eichmann in Jerusalem*, Penguin page](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/160866/eichmann-in-jerusalem-by-hannah-arendt/)). The phrase “the banality of evil” has been vulgarized into meaning that evil is ordinary. Her actual target was more exacting. It was thoughtlessness, the evacuation of judgment under bureaucratic conditions. A system of autonomous killing, she said, threatens to industrialize that evacuation. The commander can say the machine made the classification. The engineer can say the commander selected the mission. The politician can say the lawyers approved the architecture. Each person performs a function. No one stands where the dead person stood.

I pushed back. Bureaucratic diffusion of responsibility did not begin with machine learning. Artillery officers, bomber crews, and drone chains already kill at distance through layered institutions. A pilot releasing a guided bomb based on sensor fusion is not face to face with the victim either. If the moral problem is distance, modern war crossed that threshold long ago. To single out autonomy can become mystification, as if a human clicking “approve” after glancing at a box on a screen restores deep moral presence. Some ethicists therefore argue that “meaningful human control” is too vague to do real regulatory work unless it is cashed out in operational terms ([ICRC, *Autonomous Weapon Systems*](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/autonomous-weapon-systems-technical-military-legal-and-humanitarian-aspects); [Heather M. Roff, “Meaningful Human Control, Artificial Intelligence and Autonomous Weapons,” article page at *Ethics & International Affairs*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ethics-and-international-affairs/article/meaningful-human-control-artificial-intelligence-and-autonomous-weapons/)). A tired officer rubber-stamping a target recommendation in two seconds may be a theater prop, not a moral safeguard.

Arendt stopped at another stone and turned on me with almost cheerful severity. “Now you are getting somewhere,” she said. “The issue is not touching the trigger. The issue is whether a human being is still engaged in judging what is being done in his name.” In *Responsibility and Judgment*, she insisted that moral responsibility does not disappear just because one acts inside a system with rules, roles, and precedents ([Hannah Arendt, *Responsibility and Judgment*, Schocken page](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/186783/responsibility-and-judgment-by-hannah-arendt/)). Yet autonomous weapons, she argued, are uniquely dangerous because they promise officials a fantasy they have wanted for a long time, namely violence without the ordeal of decision. Not merely cleaner killing, but decisionless killing.

I said the fantasy cuts both ways. There is also the fantasy of the irreplaceable human conscience, which history gives us little reason to romanticize. Humans commit atrocities for ideology, fear, racism, boredom, careerism, and group loyalty. Machines have none of those motives. The law of armed conflict does not ask combatants to feel the weight of mortality in a deep existential register. It asks them, among other things, to distinguish military objectives from civilians and to avoid attacks expected to cause excessive civilian harm relative to anticipated military advantage ([ICRC, Customary IHL rules on distinction and proportionality](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/customary-ihl/v1)). If a future system could perform those functions more reliably than a human soldier, why should sentimentality about human agency outweigh the lives saved?

She answered by attacking the hidden premise. The problem, she said, is that target selection is not merely a classification task. It is a worldly judgment thick with context, uncertainty, and contestable interpretation. Is the man carrying a rifle a combatant, a guard, a policeman, or a farmer in a place where everyone carries rifles? Is the truck a military supply vehicle or the only way a family moves water? What counts as a hostile pattern of life, and who decided the pattern? Even the data categories are already political sediment. Machine systems do not approach war from nowhere. They are trained on human labels, sensor biases, operational doctrines, and historical patterns that can encode prior misrecognitions at scale. The National Institute of Standards and Technology has repeatedly documented uneven performance and demographic differentials in some AI systems, a reminder that “automation” often launders historically situated error into technical procedure ([NIST, *Face Recognition Vendor Test* demographic effects summary](https://www.nist.gov/programs-projects/face-recognition-vendor-test-frvt)). Although weapon targeting is not face recognition in a shopping mall, the larger epistemic point carries: computational confidence is not the same thing as truth.

I agreed with the epistemic danger, but insisted there are domains where the context is narrow enough to make autonomy morally defensible. Consider missile defense at sea. A supersonic incoming missile offers seconds, not minutes. There may be no civilian ambiguity at all, only the question of whether a defensive system intercepts in time. Even many critics of autonomous weapons make room for automated defensive systems in constrained environments, which is one reason treaty talks keep stumbling on definitions ([U.N. CCW meeting materials and background](https://disarmament.unoda.org/the-convention-on-certain-conventional-weapons/)). If autonomy is already accepted in such contexts, then the absolute ban position begins to look less like principle than category panic.

Arendt conceded the narrow case more readily than I expected. “A shield is not the same as a hunter,” she said. That distinction mattered. A system designed to intercept incoming munitions in a tightly bounded defensive setting is not morally identical to a roaming system that identifies and kills human beings based on signatures or profiles. But she would not let the concession expand. The line, she argued, must be drawn where the object of machine selection is a human target. Once the state permits a machine to determine that this person, here, now, should die, even within parameters set by humans, it has crossed from mechanizing force to mechanizing judgment.

I told her the hard question is whether that line is philosophically deep or psychologically comforting. Suppose a commander authorizes an autonomous system to patrol a clearly defined desert kill zone for enemy vehicles after civilians have been evacuated, communications are jammed, and any delay would expose friendly forces to immediate attack. If the system identifies a vehicle matching the rules and fires, did the machine really make the judgment, or did the commander make it in advance under conditions where conditional delegation was rational? Human beings delegate lethal decisions to subordinates all the time. A general does not personally approve every shot.

Arendt answered by insisting that delegation among persons is not the same as delegation to a process. A subordinate can in principle refuse, reinterpret, disobey, or answer back. A machine cannot appear before others as a bearer of judgment. It cannot be ashamed, persuaded, held answerable in the full political sense, or called to account by the dead. Here she sounded close to arguments later made by critics like Sparrow, who has claimed there is an accountability gap when autonomous systems cause wrongful deaths because no one involved neatly fits the role of the culpable agent ([Robert Sparrow, “Killer Robots,” *Journal of Applied Philosophy* 24, no. 1, 2007](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1468-5930.2007.00346.x)). Other scholars contest the gap by arguing responsibility can still be distributed across commanders, programmers, manufacturers, and states. But Arendt’s point was not only juridical. It was political. A republic teaches itself what a human life is worth partly through the rituals and burdens it attaches to killing. Remove those burdens too thoroughly and you do not merely change weapons. You educate citizens into a new relation to death.

That struck me as true, but still incomplete. Wars are not philosophy seminars, and states have obligations to protect their own soldiers as well as civilians. If autonomy can reduce friendly casualties, leaders will use it. They may also have a duty to use it. The U.S. military’s own AI strategy frames autonomy partly in terms of speed, decision advantage, and protection in contested environments ([U.S. Department of Defense, *Data, Analytics, and Artificial Intelligence Adoption Strategy*, 2023](https://media.defense.gov/2023/Nov/02/2003333300/-1/-1/1/DOD_DATA_ANALYTICS_AI_ADOPTION_STRATEGY.PDF)). To tell a platoon or a ship crew that they must forgo an available system that would likely save them because philosophers are worried about the dignity of judgment is not obviously noble. It may be a blood sacrifice to one’s own moral self-image.

Arendt gave me the coldest look of the night. “Every empire,” she said, “calls its conscience a luxury when the bill comes due.” She did not deny the protective duty. She denied that it is unlimited. States are constrained not only by what secures victory but by what forms of power they are willing to normalize. Liberal societies already prohibit certain means of war even when they might confer advantage. Chemical weapons are not forbidden because they are ineffective but because their mode of violence breaches a line the law decided to mark ([Organisation for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons, Chemical Weapons Convention overview](https://www.opcw.org/chemical-weapons-convention)). A ban on anti-personnel autonomous weapons, she argued, would belong to the same moral grammar. Not because all automation is evil, but because allowing software-mediated human hunting would alter the conditions under which political communities imagine permissible killing.

By then the argument had narrowed into its real form. The pro-autonomy side says the morality of weapons should be judged chiefly by effects: accuracy, civilian protection, force protection, deterrence, and legal compliance. If machines can produce better effects than humans in some lethal contexts, refusing them becomes fetishism about agency. The anti-autonomy side says some decisions are constitutive, not merely instrumental. Who or what is allowed to identify a human being for death is part of the moral substance of the act, not a decorative feature around its consequences.

I told Arendt I thought many people evade this by pretending the issue is further away than it is. It is not. States are investing heavily in military AI, autonomy, and human-machine teaming. The legal regime remains thin, the incentives strong, and the euphemisms plentiful ([Stockholm International Peace Research Institute, publications on autonomy in weapons systems](https://www.sipri.org/topics/emerging-military-and-security-technologies/autonomy-artificial-intelligence-and-robotics)). Once such systems promise tactical advantage, restraint becomes a unilateral temptation few militaries enjoy. That is exactly why the issue matters now, before standards are set by whatever survives first contact with battlefield procurement.

She nodded, finally, as if the resurrection had almost justified itself. “Then write this carefully,” she said. “Your century is very talented at discussing technology in a way that removes politics from it. But every machine arrives inside a world of institutions, permissions, evasions, and ambitions. If you ask whether the machine can kill, you have already asked too little. Ask what sort of people wish to become governable by such killing.”

The eastern sky had begun to pale. I knew she would not stay. Before she returned to the ground, I asked for a verdict plain enough that no ministry could hide inside it.

She gave one. Defensive automated systems aimed at incoming munitions in tightly constrained environments may be tolerable, because they mechanize interception rather than the political judgment that a human being is targetable. But systems that select and attack human beings, or that identify targets from signatures and patterns without a contemporaneous human judgment answerable for that specific act, should be prohibited by international law. Not merely regulated, not merely reviewed, prohibited.

I buried her again at sunrise, which felt less like desecration than compliance.

My own conclusion is close to hers, though for a less metaphysical reason. Democratic states should support a binding international ban on lethal autonomous weapons systems that select and attack human targets without meaningful, situation-specific human judgment, while permitting narrowly bounded automated defensive systems against materiel threats like incoming missiles. I do not think this follows because human beings are morally beautiful. History has cured that illusion. I think it follows because the act of designating a person to die is one of the places where law, politics, and moral responsibility must remain stubbornly, visibly human. Consequences matter enormously, and any serious policy must confront the possibility that some forms of automation reduce harm in narrow settings. But once killing becomes a sufficiently abstracted output of sensing, modeling, and threshold rules, the political community that authorizes it also becomes abstract to itself. It begins to experience death as a systems problem.

That is the real stake. Not whether the machine has a soul. It does not. The stake is whether we are building institutions that let human beings slowly misplace their own.
