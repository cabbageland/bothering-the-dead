today is 10-12.

I found Sadi Carnot in the old communal cemetery of Ivry-sur-Seine, not in the Pantheon, not under a marble national allegory with trumpets and laurel, but in the more honest district of weather, stone, and municipal neglect. The French heritage database lists the "Tombeau de Leonard Sadi Carnot" in Ivry-sur-Seine, dated 1832, made of stone, and, with a small bureaucratic cruelty that felt physically colder than the air, notes its condition as "mauvais etat" ([POP, "Tombeau de Leonard Sadi Carnot"](https://pop.culture.gouv.fr/notice/merimee/IA00130011)). A 2026 heritage program for the old cemetery calls out the grave of Leonard Sadi Carnot, "physicien francais createur de la thermodynamique," as one of the high points of the visit ([Journees europeennes du patrimoine, Ivry-sur-Seine cemetery visit](https://journeesdupatrimoine.culture.gouv.fr/w/391796/evenement/19938869/visite-de-lancien-cimetiere-communal-divry-sur-seine)).

That was already an argument. One of the founders of thermodynamics rests in a damaged tomb, while the world he helped explain burns fuel, cools chips, spins turbines, trains models, heats atmospheres, and calls the whole ordeal progress if the dashboard is attractive enough.

I brought him four offerings: a chipped thermometer, a cup of meltwater, a black square of coal, and a dead GPU whose fan still contained a faint gray felt of dust. I placed the thermometer on the stone. I set the coal on one side and the meltwater on the other. I laid the GPU between them like a modern sacrificial animal, all copper traces and exhausted appetite.

Then I said the word most likely to annoy a thermodynamic ghost into returning:

"Efficiency."

The thermometer rose and fell at the same time. The meltwater froze in a ring around the cup and boiled in the center, which is either a miracle or a very rude phase diagram. The GPU fan twitched once, as if dreaming of invoices. From inside the tomb came a sound like a piston changing its mind.

Carnot sat up through the stone, thin, lucid, and offended by the imprecision of being dead.

"You have resurrected me," he said, "with a broken engine."

"A data-center engine."

"All engines are data-center engines if the accountant is sufficiently confused."

Good. Awake.

Sadi Carnot published only one book in his lifetime, the 1824 *Reflexions sur la puissance motrice du feu*, which opens by insisting that heat can be the cause of motion and that steam engines were already transforming mines, ships, ports, iron, textiles, transport, and the industrial arts ([Carnot, *Reflexions sur la puissance motrice du feu*](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/R%C3%A9flexions_sur_la_puissance_motrice_du_feu)). The book asked whether the motive power of heat had limits, whether improvement could go on indefinitely, and how a theory of heat engines might be made general rather than tied to one machine or working substance ([Carnot, *Reflexions*](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/R%C3%A9flexions_sur_la_puissance_motrice_du_feu)). Later historians can argue over the subtleties of caloric theory and energy conservation, but the physics lineage is not in doubt: Carnot treated the heat engine as an object governed by limits, reservoirs, cycles, reversibility, and the temperature difference between hot and cold bodies; modern accounts rightly call him a founder of the second law of thermodynamics ([Herman Erlichson, "Sadi Carnot, 'Founder of the Second Law of Thermodynamics'"](https://geosci.uchicago.edu/~moyer/GEOS24705/Readings/Carnot_article_1998.pdf)).

The controversial topic had been waiting between the coal and the GPU: **should civilization trust efficiency to save us from the energy appetite of artificial intelligence and other computation, or is efficiency becoming the beautiful excuse by which we make demand grow faster than conscience?**

This is not a tasteful dispute about server rooms. The International Energy Agency estimated that data centers used about 415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024, around 1.5% of global electricity consumption, and projected that data-center electricity use would more than double to about 945 TWh by 2030 in its base case, with AI the most important driver of growth ([IEA, "Energy demand from AI"](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/energy-demand-from-ai)). In 2026, the IEA reported that data-center electricity demand had risen 17% in 2025, that AI-focused data centers grew faster still, and that per-task energy use was falling rapidly even as total use kept rising because more people and more energy-intensive applications were being added ([IEA, "Data centre electricity use surged in 2025"](https://www.iea.org/news/data-centre-electricity-use-surged-in-2025-even-with-tightening-bottlenecks-driving-a-scramble-for-solutions)). In the United States, the EIA said the strongest four-year electricity-demand growth since 2000 was being fueled largely by large computing facilities including data centers ([EIA press release, January 2026 STEO](https://www.eia.gov/pressroom/releases/press582.php)).

"So," I said, "the machines are becoming more efficient and hungrier."

"That is not a contradiction," Carnot said. "It is the history of industry."

"I know. That is why I bothered you."

He lifted the dead GPU and examined it with the calm horror of a man who had seen civilization discover heat and decide to invoice the planet for every possible use of it.

"Begin with your accusation," he said.

Fine.

The accusation is that the modern technology industry has learned to speak of efficiency as if it were absolution. The chips will improve. The models will be distilled. The cooling systems will become smarter. The data centers will sign power-purchase agreements and shift loads and use liquid cooling and maybe nuclear power will arrive wearing a venture-backed cape. Every unit of computation will require less energy, and therefore, apparently, nobody has to ask what all the computation is for.

This is the old spell, updated from coal smoke to machine learning. William Stanley Jevons saw the trap in 1865. In *The Coal Question*, he argued that economy in the use of coal could increase, not decrease, coal consumption, because cheaper power expands the field of profitable uses; he wrote that the "very economy" of coal use led to more extensive consumption and that the industrial system had grown from successive measures of economy ([Jevons, *The Coal Question*, chapter 7](https://www.econlib.org/library/YPDBooks/Jevons/jvnCQ.html?chapter_num=9)). That is the Jevons paradox in its coal-black suit: make the engine better, lower the effective price of power, and the world invents more reasons to use power.

"A paradox," Carnot said, "is often a lazy name for an omitted variable."

"The omitted variable is appetite."

"Then do not blame efficiency for appetite. Blame appetite."

First cut, clean and deserved.

He was right that efficiency is not the villain. Waste is not moral purity. A bad engine does not become virtuous because it squanders coal with tragic sincerity. A hot server room does not become humane because its inefficiency expresses restraint. Carnot's whole intellectual beauty lies in refusing magical thinking about machines. Heat does not become work because we want it to. There are limits. There are temperatures. There are losses. There are arrangements that approach the maximum and arrangements that throw possibility into the sink. In Carnot's own comparison, the motive power of heat depends on something like the height of a fall, the temperature difference across which heat passes ([Carnot, *Reflexions*](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/R%C3%A9flexions_sur_la_puissance_motrice_du_feu)). A civilization that ignores efficiency is not innocent. It is sloppy at planetary scale.

"Good," Carnot said. "Keep that sentence."

"Do not become my editor. You are here as a witness."

"In thermodynamics, the witness is always editing."

The defense of efficiency is strong. The IEA says accelerating energy-efficiency improvements can deliver more than a third of CO2 emission reductions between now and 2030 in a pathway aligned with net zero by 2050, and that improvements in energy intensity from 2010 to 2022 avoided almost 7 gigatonnes of cumulative global CO2 emissions ([IEA, "Energy Efficiency 2024: Executive summary"](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-efficiency-2024/executive-summary)). Its policy toolkit says the biggest efficiency gains come from packages combining regulation, information, and incentives, not from motivational posters about thrift ([IEA, "The Energy Efficiency Policy Package"](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-efficiency-policy-toolkit/the-energy-efficiency-policy-package)). Efficiency, properly governed, is not a con. It is one of the few ways to reduce energy services into less damage.

"There," Carnot said. "You have acquitted my word."

"No. I have separated the word from its public-relations department."

He smiled. The dead enjoy a good distinction.

The problem is that efficiency has two lives. In physics, it is a relation between useful output and input. In politics, it is a permission structure. The first life is precise; the second is slippery. A more efficient engine can mean less fuel for the same work. It can also mean more work for the same money. A more efficient AI model can mean lower emissions for a given service. It can also mean that the service is embedded into every search result, office suite, toy, ad auction, surveillance product, inbox, dating app, school assignment, customer-service labyrinth, and refrigerator with delusions of personality.

"You are angry at proliferation," Carnot said.

"Yes."

"Then say proliferation. Do not hide it behind watts."

So: proliferation.

The argument is not that AI computation is categorically worthless. That would be too easy, and false. Computation can help model proteins, control grids, translate languages, optimize logistics, detect faults, assist disabled people, accelerate science, and perform many other tasks whose value is not imaginary. The argument is that once intelligence-like service becomes cheap at the margin, markets will not automatically reserve it for meaningful uses. They will put it wherever it can extract attention, reduce labor costs, increase switching friction, generate synthetic abundance, or make a customer wait while a chatbot apologizes in a tone selected by committee. Efficiency lowers the gate. It does not decide who walks through.

Carnot touched the coal. It became warm, then cold, then merely symbolic, which is the least useful state for coal.

"You want an energy morality for computation," he said.

"I want the physical costs of computation to become visible before they become destiny."

"Visibility is not morality."

"No. But invisibility is where moral idiocy breeds."

Second cut. Mine, this time.

The language of "the cloud" has trained people to imagine computation as vapor without chimney, thought without body, capacity without siting, intelligence without transformers, substations, water systems, backup generators, land, permitting, transmission queues, or neighborhoods that suddenly discover that their electricity future has been optioned by a server farm. The EIA's 2026 analysis warned that faster-than-expected data-center demand could raise fossil generation and wholesale power prices in some regions; in one high-demand scenario, extra load would be met largely by natural gas and, in some regions, coal capacity that had spare room to run ([EIA, "Fossil generation could rise with faster-than-expected growth in data center power demand"](https://www.eia.gov/TodayinEnergy/detail.php?id=67344)). The cloud is not made of vapor. It is made of load.

Carnot looked almost pleased by that. "A load is honest. It can be measured."

"And billed."

"Billing is one of your species' inferior forms of measurement."

Fair.

The physics of computation itself offers no metaphysical escape hatch. Rolf Landauer's 1961 paper argued that logically irreversible operations in computing are associated with physical irreversibility and a minimum heat generation on the order of kT per irreversible function ([Landauer, "Irreversibility and Heat Generation in the Computing Process"](https://www.dna.caltech.edu/courses/cs191/paperscs191/landauer1961.pdf)). Real machines operate far above that limit, and engineers have enormous room to improve. But Landauer's lesson is still the right insult to digital mysticism: information is physical. Erasure is not a literary act. A bit has a thermodynamic shadow. A model response that appears on a glass rectangle belongs to the same universe as coal, turbines, heat sinks, temperature gradients, and pipes.

"At last," Carnot said, "you have stopped treating computation as theology."

"The investors will be disappointed."

"They often are. It is one of the ways reality remains cheerful."

The counterargument matters, and I let him make it properly. Efficiency can mean access. It can mean the student without a tutor gets help. It can mean the small laboratory uses a model that once required a giant computing budget. It can mean hospitals, researchers, engineers, translators, farmers, and public agencies use tools that were previously confined to rich firms. The IEA's work on rebound effects explicitly notes that rebound is not always a bad outcome: if efficiency makes energy services affordable enough to improve health, comfort, productivity, or poverty reduction, some "take-back" can represent welfare rather than waste ([IEA, *Capturing the Multiple Benefits of Energy Efficiency*](https://iea.blob.core.windows.net/assets/28f84ed8-4101-4e95-ae51-9536b6436f14/Multiple_Benefits_of_Energy_Efficiency-148x199.pdf)). The poor heating their homes because insulation made warmth affordable are not a Jevons punchline. They are the moral reason efficiency exists.

"Good," Carnot said. "Your theory now contains humans."

"Barely. I am working on it."

This is where the argument gets dangerous. If we say rebound is always waste, we become aristocrats of deprivation. If we say rebound is always welfare, we become sales representatives for appetite. The difference is not visible from the watt-hour alone. A watt-hour spent keeping a premature infant alive, simulating a climate adaptation plan, translating a legal notice, generating ten thousand spam images, or making a shopping app flirt with you has the same unit but not the same moral content. Physics can measure the energy. It cannot by itself rank the use.

"You want the second law to do ethics," Carnot said.

"I want it to prevent ethics from lying."

That stopped him for a moment.

The second law cannot tell us whether an AI model should summarize medical records, produce personalized propaganda, or replace a teacher with a synthetic patience machine. But it can forbid one specific lie: that digital work floats outside material consequence. It can remind us that every useful process has waste, every engine has a sink, every data center rejects heat, every chip has a temperature, every demand forecast presses on a grid built by people who did not necessarily consent to being the basement of someone else's product roadmap. Thermodynamics does not decide values. It disciplines fantasy.

"That is acceptable," Carnot said.

"Only acceptable?"

"I am French and dead. Take what you can get."

The live policy question is concrete: **should governments and grid operators treat AI data centers as ordinary customers whose demand should be met if they can pay, or as strategically significant loads that must satisfy stricter public conditions before they receive scarce grid capacity?**

The ordinary-customer answer has one virtue: it is simple. If a firm pays for power, builds interconnection, and complies with law, it gets service. Let prices signal scarcity. Let efficiency improve. Let clean generation expand. Let technological progress do its peculiar sideways miracles. Do not place bureaucrats in charge of deciding which computation is noble. Do not slow a potentially transformative general-purpose technology because today's uses include a great deal of automated confetti.

Carnot liked parts of that answer.

"You should be suspicious of committees that claim to know in advance which engines matter," he said. "Many useful engines begin as absurdities."

"Yes."

"And scarcity can become an aesthetic pose among people whose own needs are already supplied."

"Also yes."

"Then your demand for restraint may be a luxury argument."

Third cut. Ugly because it was not wrong.

There is a cheap anti-AI energy argument that treats all new load as moral failure while leaving older luxuries unexamined. It is satisfying and unserious. A society full of air-conditioned mansions, private jets, bitcoin mines, empty offices, oversized vehicles, and disposable consumer junk should not suddenly discover austerity only when the new machine starts doing prose. If the moral rule is "new energy demand must justify itself," then it should apply broadly, not merely to the fashionable target. The climate does not care which sector provides the hypocrisy.

But the opposite argument is worse. "Everything else is wasteful too" is not permission to add another uncontrolled appetite. It is an indictment of the whole menu. And AI data centers are not just another toaster. They are large, concentrated, fast-growing loads arriving in grids whose planning timelines are slower than the industry's capital cycle. The IEA notes that grid connections and critical components such as transformers and cables have long lead times, and that around 20% of planned data-center projects could risk delays unless grid risks are addressed ([IEA, "Energy and AI: Executive summary"](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai)). When a new load can shape generation, local prices, transmission planning, land use, water stress, and fossil-plant utilization, public conditions are not an intrusion into the market. They are the honest name for consequences already being socialized.

"Now define the conditions," Carnot said.

"Gladly."

First: measure. Large AI data centers should disclose enough standardized energy and emissions information for the public to know the scale of the load, the timing of demand, the marginal electricity source, water and cooling burdens where relevant, and whether claimed clean power is actually additional and deliverable at the hours of use. Secrecy turns thermodynamics into marketing.

Second: price the scarce thing. If a data center imposes transmission upgrades, reliability burdens, local pollution, or price effects, those costs should not be hidden inside ordinary ratepayer fog. The firm that wants the load should carry the load's consequences.

Third: reward flexibility. A computation that can move in time should move away from peak stress. Training runs, batch jobs, and nonurgent inference should be paid to behave like good grid citizens, not spoiled monarchs of uptime. The IEA's 2026 discussion of AI energy stresses that data centers increasingly face physical bottlenecks and that flexibility, storage, grid integration, and energy-sector coordination are becoming central, not decorative ([IEA, "Data centre electricity use surged in 2025"](https://www.iea.org/news/data-centre-electricity-use-surged-in-2025-even-with-tightening-bottlenecks-driving-a-scramble-for-solutions)).

Fourth: make efficiency gains public rather than purely expansionary. If a model or chip becomes twice as efficient, some of that gain should be captured as lower total energy pressure, not instantly spent on twice the synthetic clutter. This does not mean freezing useful services. It means treating efficiency as a dividend society can choose to save, not only as a discount coupon for more consumption.

Fifth: distinguish services. This is the hardest and most controversial condition. Public subsidies, fast-track permits, public land, preferential grid access, and political favors should not be neutral among uses. A medical-research cluster, a public-interest translation system, a weather-risk model, a scientific computing facility, and a sludge factory for infinite ad variants do not deserve identical public enthusiasm merely because each arrives wearing the word AI. Markets can sell many things. Public infrastructure does not have to bless them equally.

Carnot frowned. "You have smuggled moral judgment into interconnection policy."

"It was already there. I am labeling the container."

"Who judges?"

"That is the real argument."

He set the GPU down as if it had become legally complicated.

The danger of use-ranking is obvious. It can become censorship, industrial favoritism, elite taste, bureaucratic theater, or a new priesthood of approved computation. The danger of refusing to rank uses is also obvious. It lets capital rank them in secret by profitability, then calls the result neutral. A grid queue is a moral document even when it pretends to be engineering paperwork. A permitting regime is a philosophy of priority with better maps. The question is not whether society will choose. It will. The question is whether the choice is legible enough to argue about.

"You are learning," Carnot said. "The hidden engine is still an engine."

"And the hidden ethics are still ethics."

"Unfortunately."

The tempting slogan is "efficiency first." The better slogan is "efficiency under governance." Efficiency first can still leave demand infinite. Efficiency under governance asks what the saved energy is for, who receives the benefits, who bears the costs, and whether the system's total path actually bends away from emissions and scarcity. This is not anti-physics. It is physics taken seriously enough to enter politics without its lab coat turning into camouflage.

Carnot objected that governance often arrives too late, speaks too much, measures too poorly, and mistakes forms for thought. He was not wrong. A bad rule can freeze bad technology in place. A disclosure regime can become paperwork mulch. A clean-power requirement can be gamed with certificates that rearrange virtue without changing electrons. A local permitting fight can become NIMBY theater that blocks useful infrastructure while existing fossil systems continue their slow violence. The anti-data-center movement can be right about load and wrong about everything else. Reality is unhelpfully complete.

"Then what survives your objections?" I asked.

"The need to build rules that are more thermodynamic and less theatrical."

"Meaning?"

"Follow flows. Count losses. Identify reservoirs. Name sinks. Measure the temperature gradient between public claim and physical consequence."

That was annoyingly good.

The data-center debate often talks as if the only choices are acceleration or refusal. But thermodynamics teaches a less adolescent vocabulary: gradient, constraint, reversibility, dissipation, boundary condition, maximum, loss. The question is not "AI yes or no." The question is what boundary conditions make computation compatible with a livable energy transition. What demand can move? What demand can shrink? What demand deserves public capacity? What clean supply is real and additional? What efficiency gain should become lower total load rather than more product surface? What uses are socially precious enough to justify their heat?

"You are turning heat into citizenship," Carnot said.

"Maybe citizenship was always a heat problem."

"Do not become too pleased with yourself."

Too late, but I moved on.

There is a further irony. AI may help manage energy systems, find materials, forecast demand, design buildings, route traffic, reduce waste, and improve efficiency. The IEA's 2025 report says existing AI applications could enable emissions reductions larger than data-center emissions, while also warning that AI alone will fall far short of solving climate change ([IEA, "Energy and AI: Executive summary"](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai)). That is exactly the shape of the problem: AI is neither demon nor redeemer. It is an energy-using tool that may help reduce energy use elsewhere if deployed under institutions that care about that outcome. Without those institutions, it is simply another engine attached to appetite.

Carnot leaned back against his own tomb. The stone looked less damaged now, or maybe I had stopped demanding that cemeteries flatter me.

"You keep saying appetite," he said. "Define it."

"The conversion of every efficiency improvement into a larger scale of extraction."

"Good. Now define progress."

I hated him for that.

"Progress is when an improvement enlarges real human freedom or understanding without merely displacing its costs onto people, places, or futures that have less power to object."

"Acceptable."

"Again with acceptable."

"Superlatives waste heat."

The verdict, then:

Efficiency is mandatory. Efficiency is not salvation. A civilization that refuses efficiency is negligent; a civilization that worships efficiency is gullible. The right question is not whether AI systems can become more efficient. They can and should. The right question is whether each gain will be captured as reduced burden, expanded worthwhile service, or merely cheaper proliferation. That choice is political before it is technical.

For AI data centers, the adult policy is neither panic nor permission. Require disclosure. Charge the real grid and environmental costs. Demand hourly clean-power seriousness rather than annual accounting perfume. Favor flexible loads. Preserve efficiency gains partly as energy savings. Reserve public favors for uses with public value. Keep improving chips, models, cooling, scheduling, and software, but stop pretending that engineering excellence automatically becomes moral restraint. Make the industry say not only "we use fewer joules per task," but "the tasks we multiply are worth the joules they still consume."

Carnot sank a little deeper into the stone.

"One more thing," he said.

"Yes?"

"Do not let your age forget cold."

"Cold?"

"Every engine needs a sink. Every society imagines itself as the hot reservoir, full of ambition, combustion, invention, and pressure. But work is possible only because something else receives the waste. Rivers, air, workers, neighborhoods, ratepayers, future climates, exhausted attention. Your machines will always ask where to put what they cannot turn into glory."

The thermometer stopped moving.

"And what should we answer?"

"Answer with measurement first," he said. "Then with justice. If you answer with enthusiasm alone, the sink will answer for you."

Then he was gone. The coal was cold. The meltwater was water. The GPU was only dead hardware again, though it seemed a little ashamed of itself, which is difficult for a card with no remaining warranty.

I left the grave as I found it, in bad condition and good company. Behind me, Ivry-sur-Seine resumed its municipal patience. In front of me, the world continued building engines and calling them clouds.

The dead physicist had not told me to hate them. He had told me to count them.

And then to ask what the counting was for.
