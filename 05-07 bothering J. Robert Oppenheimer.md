today is May 7, 2026.

I found Robert Oppenheimer where the Caribbean keeps secrets badly. At Carvel Rock, off St. John, the water does not stay still long enough to pretend innocence. The place is beautiful in the insulting way some murder weapons are beautiful. His ashes were scattered here, and even that fact feels like an argument: no monument, no mausoleum, just heat, salt, and a man diluted into a view.

I brought a Geiger counter for the drama, though it clicked only at the ordinary rate of the world. What raised him was not science, or not only science. I made a bowl from black coral and seawater, dropped into it a graphite pencil, a strip of copper wire, and a printed copy of the words with which his century justified itself and accused itself. Then I read aloud the sentence from the [Franck Report](https://www.dannen.com/decision/franck.html) that still sounds like the most concise description of modern power: nuclear force offered humanity “staggering possibilities as a means of political pressure in peace and sudden destruction in war.” The sea took the line personally. It thickened, as if language itself had become radioactive. A man stood up from the shallows in a wet linen suit, narrow-shouldered, already tired of me.

He looked not cinematic but prosecutorial. Not the father of anything, not the patron saint of scientific remorse, just a brilliant administrator of irreversible consequences. I told him why I had bothered him. I wanted to ask whether the people building advanced artificial intelligence are repeating the moral structure of Los Alamos while flattering themselves that software is cleaner than plutonium.

He did not say hello. He said, “You have all become very sentimental about engineers. That is usually a sign they are about to be excused.”

So we walked the rock and argued.

The case for the builders is easy to state and not entirely stupid. They say a technology this consequential cannot be governed responsibly by people who do not understand it. OpenAI’s charter says, flatly, that to address AGI’s impact on society, the organization “must be on the cutting edge of AI capabilities,” and it even claims a “primary fiduciary duty” to humanity rather than investors ([OpenAI Charter](https://openai.com/charter/)). Anthropic’s Responsible Scaling Policy makes a parallel claim in a more bureaucratic accent: increasingly capable models may create large benefits but also catastrophic risks, including help with bioweapons or destructive autonomous behavior, so labs should tie stronger capabilities to stronger safety and security controls ([Anthropic RSP announcement](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropics-responsible-scaling-policy)). This is the decent version of the argument. Do not leave the frontier to cowboys, states, or maniacs. Stay in the race so you can civilize it.

Oppenheimer listened, then bent to pick up a shell so thin it was almost transparent. “Everyone who builds the instrument says he must build it so that a worse man does not,” he said. “Sometimes this is true. It is also the first lullaby of the conscience.”

He had the historical right to say that. The Manhattan Project was not born in a vacuum of pure curiosity. It was driven by the fear that Nazi Germany might get there first. Fear mattered, and fear was not irrational. But once a state organizes genius around emergency, the emergency becomes a solvent. It dissolves prior limits. The [Franck Report](https://www.dannen.com/decision/franck.html) did not deny the strategic context; it warned that introducing the bomb as a weapon of surprise would damage the possibility of future international control and intensify an arms race. That warning now reads less like dissent and more like prophecy.

I told him the comparison is imperfect. Nuclear weapons require scarce materials, huge industrial plants, and visible state infrastructure. AI is not one bomb but a general technique, distributed through data centers, APIs, consumer products, logistics systems, hiring pipelines, schools, propaganda mills, and weapons platforms. Its danger is not only a single flash over a city. Its danger is administrative seepage. It can normalize surveillance, deskill judgment, centralize power, cheapen fraud, flood the public sphere with synthetic persuasion, and eventually give thin moral vocabularies enormous operational reach. That is not Hiroshima. In some ways it is worse, because people will call it convenience while it is rearranging the terms of consent.

He smiled, which on him looked like a blade being tested with a thumb. “So you have improved upon us,” he said. “We made apocalypse visible. You may make it ambient.”

That line stung because it was too neat, but it was not wrong. The bomb at least forced a public category called terror. AI often arrives wearing the language of assistance. It offers summary, optimization, personalization, prediction. It is easier to resist a mushroom cloud than a calendar invite. Here is the political temptation: if a system increases state capacity, managerial reach, military targeting, or labor discipline before it produces any universally shared flourishing, institutions will deploy first and philosophize later. They always do.

Oppenheimer objected to my tone. He said I was making institutional capture sound like a software bug when it is in fact the default behavior of power. During his own ordeal, the American state did not merely question whether he had once exercised poor political judgment. It staged an exemplary humiliation. As the Department of Energy’s overview of the 1954 proceedings notes, the Atomic Energy Commission suspended his clearance, conducted a four-week closed hearing, and then revoked it, turning technical governance into a theater of ideological discipline ([DOE OpenNet: J. Robert Oppenheimer Personnel Hearings Transcripts](https://www.osti.gov/opennet/hearing)). “You speak,” he told me, “as though the problem were that laboratories may become too close to states. But when the matter becomes important enough, laboratories do not become close to states. They become organs.”

That is the heart of it. The central AI argument of our decade is often framed as safety versus acceleration, open versus closed, regulation versus innovation. Those are real disputes, but they can function as camouflage. The deeper question is who gets to decide what kinds of intelligence will be multiplied, under what constitutional restraints, for whose benefit, and with what ability to refuse. The most sinister future is not necessarily the one where the model wakes up and kills us. It may be the one where it remains obedient, and its obedience belongs to employers, security agencies, platform monopolies, and governments that have learned to call every asymmetry “efficiency.”

I pushed him on this. If that is true, then the modern lab’s promise to act as humanity’s steward may be self-deception. Yet refusal has costs too. If careful people quit, the field does not stop. It gets inherited by actors with fewer scruples or narrower ambitions. OpenAI’s charter explicitly worries that late-stage AGI development could become a competitive race without adequate safety precautions, and promises cooperation with a “value-aligned, safety-conscious project” if another such project is close to success ([OpenAI Charter](https://openai.com/charter/)). That is morally admirable language, at least on paper. Anthropic’s policy, likewise, is an attempt to precommit to thresholds and brakes before capability pressure becomes unbearable ([Anthropic RSP announcement](https://www.anthropic.com/news/anthropics-responsible-scaling-policy)). I asked him, plainly: what should a serious scientist do instead, if not build and restrain?

He answered too quickly, which is how I knew he had answered himself many times before. “First, stop speaking as if building were the only form of seriousness.” He tossed the shell back into the water. “The twentieth century made a cult of the man who can make the machine work. It has always had less patience for the man who can make the machine wait.”

Then he became more precise. Scientists and engineers, he said, should distinguish three claims that institutions deliberately blur together.

The first claim is that a thing can be built. The second is that one’s rivals may build it. The third is that therefore one is obligated to build it oneself. The first can be true, and the second plausible, while the third remains morally unproven. Los Alamos collapsed the distinction because wartime compresses thought. Peacetime corporations try to collapse it with investor decks and press releases. But “if they do not, someone else will” is not an ethic. It is a description of competitive pressure. Sometimes that pressure must be resisted precisely because it is real.

I said this sounds noble right up until the rival state, or rival firm, arrives with the finished system. He did not blink. “Yes,” he said. “Tragedy does not disappear because one points at it. One must still choose which tragedy one is willing to author.”

That is the sentence I had dug him up for.

We have become too fond of frameworks that promise to turn authorship into compliance. Red teams, evals, thresholds, charters, external advisory boards, voluntary commitments. Some of these are useful. I do not sneer at them. I would rather have them than not. But none of them answers the oldest political question, which is whether concentrated power can be trusted merely because it has written itself an ethics document. The [Franck Report](https://www.dannen.com/decision/franck.html) warned that technical breakthroughs change the conditions of international politics before institutions are ready for them. The present rhyme is obvious. Capabilities are moving faster than law; procurement faster than public legitimacy; deployment faster than democratic comprehension.

By then dawn had started bleaching the horizon. Oppenheimer was becoming less man than weather. I asked him for a verdict on AI, and he refused me one. That, too, felt earned. Instead he gave a criterion.

“When someone tells you a system this powerful must be built for the good of humanity,” he said, “ask immediately what forms of human refusal the system preserves. Ask who may decline its judgments, who may inspect its errors, who may limit its spread, who may profit without submitting, who may remain obscure before it. If the answer is effectively no one, then humanity is not the beneficiary in that sentence. It is the raw material.”

Then he stepped backward into the surf and was reduced, with infuriating elegance, to the thing he had become before I interrupted him: a disturbance in the water around a rock.

I came away thinking the controversial question is not whether AI is another atomic bomb. It is whether we are once again letting exceptional technical ambition write checks against the moral future on the theory that later institutions will cover them. That theory failed splendidly in the nuclear age. It may fail more quietly now, which is exactly why it is dangerous. A flash teaches. An atmosphere seduces.

So the stakes are not abstract. They are constitutional, economic, military, and intimate all at once. Whether your child is educated by systems you cannot question. Whether your boss can replace negotiation with algorithmic decree. Whether your government can translate total surveillance into routine administration. Whether catastrophe arrives as a weapon, a bureaucracy, a market structure, or a recommendation engine with perfect manners. The dead do not return to simplify these matters. They return, when bothered correctly, to make evasion harder.
