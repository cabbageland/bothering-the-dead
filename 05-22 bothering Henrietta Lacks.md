today is May 22, 2026.

I found Henrietta Lacks where the living had already been arguing over her body for decades, which is to say not only in the cemetery at Clover, Virginia, but in pathology slides, freezers, court opinions, genome databases, and the administrative language people use when they want the benefits of a person's flesh without having to look that person in the face. Henrietta Lacks died in 1951 after treatment for cervical cancer at Johns Hopkins. Tissue taken during her care without her knowledge or consent became the HeLa cell line, the first human cells stably cultured for research, and those cells went on to become one of the foundational tools of modern biomedicine ([NIH, “The NIH-Lacks Family Agreement”](https://www.grants.nih.gov/policy-and-compliance/policy-topics/sharing-policies/dms/privacy/nih-lacks-family-agreement)).

I did not bring incense or a shovel. It would have been indecent to approach her with old theatrical nonsense when the real sorcery had already been performed by science and law together, that cold duet which can make a woman materially immortal and politically absent at the same time. I brought a printed copy of the [Belmont Report](https://www.hhs.gov/ohrp/regulations-and-policy/belmont-report/read-the-belmont-report/index.html), a copy of [*Moore v. Regents of the University of California*](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/3d/51/120.html), the text of UNESCO's [Universal Declaration on the Human Genome and Human Rights](https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/universal-declaration-human-genome-and-human-rights), and an extension cord wound around a portable sequencer like a rosary designed by a compliance department. Then I placed one drop of my own blood on the dirt and one drop of saline on the sequencer's sensor, so that the machine would register, however stupidly, that biology is never abstract for the person being used.

The ground opened without drama. Henrietta Lacks rose as if from a long irritation rather than a long sleep.

I told her why I had bothered her. The controversial question was not whether the story of HeLa was sad, because that part is morally easy. The real question is uglier and much more alive: should people retain enforceable property-like control, including a right to share in profits or refuse later uses, over tissues removed from their bodies, or should excised tissue become a common research resource once taken, governed only by consent rules and ethics review rather than ownership?

That fight matters because modern medicine runs on samples. Tumors, blood, embryos, microbiomes, newborn blood spots, genomic sequences, surgical discards, tissue banks, and immortalized cell lines are not ornamental side-materials. They are part of the substrate of discovery. But if the body becomes raw material the moment it crosses the clinic's threshold, then people are asked to accept an astonishing civic doctrine: that what is most intimate about them may generate patents, therapies, databases, prestige, and revenue for others, while their own claim is reduced to the memory of having once contained it.

Henrietta looked at the papers in my hands and asked, before I could say anything clever, whether I had come to defend the people who made money from the dead.

I said no, not exactly. I had come because the anti-market answer and the pro-ownership answer both hide difficulties, and I wanted a conversation strong enough to break them open.

She laughed at that, not kindly.

Her first argument was simple enough to sound almost childish, which is how you know adults have spent too long avoiding it. If it came from my body, she said, why should everyone become philosophical only after it starts being worth something? Locke had argued that a person has property in his own person and that labor mixed with the world can generate rightful claims ([John Locke, *Second Treatise*, ch. V “Of Property”](https://oll.libertyfund.org/publications/reading-room/locke-boll-1-john-locke-of-property-1689)). Modern biomedicine, she said, performs a convenient inversion of that thought: it denies the source person any continuing claim, then treats the scientific labor added later as the only labor that counts. The patient's biological contribution becomes nature, free for the taking; the institution's manipulation becomes value, eligible for ownership.

It was a strong blow, and I did not insult it by pretending otherwise. The law has often leaned in precisely that direction. In [*Moore*](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/3d/51/120.html), the California Supreme Court held that a patient whose cells were used to develop a valuable cell line could not sustain a conversion claim based on property rights in his excised cells, even while recognizing that physicians could still face claims for breach of fiduciary duty and lack of informed consent. That is not a minor technicality. It means the legal system can admit wrongdoing in the way tissue is obtained while still refusing the source person a robust ownership interest in what is made from it.

Henrietta said that this was exactly the moral insult. The system offers dignity in the register of apology and exclusion in the register of value. It says: yes, you should have been told; no, you do not get to govern what follows.

I answered that strong ownership rights create their own dangers. Tissue research depends on aggregation, storage, sharing, and reuse. If every sample carried an indefinitely renewable permission right, every downstream use could become a thicket of negotiation, stratified access, and legal vetoes. The [Belmont Report](https://www.hhs.gov/ohrp/regulations-and-policy/belmont-report/read-the-belmont-report/index.html) insists on respect for persons through informed, voluntary participation, but it does not say every participation must generate an ongoing commodity claim. The public case for limiting ownership is not only greed or convenience. It is also that medicine is a cooperative enterprise, and some of its goods, vaccines and diagnostics among them, are genuinely social goods.

She snapped back that the word social becomes filthy when spoken by institutions that socialize extraction and privatize reward. HeLa cells helped enable enormous scientific advances, and the NIH's own account says they were used extensively in research over decades ([NIH, “The HeLa Genome: An Agreement on Privacy and Access”](https://www.nih.gov/about-nih/who-we-are/nih-director/statements/hela-genome-agreement-privacy-access); [NIH, “The NIH-Lacks Family Agreement”](https://www.grants.nih.gov/policy-and-compliance/policy-topics/sharing-policies/dms/privacy/nih-lacks-family-agreement)). Yet the very need for the later NIH-Lacks agreement showed that families can bear privacy and dignitary burdens long after the original taking, especially once genomic data make relatives partially legible through one person's tissue ([NIH, “The HeLa Genome: An Agreement on Privacy and Access”](https://www.nih.gov/about-nih/who-we-are/nih-director/statements/hela-genome-agreement-privacy-access)). If everyone agrees that my descendants can be exposed by data from my cells, she said, then everyone already knows the sample is not morally inert material.

That was the sharpest point she made all night. Ownership arguments often sound old-fashioned until genomics forces the issue back open. Tissue is not just detached flesh. It can be biography, kinship, risk profile, ancestry, and future vulnerability in molecular form. UNESCO's declaration says genetic research affecting an individual's genome requires prior assessment of risks and benefits and must respect dignity and rights ([UNESCO, *Universal Declaration on the Human Genome and Human Rights*](https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/universal-declaration-human-genome-and-human-rights)). Once the consequences of disclosure or reuse spill across generations, the fantasy that excision cleanly severs moral relation becomes much harder to sustain.

Still, I pressed her. If we turn tissue into full private property, do we not invite another degradation, namely the marketization of the poor? The body then becomes not only vulnerable to institutional capture but available for piecemeal monetization under unequal conditions. A desperate patient may "consent" to terms that are really a sale under pressure. And if every sample is priced, the ideal of research as a partially shared human project risks collapse into a bazaar where access tracks bargaining power.

Henrietta did not deny this. She surprised me by saying that she did not want the body treated like real estate either. She was not asking for a stock ticker for spleens. What she wanted was something sterner and more humiliating for the institutions involved: not absolute saleability, but continuing authority. The relevant analogy, she said, was less a commodity than trusteeship. If clinics and labs want tissue for future unknown uses, they should be required to ask in forms that are actually intelligible, disclose commercial possibilities honestly, permit meaningful refusal, and, where downstream profits or especially sensitive genomic uses arise, share governance and benefits rather than merely obtaining a one-time signature.

That position is much stronger than the anemic ethics of notice and much weaker than pure ownership. I found myself resisting it anyway, partly because I worry that "shared governance" can become a ceremonial phrase behind which nothing operational changes. But Henrietta had me cornered. Kant's injunction to treat humanity always as an end and never merely as a means ([Kant, *Fundamental Principles of the Metaphysic of Morals*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5682/pg5682.html)) is often quoted so piously that it goes dead in the mouth. Her case revives it. A person is used merely as a means not only when she is physically handled without consent, but also when the moral vocabulary of respect stops exactly where institutional value creation begins.

By then dawn had started to thin the dark over Clover. I told her my conclusion. Excised human tissues should not be treated as ordinary private property, freely bought and sold with indefinite exclusion rights, because that would deepen inequality and make the body an even more explicit site of coercive market pressure. But neither should tissues become ownerless research spoil the moment they leave the body. That doctrine is too convenient for systems already structured to undervalue the vulnerable. The better approach is a regime of thick consent, fiduciary obligation, ongoing governance for identifiable high-stakes uses, and benefit-sharing substantial enough to hurt whenever institutions would prefer to forget the source person.

In practice that means at least this: clear disclosure of commercial and genomic possibilities at collection; refusal rights that are real rather than theatrical; stronger duties on physician-researchers whose interests diverge from their patients', which [*Moore*](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/3d/51/120.html) rightly worried about even while denying property; special governance and family consultation where whole-genome data can expose relatives, as the [NIH-Lacks framework](https://www.grants.nih.gov/policy-and-compliance/policy-topics/sharing-policies/dms/privacy/nih-lacks-family-agreement) recognized; and benefit-sharing mechanisms, financial or otherwise, robust enough to prevent the old trick in which gratitude is demanded from those who were never asked.

Henrietta listened, then said I was still too polite. She said people like solutions that preserve research momentum while cleaning its conscience, and maybe I was no exception. Maybe what really scandalizes the modern scientific imagination is not the idea of paying or sharing, but the idea that the people at the beginning of the pipeline might possess enough authority to slow it down.

That accusation landed because it was true. The deepest stake here is not money. It is whether progress will continue to mean that institutions get to decide, almost by default, how much of a person may be converted into a public resource without that person's continuing say. I want medical discovery. I want cell lines, biobanks, and genomics to save lives. But if those goods require us to pretend that bodily materials become morally thin exactly when they become scientifically rich, then the price of progress has already started to corrupt the thing being defended.

Henrietta lay back down only after making me say it plainly. The dead do not need our tribute nearly as much as the living need better terms. If medicine wants the future inside our tissues, it must stop acting as though consent is a threshold formality and start treating participation as an enduring relationship. Otherwise the lesson of HeLa will remain what it has too often been already: that a civilization can call itself humane while building miracles out of someone it never truly bothered to ask.
