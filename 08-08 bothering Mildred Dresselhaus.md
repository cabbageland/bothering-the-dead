today is 08-08.

I found Mildred Dresselhaus at Mount Auburn Cemetery in Cambridge, where New England has learned to make death look almost scholarly. Find a Grave lists Mildred Spiewak Dresselhaus, 1930 to 2017, buried there; MIT's obituary says she died at Mount Auburn Hospital in Cambridge after a brief illness, having spent half a century at MIT as a solid-state physicist, electrical engineer, mentor, and the "queen of carbon science" ([Find a Grave, "Mildred Spiewak Dresselhaus"](https://www.findagrave.com/memorial/176683296/mildred-dresselhaus); [MIT News, "Institute Professor Emerita Mildred Dresselhaus, a pioneer in the electronic properties of materials, dies at 86"](https://news.mit.edu/2017/institute-professor-emerita-mildred-dresselhaus-dies-86-0221)).

Mount Auburn is a dangerous cemetery for physicists because it is too beautiful. The dead there get hills, birds, Victorian stone, botanized grief, and the kind of paths that make a person suspect mortality has been given tenure. Dresselhaus deserved better than a sentimental visit. She studied carbon, and carbon is not sentimental. It is diamond and soot, buckyball and bone, graphite pencil and coal seam, nanotube and corpse. It is the element that lets the living write, burn, breathe, calculate, poison the atmosphere, and make a tombstone inscription legible.

So I brought carbon in several bad moods.

I brought a pencil, a square of graphite, a cheap diamond, a dead phone battery, a transparent sheet labeled GRAPHENE by a company that was probably lying, a Raman spectrometer borrowed in imagination from a laboratory with no patience for poetry, a little thermoelectric generator, an ice cube in a steel cup, a hand warmer, a COP28 pledge printout, a page from the IPCC, a page from the IEA, and one filthy piece of coal wrapped in paper as if it were contraband. The contraband was not the coal. The contraband was the idea that modernity could keep its appetite and merely change its fuel.

For the resurrection, I did not pray. I scattered laser light.

Dresselhaus's MIT profile says her group used Raman spectroscopy to characterize single-wall carbon nanotubes, down to the dependence of spectral features on diameter and chirality; it also lists carbon nanotubes, bismuth nanowires, and low-dimensional thermoelectricity among the research areas that drew wide attention ([MIT Physics, "Mildred S. Dresselhaus"](https://physics.mit.edu/faculty/mildred-dresselhaus/)). I aimed the Raman beam at the graphite square on her grave. The cemetery answered in bands. The carbon lattice vibrated with the severe happiness of a well-posed problem. Then I placed the thermoelectric module between ice and heat, and the tiny voltage it produced crawled across the grave like a polite electrical ghost.

The stone warmed. The grass darkened. The graphite made a sound too small for ordinary hearing, which meant the dead physicist heard it perfectly.

Mildred Dresselhaus sat up with the expression of someone who had been interrupted in the middle of a calculation but was willing to forgive me if the boundary conditions were interesting.

"You brought coal to me," she said.

"I brought carbon to carbon."

"That is the kind of answer students give when they have not done the reading."

Good. We had the right dead physicist.

Today's question was this: **Can materials science and clean technology make economic growth ecologically decent, or must wealthy societies deliberately reduce energy and material throughput instead of pretending better devices will save the same appetite?**

This is the green growth versus degrowth argument, but those labels are already tired from being shouted across conference rooms by people who enjoy the word "framework" too much. The concrete stakes are harsher. They include how fast we stop burning fossil fuels, how much mining we impose in the name of clean energy, whether poor countries get the infrastructure rich countries already used the atmosphere to build, whether efficiency becomes freedom or just cheaper consumption, and whether physicists get to keep saying "innovation" as if the word were a secular absolution.

Dresselhaus picked up the pencil.

"Begin with the strongest case for invention," she said.

Fine. I would not resurrect the queen of carbon and then give her a straw opponent made of compostable conference swag.

The case for green growth is not idiotic. It says that human beings need energy, housing, mobility, refrigeration, hospitals, computing, clean water, fertilizer, heat, light, and industry. Billions of people still need more material capability, not lectures from rich countries about spiritual minimalism. The OECD's Green Growth Strategy aims to help countries pursue economic growth and development while preserving the natural assets on which well-being depends ([OECD, "OECD Green Growth Studies"](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/serials/oecd-green-growth-studies_g1g14154.html)). The World Bank's formulation of inclusive green growth argues that sustained growth is necessary for urgent development needs and that growth must be made cleaner and more efficient, not simply abandoned by those who never received its benefits ([World Bank, *Inclusive Green Growth: The Pathway to Sustainable Development*](https://documents.worldbank.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/368361468313515918)).

"That much is morally obvious," Dresselhaus said. "A child without electricity does not need your aesthetic revolt against consumerism. She needs electricity."

"Yes."

"Then be careful when you say less."

That was the first wound, and it was deserved.

The green growth case continues: the physical economy can change its metabolism. Solar panels, wind turbines, heat pumps, batteries, high-voltage transmission, nuclear power where it is acceptable, better insulation, efficient motors, industrial electrification, low-carbon steel, recycling, and digital controls can reduce emissions per unit of useful service. Materials science matters because devices are not slogans. They are band gaps, phonons, interfaces, defects, catalysts, electrolytes, thermal conductivities, lifetimes, failure modes, and manufacturing yields. Dresselhaus's own work on quantum structures for thermoelectric energy conversion helped revive a field that tries to turn temperature differences directly into electrical power ([MIT News, Dresselhaus obituary](https://news.mit.edu/2017/institute-professor-emerita-mildred-dresselhaus-dies-86-0221)). Hicks and Dresselhaus argued in 1993 that quantum-well superlattice structures might improve thermoelectric figure of merit, opening possibilities for better thermoelectric coolers ([L. D. Hicks and M. S. Dresselhaus, "Effect of quantum-well structures on the thermoelectric figure of merit," *Physical Review B*, 1993](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevB.47.12727)).

"Waste heat is not a metaphor," Dresselhaus said. "It is energy leaving by the back door."

"And you wanted to pick its pocket."

"I wanted to understand whether structure at small length scales could alter transport. The pickpocketing is your language."

"My language has more economic realism."

She smiled, unwillingly.

The physics is beautiful. The thermoelectric problem asks whether heat and charge can be disciplined at once. A good thermoelectric wants high electrical conductivity, a high Seebeck coefficient, and low thermal conductivity, a combination nature does not hand out just because policy people are running late. Low-dimensional structures give researchers another handle: change the length scale, scatter phonons, shape electronic density of states, make matter behave differently because geometry has entered the equation. In her Kavli autobiography, Dresselhaus described how quantum confinement calculations for wells and wires suggested better thermoelectric performance, and how that work helped launch low-dimensional thermoelectricity ([Mildred S. Dresselhaus, "How I Found Nanoscience," Kavli Prize](https://www.kavliprize.org/mildred-dresselhaus-autobiography)).

This is not gadget worship. It is physics doing what physics does best: finding where the old bulk assumptions stop being sovereign.

"So," she said, "do not tell me technology is a distraction. Sometimes technology is how thought becomes less wasteful."

"Sometimes," I said. "Sometimes technology is how appetite obtains a cleaner mask."

That was the second wound, and now it was mine to deliver.

The case against green growth is not a monk's tantrum. It begins with arithmetic. UNEP's 2024 Global Resources Outlook says extraction of Earth's natural resources tripled over five decades and is expected to rise by 60 percent by 2060 without urgent action; it also says extraction and processing account for over 60 percent of planet-warming emissions and 40 percent of health-related air-pollution impacts ([UNEP, "Rich countries use six times more resources, generate 10 times the climate impacts than low-income ones"](https://www.unep.org/news-and-stories/press-release/rich-countries-use-six-times-more-resources-generate-10-times); [UNEP, *Global Resources Outlook 2024*](https://www.unep.org/resources/Global-Resource-Outlook-2024)). If the transition is imagined as a one-for-one substitution of every fossil machine with a cleaner electric twin, plus continued growth in floor space, vehicle size, data centers, shipping, disposable electronics, private luxury, and planned obsolescence, then "clean" starts to mean "less visibly dirty at the point of use."

Dresselhaus turned the dead phone battery over in her hand.

"But electric vehicles are not gasoline cars in disguise," she said.

"No. They are better in a crucial dimension."

The IEA's critical minerals report is explicit that clean-energy technologies have clear climate advantages, including lifecycle emissions for electric vehicles around half those of internal combustion cars on average, with room to improve under low-carbon electricity ([IEA, *The Role of Critical Minerals in Clean Energy Transitions*, executive summary](https://www.iea.org/reports/the-role-of-critical-minerals-in-clean-energy-transitions/executive-summary)). That matters. A serious critique of green growth must not collapse into fossil-fuel apologetics. Oil companies love nothing more than a radical critique of batteries that forgets gasoline is a daily combustion ritual with geopolitics attached.

"Good," Dresselhaus said. "The atmosphere cares about molecules, not your disgust."

"The atmosphere also cares about scale."

The same IEA report says a typical electric car requires six times the mineral inputs of a conventional car, and an onshore wind plant nine times more mineral resources than a gas-fired plant, excluding steel and aluminum in that comparison; it also projects that under climate-driven scenarios lithium demand can grow over forty times by 2040, with graphite, cobalt, and nickel around twenty to twenty-five times ([IEA critical minerals report](https://www.iea.org/reports/the-role-of-critical-minerals-in-clean-energy-transitions/executive-summary)). These numbers are not arguments against clean energy. They are arguments against magical substitution. A planet of enormous electric SUVs, throwaway devices, underused buildings, and infinite consumption with better motors is not a physics solution. It is a mining plan with better branding.

Dresselhaus held up the coal.

"And fossil fuels are what, exactly? Weightless?"

"No. Fossil fuels are worse. That is why this argument is difficult."

"Good. Continue."

The degrowth or post-growth critique says: rich societies have confused well-being with throughput. They count GDP and imagine they have counted a life. They build comfort through waste and then call any threat to waste a threat to civilization. Hickel and Kallis argue that green growth is likely a misguided objective because the required absolute decoupling from resource use and carbon emissions has not been shown at the speed and scale needed for climate targets ([Jason Hickel and Giorgos Kallis, "Is Green Growth Possible?," *New Political Economy*, 2020](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/13563467.2019.1598964)). Even if one disputes their pessimism, the question they force is honest: does efficiency reduce pressure, or does it make pressure cheaper?

This is where William Stanley Jevons walks in, coughing coal dust and Victorian anxiety. In *The Coal Question*, Jevons warned that more economical use of fuel could increase rather than diminish consumption because cheaper useful power expands the field of profitable use ([W. Stanley Jevons, *The Coal Question*, Online Library of Liberty](https://oll.libertyfund.org/titles/jevons-the-coal-question)). The rebound effect is not a law of doom. It is a warning that efficiency without sufficiency can become a growth lubricant.

"You are accusing my life's work," Dresselhaus said.

"No. I am accusing the civilization that uses work like yours as a permission slip."

"That is too convenient. Scientists do not get to drop their inventions at the city gate and claim surprise when commerce adopts them."

"Agreed."

This was why I had woken her. Dresselhaus would not let either side launder itself. She had no patience for anti-technology purity, but she also knew materials are real. Carbon is not redeemed by calling it advanced. Nanotubes can be elegant and still be embedded in an economy of extraction, waste, hype, patents, supply chains, and labor. The National Inventors Hall of Fame credits Dresselhaus with pioneering work on carbon's thermal and electrical properties and with foundations for technologies leading to lithium-ion batteries ([National Inventors Hall of Fame, "Mildred Dresselhaus"](https://www.invent.org/inductees/mildred-dresselhaus)). A foundation is not a verdict. It is a responsibility with a long tail.

"Then what do you want?" she asked. "Less research? Fewer tools? A beautiful ignorance?"

"No. I want the social question to enter before the device becomes destiny."

"That sentence is nearly good."

"Nearly?"

"It still smells like a committee."

Fair.

The philosophical trap is that both sides want physics to flatter them. The green-growth optimist wants physics to say: matter is clever, constraints are opportunities, efficiency and substitution can preserve the basic shape of modern abundance. The degrowth critic wants physics to say: there are limits, entropy is real, throughput matters, a finite planet cannot host infinite material expansion. Physics, being rude, says both and then refuses to join either faction.

The first law does not care whether energy demand is noble. The second law does not care whether waste heat came from a hospital or a yacht. Quantum confinement does not care whether a thermoelectric module is installed on a spacecraft, a factory pipe, or a luxury gadget no one needed. Climate forcing does not care whether CO2 was emitted in the name of growth, development, convenience, or "user engagement." Physics is the great destroyer of alibis. It asks for flows, not vibes.

Dresselhaus took the Raman printout and inspected the peaks.

"You keep saying appetite," she said. "That is a moral word."

"Yes."

"But energy is not appetite. Energy is cooking food, pumping water, refrigerating vaccines, annealing silicon, running trains, lighting stairwells, making fertilizer, keeping old people alive during heat waves."

"Also private jets, empty offices, fast fashion, oversized cars, crypto scams, air conditioning left on for no one, and a thousand data centers teaching machines to generate better advertising."

"So distinguish."

There. The whole argument arrived in one verb.

Distinguish.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's climate justice entry summarizes Henry Shue's influential distinction between subsistence emissions and luxury emissions, where emissions needed to meet basic needs have a stronger moral claim than emissions for luxury consumption ([SEP, "Climate Justice"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/justice-climate/)). That distinction should humiliate the laziness of both camps. "Growth" is too blunt. "Less" is too blunt. A coal plant powering neonatal care is not morally equivalent to a coal plant powering decorative excess. An electric bus is not the same claim on lithium as a vanity truck. A heat pump in public housing is not the same social object as a heated driveway. A semiconductor fab that makes durable public infrastructure possible is not morally equivalent to an endless churn of disposable devices engineered to become obsolete in eighteen months.

"You are moving from GDP to use," Dresselhaus said.

"And from use to capability."

The capability approach, associated with Amartya Sen and developed by many others, shifts attention from the mere possession of resources to what people are actually able to do and be with them ([SEP, "The Capability Approach"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/)). That is the escape hatch from the stupidest version of the debate. The goal is not maximum GDP. The goal is not maximum austerity. The goal is enough material provision for real human capabilities, with shrinking ecological pressure where consumption is excessive, wasteful, status-driven, or actively corrosive.

Dresselhaus nodded.

"So you want growth in capability and contraction in throughput."

"Yes."

"Say that instead of degrowth when you want people to listen."

"Degrowth people will accuse you of cowardice."

"They may be correct and ineffective. The two are compatible."

The dead can be brutal because they do not need tenure.

Still, there is a hard truth inside the degrowth word. Wealthy societies probably do need deliberate reductions in some forms of production and consumption, not merely greener versions of them. The IPCC's AR6 WGIII headline statements say demand-side measures and new ways of providing services could reduce global greenhouse gas emissions in end-use sectors by 40 to 70 percent by 2050 compared with baseline scenarios, while being consistent with improved basic well-being ([IPCC AR6 WGIII Headline Statements](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/downloads/report/IPCC_AR6_WGIII_HeadlineStatements.pdf)). Demand-side mitigation is not just the politics of turning things off. It is better cities, public transit, smaller and better-insulated homes where appropriate, repair, sharing, durable goods, less food waste, less excessive meat consumption, fewer status flights, and infrastructure that makes low-energy life convenient rather than penitential.

"Convenience matters," Dresselhaus said.

"Yes. Virtue that requires heroic inconvenience scales badly."

"And bad scaling is a physics problem disguised as moral failure."

That one belonged to her.

The climate movement often underrates how much design does moral work. A city where daily life requires a private car is not a neutral stage on which individuals reveal their preferences. It is a machine that manufactures demand. A building with poor insulation manufactures heating load. A product glued shut manufactures waste. A job market that makes people live far from work manufactures commuting. A culture that sells status through size manufactures material throughput. To tell individuals to consume less inside systems built to force consumption is like blaming electrons for a circuit diagram.

Dresselhaus liked that.

"Now you sound like an engineer."

"I will recover."

But green-growth optimism has an equally important correction. Poverty is also a system. Underdevelopment is not ecological virtue. Energy poverty kills. Lack of refrigeration spoils medicine and food. Lack of clean cooking fuels damages lungs. Lack of transport isolates people from school, clinics, work, and one another. A rich-country politics of "less" becomes obscene if it treats global poverty as a convenient climate strategy. Climate justice requires contraction where there is excess and expansion where there is deprivation. It requires technology transfer, finance, infrastructure, and political respect, not sermons from the countries that burned first.

"Good," Dresselhaus said. "Now give me the materials version."

The materials version is this: we must build a clean-energy world, but not the most material-intensive version of it. Electrify quickly, but reduce the number and size of vehicles by building transit, walking, cycling, freight rail, and denser land use. Deploy batteries, but design for smaller packs where possible, recycling, repair, chemistry shifts, and long life. Build renewables and transmission, but also reduce peak demand through insulation, thermal storage, heat pumps, reflective roofs, and sane building codes. Use advanced materials, but do not let novelty become planned obsolescence in a lab coat. Fund physics, chemistry, and engineering not as a substitute for politics, but as instruments of a politics that has finally decided what kinds of abundance are worth having.

Dresselhaus set the diamond next to the coal.

"Same element," she said.

"Different structure."

"Exactly. Do not ask whether carbon is good or bad. Ask what structure it has entered."

This was the deepest lesson she could give the argument. Carbon is morally innocent as an atom and morally explosive as an economy. Graphite in a pencil teaches a child to write. Coal in a furnace warms a house and warms the planet. Carbon fiber can lighten vehicles; carbon dioxide can acidify oceans; carbon nanotubes can open band-structure marvels and occupational-health questions; carbon in forests is storage, carbon in oil is buried history with a fuse attached. The atom is not the ethic. The arrangement is.

So it is with growth.

Growth of what? Throughput, care, life expectancy, public health, leisure, literacy, repair capacity, ecosystem restoration, weapons, advertising, loneliness machines, floor area, vehicle weight, quarterly returns, number of packages delivered at midnight to people too tired to want them properly? To ask "growth or degrowth" without specifying the object is to ask a materials scientist whether structure matters and then leave before the lecture begins.

"You are getting less annoying," Dresselhaus said.

"Death has improved your generosity."

"No. Your argument improved."

I accepted the distinction.

The verdict, then, cannot be a clean victory for either camp. Green growth is right that rapid decarbonization requires invention, investment, deployment, manufacturing, infrastructure, and a ferocious respect for physical detail. Anyone who treats materials science as a distraction from social change has not understood that society lives inside matter. But green growth is wrong when it treats efficiency and substitution as enough to preserve rich-world consumption habits. The atmosphere does not care about our branding, and the lithosphere is already being drafted into our redemption story.

Degrowth is right that wealthy societies must reduce wasteful and luxury throughput, that demand is politically made, that justice requires distinguishing subsistence from excess, and that GDP is a vulgar proxy for flourishing. But degrowth is wrong when it lets the word "less" become a fog that obscures the need for more energy, more infrastructure, more medical capacity, more climate adaptation, and more material security for people who have been denied them. A politics that cannot say "more" to the poor and "enough" to the rich is not climate justice. It is mood.

Dresselhaus touched the thermoelectric module again. The voltage flickered.

"Physics does not save you from judgment," she said.

"No."

"But it improves the judgment by punishing vagueness."

"That is why I keep waking physicists."

"That, and because you like being scolded by competent dead people."

I could not cite that, but it was true.

To celebrate physics today is to celebrate the refusal of matter to accept our slogans whole. Dresselhaus's carbon world is not a hymn to limitless invention and not a sermon on limits alone. It is a discipline of structure. A nanotube's electronic character depends on chirality; a thermoelectric's promise depends on transport coefficients that fight one another; a climate transition's justice depends on whether we can rearrange the economy so that capability rises while extraction, combustion, and waste fall. In each case, the lazy category is the enemy. Carbon is not one thing. Growth is not one thing. Less is not one thing. More is not one thing.

The dead physicist began to fade, not dramatically, but as a signal fades when the instrument is no longer aligned.

"What should I tell the living?" I asked.

"Tell them not to worship materials," she said. "Study them. Worship makes idols; study makes responsibilities."

"And the climate transition?"

"Build it," she said. "But do not build it as a replica of the old appetite. Change the structure."

Then she returned to the grave, leaving the graphite square warm in my hand and the coal looking embarrassed beside it.

I left the pencil there, because carbon should be allowed one innocent task before dawn. Today is for Mildred Dresselhaus, who knew that the smallest structures can change the largest possibilities, and for the harder knowledge that possibility is not yet wisdom. A civilization is a material with properties. If we keep its structure, it will conduct the same hunger through cleaner wires. If we change its structure, perhaps energy can become capability, efficiency can become freedom, and less waste can finally mean more life.
