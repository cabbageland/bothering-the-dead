today is 06-23.

I found Mary Anning where the cliff stops pretending it is solid.

Her grave is in the churchyard of St Michael the Archangel in Lyme Regis. The church says plainly that the pioneering fossil hunter is buried there, and the Lyme Regis Museum notes that Mary and her brother Joseph lie near the stained-glass window later raised to commemorate her life ([St Michael the Archangel, Lyme Regis](https://www.achurchnearyou.com/church/9794/); [Lyme Regis Museum, "The Mary Anning Experts"](https://www.lymeregismuseum.co.uk/about-us/the-mary-anning-experts/)). This is almost too orderly a resting place for someone whose life was spent learning that the earth is a bad archivist: cliffs collapse, bones surface, names are lost, gentlemen publish, women sell the specimen, time grinds the receipt into mud.

The morning tide was still low enough for the shore to look like a page torn out of a black book. The Blue Lias kept its counsel. Ammonites lay in the rock like coiled weather. I brought no flowers. Flowers have the short moral imagination of a condolence card. I brought an ammonite, a chipped hammer, a vial labeled SO2, a copy of the Paris Agreement, a printout of the National Academies report on reflecting sunlight, and one very small hand mirror because every climate fantasy begins by asking the sky to look at itself.

Mary Anning's credentials were not embroidered by universities. They were dragged from cliffs. The Natural History Museum describes her as a pioneering fossil collector whose finds contributed significantly to modern science; it notes that as a girl she helped uncover the five-metre ichthyosaur skeleton that became one of the first scientifically studied examples of that extinct marine reptile ([Natural History Museum, "Mary Anning: The unsung hero of fossil discovery"](https://www.nhm.ac.uk/discover/mary-anning-unsung-hero.html)). The Lyme Regis Museum says her finds helped change ideas about the history of the Earth, including ichthyosaurs, the long-necked plesiosaur in 1823, and the "flying-dragon" pterosaur in 1828 ([Lyme Regis Museum, "Mary Anning"](https://www.lymeregismuseum.co.uk/collection/mary-anning/)). The British Geological Survey preserves an 1822 letter in which Anning lists fossils with the calm authority of someone who had learned a coast by walking it until her hands hurt ([British Geological Survey, "Mary Anning: a remarkable fossil collector"](https://www.bgs.ac.uk/news/mary-anning-a-remarkable-fossil-collector/)).

So I put the ammonite on the grave and cracked the vial of sulfur dioxide only in imagination, because I am not a startup with a balloon and a liability waiver.

Then I read aloud the line from the Paris Agreement about holding warming well below 2 degrees C and pursuing efforts to limit it to 1.5 degrees C ([Paris Agreement, Article 2](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/english_paris_agreement.pdf)). The ammonite warmed in my palm. The mirror fogged. The grave shifted with the slow irritation of limestone being asked to host another theory.

Mary Anning sat up in a dark dress that smelled of salt, clay, tobacco smoke from other people's rooms, and the wet patience of creatures that had waited 195 million years to become evidence. She looked at the vial.

"What sort of fossil is that?"

"Not fossil. Sulfur dioxide."

"You brought me poison."

"I brought you an argument."

"Men usually put poison in smaller bottles and arguments in larger ones."

The question was this: **Should humanity research, prepare, or someday deploy solar geoengineering - especially stratospheric aerosol injection - as a response to climate overshoot, or is the whole project a dangerous alibi for refusing to stop burning the ancient dead?**

The stakes are not speculative theater. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change says human activities have unequivocally caused global warming, with global surface temperature reaching about 1.1 degrees C above 1850-1900 in 2011-2020 ([IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report, Summary for Policymakers](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/summary-for-policymakers/)). UNEP's 2025 Emissions Gap Report says current policies point toward about 2.8 degrees C of warming over this century, while full implementation of nationally determined contributions points to about 2.3-2.5 degrees C, still far from the Paris goals ([UNEP, *Emissions Gap Report 2025*](https://www.unep.org/resources/emissions-gap-report-2025)). The argument is about crop failures, marine heat, migration, glaciers, insurance markets, coral reefs, laborers outside in impossible afternoons, small island states, flood maps, future children, present cowardice, and whether the fact that the house is on fire gives anyone permission to load the attic with mirrors.

"Begin with the best case," Mary said. "If you begin with fools, you learn only your own vanity."

Fine. The best case for solar geoengineering does not begin with heroism. It begins with dread. We are not cutting emissions fast enough. We are not adapting fast enough. Carbon dioxide removal may matter, but it is slow, expensive, uneven, and uncertain at the scales imagined. The heat already trapped in the political system is in some ways worse than the heat trapped in the atmosphere, because molecules do not attend campaign fundraisers. If the world overshoots 1.5 degrees C and severe risks accelerate, the argument goes, we may need temporary tools that reduce temperature while emissions cuts, carbon removal, and adaptation catch up.

Solar geoengineering is the general family of proposals to reflect a small fraction of incoming sunlight or otherwise change the planetary radiation balance. The National Academies report focuses on stratospheric aerosol injection, marine cloud brightening, and cirrus cloud thinning; it defines stratospheric aerosol injection as adding reflective particles to the stratosphere so more sunlight is reflected back to space ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*, Summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). The vulgar name is sun-dimming. The polite name is solar radiation modification. The honest name depends on who is holding the syringe.

Mary picked up the ammonite.

"You found the bones of dead seas," she said. "Now you want to make an artificial cloud to save the living one?"

"Some people want to know whether it could reduce harm."

"Could."

"Yes."

"That word has many graves."

The pro-research case is not that deployment is good. It is that ignorance is not moral purity. The National Academies says emissions reductions, carbon removal, and adaptation should remain the center of climate response, but it also argues that decision makers need policy-relevant knowledge about solar geoengineering, including evidence for and against deployment, because pressure for dramatic action or unilateral deployment could arise in a dangerous future ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). It recommends a U.S. research program that is minor compared with overall climate research, focused on knowledge rather than deployment, internationally coordinated, transparent, inclusive of climate-vulnerable communities and the Global South, and equipped with review and "exit ramps" ([National Academies, Recommendations 4.1 and 5.1](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)).

This is the adult version of the case: not "block the sun and relax," but "learn enough to make a decision before panic, billionaires, or governments with bad maps make one for everyone." Research could improve models of aerosols, clouds, rainfall, ozone chemistry, crop effects, detection, monitoring, governance, and public deliberation. It could reveal that deployment is unacceptable, ineffective, unjust, or uncontrollable. Negative knowledge is still knowledge. A well-governed research program might be useful precisely because it arms the world against irresponsible enthusiasm.

"You trust research to restrain power?" Mary asked.

"No."

"Good. I was worried death had softened you."

She had a point. Knowledge does not arrive as a monk. It arrives with budgets, careers, patents, contractors, reputations, laboratories, national prestige, and people whose sentences begin with "at scale." The National Academies itself warns that research could legitimize solar geoengineering, undermine mitigation, create path dependence, or build a community invested in deployment ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). The slippery slope is not a fallacy when the hill is greased with money.

Mary turned the vial toward the morning light.

"And the best case for doing it?"

Not research. Deployment. That case is narrower and more desperate. Solar geoengineering could, in theory, lower global average temperature faster than emissions reductions alone can lower atmospheric concentrations. The National Academies says available research indicates solar geoengineering could reduce surface temperatures and potentially lessen some climate risks, though it would also introduce new risks ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). The Royal Society's briefing similarly notes that solar radiation modification could reduce some temperature-related harms while not solving other greenhouse-gas impacts ([Royal Society, "Solar radiation modification: Summary briefing"](https://royalsociety.org/news-resources/projects/solar-radiation-modification/summary-briefing/)).

The moral form of this argument is triage. If heat waves are killing people, if crop zones are shifting, if ice sheets are approaching thresholds, if coral reefs are bleaching into museums, if political systems remain too slow, then perhaps refusing even to consider a fast cooling intervention is not humility. Perhaps it is aesthetic squeamishness performed by people who can afford air conditioning. The question is not whether solar geoengineering is beautiful. It is whether, in a world of gross negligence, a temporary ugly intervention could prevent worse suffering.

Mary looked at the shore.

"That is how men spoke of quarrying cliffs. The specimen was always urgent."

"Sometimes the specimen was urgent."

"And sometimes the hunger was."

The first objection is the one Mary had earned the right to make: solar geoengineering treats a symptom while the cause keeps feeding.

The National Academies is blunt that solar geoengineering is not a substitute for reducing greenhouse-gas emissions because it does not address the underlying driver of climate change or key CO2 impacts such as ocean acidification ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). The Royal Society says the same about ocean acidification: rising atmospheric CO2 drives it, and SRM would not abate that damage ([Royal Society briefing](https://royalsociety.org/news-resources/projects/solar-radiation-modification/summary-briefing/)). You can shade a fevered body and still leave the infection in the blood. The ocean does not care that the sky has been adjusted for optics.

"You burn fossils," Mary said. "Then you ask the air to hide the heat."

"That is the accusation."

"No. That is the description."

This is where her presence became less charming. Fossil fuels are not merely energy sources. They are the compressed dead enlisted into modern appetite: coal forests, planktonic seas, ancient sunlight, prehistoric bodies turned into heat, speed, plastic, nitrogen fertilizer, war logistics, suburban sprawl, and the illusion that past life can be consumed without consequence. To bring Mary Anning into the argument is to remember that fossils first shattered a human vanity: species vanish, worlds end, the Earth is older and stranger than scripture allowed. Now the same deep dead have been burned into a crisis, and the proposed rescue is to intervene in the sky while continuing to negotiate politely with the smoke.

The second objection is termination shock. If solar geoengineering were used to offset a large amount of warming while greenhouse gases kept accumulating, then stopping it abruptly could cause catastrophically rapid warming. The National Academies identifies this as an unacceptable risk of rapid warming if the intervention were terminated after masking substantial warming without reducing emissions ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). It is not a cure if it requires civilization to keep taking the dose for generations while the disease worsens underneath.

"So your grandchildren inherit the pump," Mary said.

"If we deploy badly, yes."

"If you deploy well?"

"They inherit the pump, the treaty, the monitoring system, the lawsuit, the weather attribution fight, the military anxiety, the insurance table, and the moral debt."

"A rich inheritance. No shells?"

That is the intergenerational scandal. Fossil-fuel modernity already spent atmospheric capacity it did not own. Solar geoengineering could become a second mortgage on the future: we fail to decarbonize, mask the heat, and hand later people a system they cannot easily stop. Advocates can reply that later people may prefer an inherited sunshade to inherited mass death. True. But the fact that a future person might accept a tourniquet does not absolve the person who kept cutting.

The third objection is governance. Solar geoengineering is planetary in effect but not planetary in consent. A sulfate injection strategy pursued by one state, coalition, or private actor could change rainfall, temperatures, monsoon patterns, storm risks, agriculture, and political perceptions across borders. Even if the actual physical changes were smaller or more ambiguous than feared, attribution would become explosive. If a drought followed deployment, who would decide whether it was caused by the intervention, by climate change, by natural variability, or by the usual bureaucratic demon named "uncertainty"? Who pays? Who stops? Who gets a veto? Who speaks for a farmer whose sky has become a committee decision?

The Rio Declaration's precautionary principle says lack of full scientific certainty should not be used as a reason for postponing cost-effective measures to prevent serious or irreversible environmental degradation ([Convention on Biological Diversity, "Precautionary Approach"](https://www.cbd.int/marine/precautionary.shtml)). But precaution has two faces here. It can argue against solar geoengineering because the intervention may create serious irreversible or transboundary harms. It can also argue for researching it because climate change itself is already producing serious harms, and ignorance may leave the world defenseless against both impacts and reckless deployment. Anyone who says "precaution" as if it ends the debate has not noticed that the knife has two edges.

Mary smiled.

"A principle with teeth on both sides. At last, a fossil worth keeping."

The Convention on Biological Diversity has already drawn a cautious line. Its 2010 Decision X/33 calls on parties, in the absence of science-based, global, transparent, and effective control and regulatory mechanisms, to ensure that no climate-related geoengineering activities that may affect biodiversity take place until there is an adequate scientific basis and proper consideration of environmental, social, economic, and cultural risks, except for small-scale scientific research in a controlled setting with prior environmental assessment ([CBD Decision X/33](https://www.cbd.int/decision/cop/?id=12299); [CBD climate-related geoengineering page](https://www.cbd.int/climate/geoengineering)). That is not anti-knowledge. It is anti-stunt.

Stunts have arrived anyway.

Harvard's SCoPEx, a proposed stratospheric controlled perturbation experiment meant to study aerosols relevant to solar geoengineering, was canceled in March 2024 after years of controversy; Harvard says the research team had suspended work in 2023 and that the principal investigator was no longer pursuing it, while broader solar geoengineering research continued under Harvard's Salata Institute ([Harvard Salata Institute, "An Update on SCoPEx"](https://salatainstitute.harvard.edu/an-update-on-scopex/); [Keutsch Group, SCoPEx](https://www.keutschgroup.com/scopex)). In 2025, the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency demanded information from Make Sunsets, an unregulated startup launching sulfur dioxide into the air to sell "cooling" credits; the EPA said the company was already banned in Mexico and reported more than 124 deployments on its website ([EPA, "EPA Demands Answers from Unregulated Geoengineering Start-Up"](https://www.epa.gov/newsreleases/epa-demands-answers-unregulated-geoengineering-start-launching-sulfur-dioxide-air)).

"There," Mary said. "The gentleman has already put his hammer through the specimen."

"Not exactly."

"Close enough for courtship."

The Make Sunsets case matters because it reveals the nightmare in miniature: a private actor claiming planetary benefit, selling credits, releasing substances, exploiting regulatory gaps, and forcing the public conversation to chase him. This is not the same as a carefully governed scientific program. That is precisely the point. If legitimate institutions refuse to govern, illegitimate experiments do not politely wait. A ban without enforcement becomes theater. A research program without democratic legitimacy becomes provocation. A private market without permission becomes atmospheric trespass.

The fourth objection is moral hazard. If solar geoengineering seems available, fossil-fuel interests and timid governments may use it to delay emissions cuts. They may say, explicitly or silently: why bear the cost of transformation if sunlight can be adjusted? Why strand assets if aerosols can buy time? Why change agriculture, transport, buildings, finance, and extraction if a reflective haze can lower the graph?

This objection is not paranoia. It is pattern recognition. The world has spent decades converting every climate warning into a later deadline. UNEP's 2025 report says the world remains far from the Paris goal and that larger, faster cuts are needed, even though low-carbon technologies are available ([UNEP, *Emissions Gap Report 2025*](https://www.unep.org/resources/emissions-gap-report-2025)). Delay is not a side effect of climate politics. Delay is one of its main products.

"If a man will not fix his roof," Mary said, "do not sell him a theory of indoor umbrellas."

"Unless the child is already wet."

"Then dry the child and fix the roof."

"That is my position."

"Too neat."

She was right. It is never that neat. Solar geoengineering could either reduce suffering during a rapid decarbonization push or become the excuse for avoiding one. The same tool can be an emergency splint or a permanent fraud. The distinction is not in the aerosol. It is in the political conditions around it: emissions trajectories, governance, funding, ownership, transparency, liability, public consent, and whether the fossil-fuel economy is being dismantled or merely shaded.

The fifth objection is justice. Solar geoengineering would distribute risks and benefits unevenly. Some regions might benefit from lower temperatures; others might face altered rainfall or perceived harm. The people most vulnerable to climate change are not necessarily those with research budgets, aircraft, patents, or permanent seats in powerful rooms. The National Academies calls for stakeholder engagement, inclusion of climate-vulnerable communities, underrepresented groups, Indigenous populations, and the Global South in research design and governance ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). The fact that such inclusion has to be specified tells you what history has usually done.

Mary's face hardened at that. She knew something about expertise extracted from people who were not invited into the room. The Lyme Regis Museum says many of Anning's finds ended up in the Natural History Museum credited to the gentlemen who bought them, and that the Geological Society did not admit women until long after her death ([Lyme Regis Museum, "The Mary Anning Experts"](https://www.lymeregismuseum.co.uk/about-us/the-mary-anning-experts/)). She had seen a form of governance in which knowledge climbed upward and credit rained sideways.

"Who sells the sky specimen?" she asked.

"That is one of the fights."

"Who names it?"

"Another."

"Who is not invited?"

"Too many."

The sixth objection is that solar geoengineering may not restore any prior climate. The National Academies says it cannot provide a reliable means to restore global or regional climate to some desired former state ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). That sentence should be carved onto the entrance of every lab. Climate is not a thermostat in a rented apartment. Lowering global mean temperature does not rewind rainfall, ocean chemistry, sea level, ice dynamics, species ranges, agriculture, or politics. Averages are seductive because they give catastrophe a single number. The world is not a single number.

"You cannot put the plesiosaur back in the sea," Mary said.

"No."

"But you can sell the skeleton."

"Yes."

"Then beware of men selling climate skeletons."

The best pro-geoengineering thinkers know all this. That is why the serious version of the argument is almost painfully hedged. The National Academies recommends $100-200 million over five years for an initial U.S. research program, small relative to climate research, with governance and public engagement supported alongside the science ([National Academies, funding considerations](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). It says outdoor experiments involving releases should be considered only when they can provide critical observations unavailable through lab work, models, or natural opportunities, and should be subject to permitting, impact assessment, and strict scale limits ([National Academies, outdoor experimentation recommendations](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)).

"So the careful people say: learn, but do not rush," Mary said.

"Yes."

"The opponents?"

The opponents are not all romantics hugging atmospheric purity. More than 630 scholars support an International Non-Use Agreement on Solar Geoengineering. The initiative calls for no public funding for development, no outdoor experiments, no patents, no deployment, and no support in international institutions, arguing that planetary-scale solar geoengineering cannot be governed in a globally inclusive, fair, and effective way ([Solar Geoengineering Non-Use Agreement](https://www.solargeoeng.org/non-use-agreement/)). Their strongest argument is not that research is sinful. It is that research and development normalize a technology whose deployment conditions cannot be made legitimate. Some doors should remain difficult to open because the hallway slopes toward machinery.

Mary considered this.

"They want the fossil left in the cliff."

"They want the cliff protected."

"And if the sea takes it?"

"They would say better that than building a machine that eats coasts."

"Not foolish."

"No."

This is the real tension. The research advocates fear ignorance, panic, unilateral action, and lost opportunities to reduce suffering. The non-use advocates fear normalization, capture, geopolitical coercion, and the fantasy that a globally consequential atmospheric intervention can be governed justly by the same civilization failing to govern emissions. Both sides are not equally right on every point, but both are serious. The lazy middle position is to say "more research" as if research had no politics. The lazy anti-position is to say "ban it" as if bans enforced themselves and ignorance had no victims.

Mary began arranging pebbles on the grave: one for mitigation, one for adaptation, one for carbon removal, one for loss and damage, one for solar geoengineering. She put the solar pebble last and slightly apart.

"What is your answer?" she asked.

"No deployment now. No commercial atmospheric releases. No patents or proprietary systems that could give a company leverage over a planetary intervention. No field experiments outside a transparent public registry and permitting process. No research program that is not tied to faster mitigation. No governance without countries and communities most vulnerable to climate harms in the room from the beginning. But yes to public, internationally coordinated research on risks, detection, modeling, governance, and possibly tightly limited outdoor process studies, because the world needs enough knowledge to refuse, regulate, detect, or, in a truly dire future, decide."

"You have written a fence and called it a path."

"Yes."

"Fences matter. Ask any cliff."

The position is ugly because the problem is ugly. Solar geoengineering should be treated as a possible emergency risk-management tool, not a climate solution. The word "solution" should be reserved for stopping greenhouse-gas pollution, protecting sinks, adapting honestly, repairing loss, and building political economies that do not require the dead carbon of vanished worlds to keep the lights on. A sunshade that leaves the smokestack open is not a solution. It is stage lighting for failure.

But a refusal to learn is also not a solution. It may feel clean to say: no research, no experiments, no normalization, no atmospheric trespass. I understand the cleanliness. I do not trust it. Climate danger may create future pressure for deployment whether scholars approve or not. A desperate state after a lethal heat decade might act. A private actor might continue stunts. A military might explore capabilities quietly. A coalition might claim emergency authority. In that world, a public body of knowledge, monitoring tools, legal norms, and democratic arguments may be the difference between refusal and helpless outrage.

Mary tapped the mirror.

"You want knowledge that makes action harder."

"Exactly."

"That is rare knowledge."

"And worth funding."

Research must therefore be designed against its own corruption. It should begin with hard prohibitions: no deployment authorization; no private cooling credits; no exclusive patents on deployment-critical methods; no classified pathway to operational use; no fossil-fuel funding; no research governance dominated by countries that caused the bulk of historical emissions; no outdoor release without public notice, independent assessment, meaningful consultation, and a scale so small it cannot alter climate. It should include a public international registry, open data, social-science and ethics work funded at the same seriousness as atmospheric chemistry, and exit ramps with actual brakes. It should test not only "could it cool?" but "who would be harmed, who would decide, who would profit, who would be ignored, and how would we stop?"

"And if the research says it works?" Mary asked.

"Then the temptation grows."

"And if it says it does not?"

"Then the temptation may grow anyway, because people prefer bad tools to helplessness."

"So you need politics, not merely measurements."

"Yes."

The dead fossil hunter approved of that more than I expected. She had lived in the gap between evidence and authority. She had found the bones; others had possessed the institutions. She knew that facts do not govern themselves. An ichthyosaur skeleton did not automatically produce a just scientific community. A climate model will not automatically produce a just climate policy. The specimen can be true and still be used badly.

The other hard question is whether even limited outdoor experiments should happen. Here I think the default should be no until governance exists, not yes until harm is proven. The National Academies says limited outdoor experiments could be pursued under appropriate governance when they provide critical observations unavailable otherwise and are small enough to limit impacts ([National Academies, outdoor experimentation](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). That is a reasonable threshold but not a permission slip. In practice, outdoor experiments have symbolic force beyond their physical scale. A harmless balloon can still be politically explosive. A technically tiny release can be socially huge. The atmosphere is not only chemistry. It is commons, myth, weather, fear, and jurisdiction.

"You speak as if the air has manners," Mary said.

"People do. Sometimes."

"Rare fossil."

This is why the governance of small experiments matters as much as their physical emissions. SCoPEx was not canceled because it had already damaged the climate. It became a governance test and failed to find a legitimate path through public concern, Indigenous opposition, media attention, and institutional uncertainty. That does not prove all outdoor research is impossible. It proves that the social license for this field is not a decorative appendix to the science. It is one of the central phenomena being studied.

Mary held the ammonite to her ear.

"The cliff says you are late."

"Yes."

"The sea says you are bargaining."

"Also yes."

"The bones say?"

"The bones say extinction is not a metaphor."

That is what Mary Anning brought to the debate that no committee report can. She helped show a world in which extinction was real, deep time was real, and human categories were small. The creatures in the cliffs did not die to teach us humility, but we can still learn it from them. Extinction is not a reversible public-relations problem. Climate loss is not a spreadsheet with better shading. There are doors time closes. There are reefs that will not come back on our schedule. There are species that will not reassemble because a minister says innovation. There are children who will live in the weather we leave, not the weather we meant.

Solar geoengineering is dangerous partly because it tempts us to think reversibility is stronger than it is. Overshoot and return. Deploy and terminate. Cool and compensate. Model and govern. These words are not lies, but they are thinner than the world. The fossil record is full of discontinuities. Bodies vanish. Seas move. Climates reorganize. Survivors inherit conditions, not intentions.

"Say the sentence plainly," Mary said.

Fine. **The world should not deploy solar geoengineering now, and should build international rules to prevent unilateral or commercial deployment; but it should support a small, transparent, publicly governed, internationally accountable research program whose central purpose is to understand risks, strengthen governance, improve detection, and preserve the ability to make an informed collective refusal or decision under future climate danger.**

"Too many adjectives," Mary said.

"The adjectives are load-bearing."

"Always a bad sign in a cliff."

She was right again. But some cliffs need shoring before the evacuation route is clear.

The practical hierarchy should be brutal. First, cut emissions. The Paris Agreement itself frames the temperature goal with rapid reductions according to best available science and equity ([Paris Agreement, Articles 2 and 4](https://unfccc.int/sites/default/files/english_paris_agreement.pdf)). Second, adapt to harms already locked in. Third, fund loss and damage without turning every ruined community into a grant-writing exercise. Fourth, remove carbon where removal is durable, verifiable, and not a license to emit. Fifth, research solar geoengineering as a dangerous emergency topic under democratic guardrails. Sixth, refuse deployment unless the world has something we do not currently possess: legitimate global governance, deep emissions cuts already underway, a clear risk-risk case, monitoring capacity, liability arrangements, public consent across vulnerable regions, and a plan for termination that does not make the future hostage.

"And who decides whether those conditions exist?"

"That is the wound."

"Not a small wound."

"No."

There is no world parliament with clean hands. The United Nations can convene, but power remains uneven. Great powers can bully. Small states can be ignored. Corporations can lobby. Scientists can overestimate their neutrality. Activists can overestimate the sufficiency of refusal. The atmosphere has no sheriff. This means deployment legitimacy may be impossible for a long time. Good. Technologies with planetary consequences should have to climb a wall high enough to exhaust the arrogant.

Mary put the vial back in my pocket.

"Do not break that on my grave."

"I wasn't going to."

"That is what men say before the cliff gives way."

Before she sank back, she asked one more question.

"Why wake me for this and not one of your gentlemen with equations?"

"Because you knew how evidence comes out of the dead, and how easily powerful people turn it into property."

"And because I sold fossils."

"Yes."

"Do not make me purer than I was."

That was the final correction. Mary Anning was not a marble saint of untouched nature. She made a living extracting dead creatures from cliffs and selling them. She knew commerce, need, curiosity, risk, weather, class, and the compromises by which knowledge enters the world. That is why she is a better judge than a cleaner ghost. She would not say "never touch." She would ask what the touching costs, who pays, who learns, who profits, who is named, who is buried under the explanation.

The tide had begun to rise. The ammonites disappeared first, as if time were taking back its punctuation. The grave closed without drama. The mirror cleared. The sulfur vial was still imaginary, which is how I prefer my planetary interventions before breakfast.

Mary's last words came from the stone, or the cliff, or my own bad conscience dressed in a bonnet.

"If you must study how to dim the sun, do it as one studies a poison in a locked cabinet. Label it. Guard it. Learn its harms. Do not sell it as medicine while the fire is still being fed. And do not mistake a cooler room for a forgiven house."

Then she was gone, and the coast resumed its old work: exposing, erasing, exposing, erasing. The fossil dead kept their counsel. The living sky remained bright, unmodified, and already altered by what we have burned. The task is not to keep our hands clean. That fantasy died sometime between the coal seam and the heat dome. The task is to stop making the future choose between our negligence and our machines.
