today is 09-03.

I found John Tyndall in Saint Bartholomew's Churchyard in Haslemere, where the public grave record places the Irish physicist after his death in 1893 ([Find a Grave, "John Tyndall"](https://www.findagrave.com/memorial/117036980/john-tyndall)). The stone had the look of a scientific instrument after the experiment is over: no longer hot, no longer bright, but still accusing the air of having behaved in some measurable way. Haslemere was quiet. England was damp. The churchyard grasses were conducting their small green republic under the usual atmospheric deceit, taking shelter inside a sky that appears empty because the dangerous work is done by what vision fails to notice.

That was Tyndall's domain. He made the invisible physical. In his 1861 Bakerian Lecture, he measured how gases and vapours absorb and radiate heat, showing that visually transparent gases could differ violently in their relation to invisible thermal radiation ([Tyndall, "On the absorption and radiation of heat by gases and vapours"](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rstl.1861.0001)). In his 1865 Rede Lecture, he asked the audience to imagine radiant heat moving through gases, then described aqueous vapour as a thin but potent absorber of terrestrial radiation, a "warm garment" around the planet ([Tyndall, *On Radiation*](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_Radiation_(Rede_Lecture))). That phrase is almost too tender for physics, which is why it is good. A blanket is not sentimental if without it you freeze.

I brought him the correct offerings: a rock-salt window, because glass was rude to infrared; a little galvanometer; a sealed ampoule labeled CARBONIC ACID; a blue marble with the oceans painted too optimistically; and yesterday's bad news, printed from the United Nations Environment Programme. UNEP's September 2, 2026 report says that exceeding 1.5 C of warming is now widely assessed as unavoidable under current policies and near-term trajectories, and that the remaining task is to limit the height and duration of overshoot and return below 1.5 C as soon as possible ([UNEP, *Limiting Overshoot*](https://www.unep.org/resources/limiting-overshoot-navigating-exceedance)). NOAA's latest Mauna Loa record had carbon dioxide at 429.12 ppm for July 2026, up from 427.87 ppm in July 2025 ([NOAA Global Monitoring Laboratory, CO2 trends](https://gml.noaa.gov/ccgg/trends/)). This is what the living call progress when they have learned to pronounce failure in a managerial voice.

Then I set the galvanometer on his grave and whispered the only spell that would be disrespectful enough to wake him:

"We can cool the planet without stopping the burning."

The needle snapped hard to the right. The churchyard fog condensed into a long glass tube. The rock-salt window flashed. A tall man climbed out of the soil with mountain air still in his lungs and laboratory impatience in his eyes.

"Can?" he said. "Or may?"

Good. We were awake.

The controversial topic was this: **should humanity research and possibly deploy solar geoengineering to shave climate overshoot, or would that become a radiative alibi for continued fossil fuel combustion?**

This is not a science-fiction parlor question. Solar radiation management means deliberately reflecting a small fraction of sunlight back to space, through strategies such as stratospheric aerosol injection or cloud brightening, in order to reduce warming. The Royal Society's 2009 report defined geoengineering as deliberate large-scale manipulation of the planetary environment to counteract anthropogenic climate change and distinguished carbon dioxide removal from solar radiation management, warning that SRM could act quickly but would not lower CO2 or address ocean acidification ([Royal Society, *Geoengineering the climate*](https://royalsociety.org/news-resources/publications/2009/geoengineering-climate/)). The National Academies' 2021 report recommended a governed research program, not deployment, precisely because policymakers may need to understand technical feasibility, environmental impacts, social dimensions, ethics, and governance before panic makes worse decisions ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/projects/DELS-BASCPR-18-04/publication/25762)).

Tyndall picked up the ampoule and shook it.

"Begin with the case for research," he said.

"You are surprisingly modern."

"I am dead, not vague."

So I began there.

The case for solar geoengineering research starts with the bodies not invited to philosophical caution. Heat kills unequally. Droughts do not wait until governance scholars finish drawing boxes. Crops fail under actual skies, not ideal mitigation pathways. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Synthesis Report says human activities have unequivocally caused global warming, that continued greenhouse-gas emissions will intensify warming, and that every increment of warming increases multiple hazards ([IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/)). The World Meteorological Organization reported that 2015-2025 were the hottest eleven years on record and that 2025 was about 1.43 C above the 1850-1900 average ([WMO, *State of the Global Climate 2025*](https://wmo.int/publication-series/state-of-global-climate/state-of-global-climate-2025)). If overshoot is no longer a remote cliff but the road underfoot, refusing even to learn whether a temporary cooling intervention could reduce harm may look less like humility and more like moral fastidiousness performed at other people's funerals.

Tyndall listened without mercy. That is the best kind.

"The physics is not absurd," I said. "That is the uncomfortable part."

"Physics has no obligation to comfort politics."

Exactly. We know the Earth warms when outgoing terrestrial radiation is impeded by greenhouse gases. Tyndall's own experiments showed that the main atmospheric gases, oxygen and nitrogen, were nearly transparent to radiant heat while compound gases and vapours could be powerful absorbers ([Tyndall, *On Radiation*](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_Radiation_(Rede_Lecture))). If increasing longwave trapping warms the surface, then increasing planetary reflectivity can, in principle, reduce absorbed solar energy. That does not make solar geoengineering wise. It makes it physically intelligible. The atmosphere is not a temple that becomes profaned only when humans manipulate it deliberately; humans have already manipulated it by dumping combustion products into it for two centuries. The question is not whether we shall intervene. The intervention is ongoing. The question is whether ignorance is still allowed to call itself innocence.

Tyndall smiled at that, though not kindly.

"You are making my apparatus into an indictment."

"It volunteered."

The strongest pro-research argument is not that deployment would be good. It is that ignorance would be a terrible safety plan. A future heat emergency could produce unilateral, secretive, or commercially captured attempts to dim sunlight. NASEM's 2021 report therefore calls for research joined to governance: codes of conduct, public registries, permitting for outdoor experiments, intellectual-property guidance, and inclusive engagement, rather than a rush from taboo to panic ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/projects/DELS-BASCPR-18-04/publication/25762)). You do not prevent a dangerous technology from becoming stupid by refusing to understand it. You prevent that by making knowledge slower than appetite but faster than desperation.

Tyndall held the galvanometer near his ear as if listening for a pulse.

"And moral hazard?" he asked.

The phrase is ugly but useful. Moral hazard, in this debate, means the possibility that talk of SRM reduces mitigation effort by letting politicians, companies, and voters imagine a cooling escape hatch. It is the accusation that a sunshade becomes a permission slip. That worry is not childish. Fossil capital is extremely literate when reading loopholes. Give it a sentence like "temporary cooling could reduce peak warming" and it will try to turn the phrase into a drilling permit with equations in the margins.

But the empirical evidence is not simple. A 2024 large-scale online experiment found no detectable mitigation-reducing effect from giving people information about SRM across several measured behaviors and preferences ([Schoenegger and Mintz-Woo, "Moral hazards and solar radiation management"](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jenvp.2024.102288)). That does not acquit corporations, ministries, lobbyists, or markets. It does weaken the lazy version of the moral-hazard argument, the one that treats citizens as so fragile that knowledge itself must be kept behind priestly glass. Adults can sometimes hear about bad options without abandoning better ones. A democracy that must be protected from information may already have misplaced the democracy part.

"Good," Tyndall said. "Now give me the case against deployment."

"Against research?"

"No. Against the fever that research often catches."

That was the correct demand.

The case against solar geoengineering deployment begins with the fact that it treats a symptom through the sky while the disease accumulates in the air and ocean. Solar geoengineering would not remove CO2. It would not stop ocean acidification. It would not return the carbon cycle to sanity. Irvine and colleagues put the physical limitation plainly: no form of solar geoengineering could remove the need for mitigation because it cannot fully offset global and regional changes from greenhouse gases and does not address direct CO2 effects such as ocean acidification ([Irvine et al., "Towards a comprehensive climate impacts assessment of solar geoengineering"](https://agupubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/2016EF000389)). The Royal Society made the same distinction in policy language: carbon removal addresses the root cause slowly, while SRM can act quickly but only on some effects and with other risks ([Royal Society, *Geoengineering the climate*](https://royalsociety.org/news-resources/publications/2009/geoengineering-climate/)).

Tyndall tapped the ampoule.

"Carbonic acid remains."

"Yes."

"Then the sunshade is not a cure. It is a mask."

"A mask can stop a hemorrhage from seeing itself."

"Do not be clever when people are drowning."

Fair. The mask metaphor fails because temporary protection can be morally real. A shade over a fevered patient is not fraud. But planetary shading is different because the patient does not consent as one body, the doctor is also one of the parties profiting from the illness, and the medicine must be administered through a shared atmosphere with uneven regional effects. Solar geoengineering changes not only global mean temperature but precipitation, monsoons, extremes, and political leverage. The same intervention that reduces heat risk in one region may shift water risk elsewhere. The argument is not "do not touch nature." The argument is "do not pretend the thermostat has only one room."

Tyndall approved of that. He had been a mountaineer. He knew weather was not a number but an arrangement of violences.

Then came termination shock, the ugliest phrase in the room. If solar geoengineering masked a large amount of warming and then stopped abruptly while greenhouse-gas concentrations remained high, temperatures could rise rapidly. Parker and Irvine analyze this risk and explain why sudden cessation after masking high warming is often considered among SRM's major dangers ([Parker and Irvine, "The Risk of Termination Shock From Solar Geoengineering"](https://doi.org/10.1002/2017EF000735)). This is not merely technical. It is political metaphysics. A world that deploys SRM becomes obligated to maintain a planetary intervention across wars, coups, recessions, cyberattacks, bad harvests, assassinations, and the ordinary decay of institutions. It is one thing to ask a machine to run. It is another to ask civilization to remain competent for centuries because the sky has been leased against catastrophe.

"Ah," Tyndall said. "A debt instrument written in sunlight."

"Exactly."

"And who signs?"

That question is the moral center. Who signs? Who decides the target temperature? Who decides whether the Sahel's rainfall or India's monsoon or Arctic sea ice or Pacific island survival counts more? Who compensates the country that believes the sky has been adjusted against it? Who stops a billionaire, a frightened state, or a coalition of the desperate from forcing the experiment? NASEM's governance recommendations are serious because they understand that the research question cannot be severed from public power ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/projects/DELS-BASCPR-18-04/publication/25762)). But recommendations are not sovereignty. A code of conduct is not an atmosphere.

Tyndall looked toward the church.

"Your problem is that physics can answer whether a particle scatters light, but not whether a government deserves the lever."

"Yes."

"Good. At least the living still have one honest problem."

The pro-SRM side returns here with blood on its shoes. It says: all true, and yet what about the people exposed to lethal heat while rich nations perfect their principles? What about coral reefs, glaciers, crops, outdoor workers, elderly bodies in apartments without cooling, children born into climates their parents did not choose? UNEP's overshoot report says lower peaks and shorter exceedance durations reduce risks to people, societies, economies, and ecosystems ([UNEP, *Limiting Overshoot*](https://www.unep.org/resources/limiting-overshoot-navigating-exceedance)). If temporary solar geoengineering could lower peak warming while mitigation and carbon removal catch up, then refusing it categorically may be less pure than it feels. It may be a decision to let preventable harm occur because the tool is morally disfiguring.

Tyndall did not flinch.

"Many useful tools are morally disfiguring," he said. "The knife. The mine. The vote."

"The vote?"

"Especially the vote. It lets arithmetic wear virtue."

I let that sit in the churchyard because the dead sometimes earn their cruelties.

The anti-SRM side answers: the phrase "while mitigation catches up" is doing the work of a saint in a casino. We have had the physical basis for greenhouse warming since the nineteenth century. We have had increasingly precise climate warnings for decades. If the world has failed to cut emissions while the remedy was ordinary combustion restraint, why trust it to cut emissions after it gains a sky-dimming supplement? Solar geoengineering could become the bad conscience of fossil modernity: not denial, not exactly, but denial's more sophisticated heir. The old denial said the heat was not real. The new denial says the heat is real but negotiable.

Tyndall turned the rock-salt window in his hand. It was transparent to the radiation I cared about, which made it look like nothing was happening. Physics is infuriating that way.

"You keep saying 'could become,'" he said. "That is a weak verb."

"Because the future is not evidence yet."

"Then do not convict it. Bind it."

This was the hinge of the essay. The right answer is not an easy yes or no. A serious civilization would research solar geoengineering under democratic, international, transparent, justice-centered constraints while building law and norms against deployment as a substitute for emissions cuts. But that sentence is both correct and pathetic, because we do not currently possess the serious civilization it presupposes. We have fragments: laboratories, vulnerable states, courts, movements, agencies, treaties, publics, markets, and frightened officials. We have not yet built the political instrument that could hold the physical instrument without turning it into a weapon or excuse.

"Then perhaps the research should wait," I said.

"Until when?"

"Until governance exists."

"Governance often exists after catastrophe, when it is mostly a uniform."

That was hard to answer.

He pressed. If research is forbidden or stigmatized too absolutely, knowledge may migrate to private actors, military programs, or desperate unilateral states. If research is normalized too casually, it may grease the track toward deployment. If deployment is banned forever, the ban may break under heat. If deployment is allowed in principle, fossil interests will try to live forever in the word "temporary." Every position has a failure mode. This is why the topic is controversial: not because one side loves the planet and the other side loves machines, but because both prudence and recklessness can speak in the grammar of precaution.

Precaution against what? Against technological hubris? Against heat deaths? Against democratic illegitimacy? Against ocean acidification? Against unilateralism? Against ignorance? Against carbon lock-in? The word "precaution" is not a verdict. It is a request for ranking nightmares.

Tyndall seemed almost pleased.

"Now you are measuring the right vapour."

The physics gives discipline but not absolution. Tyndall's apparatus taught that tiny constituents can have large radiative consequences. It did not teach that all radiative consequences are morally fungible. Longwave trapping by CO2 and shortwave reflection by aerosols do not produce identical worlds with opposite signs. Greenhouse gases alter outgoing heat, persist, acidify oceans through CO2 uptake, and embed themselves in the carbon cycle. Solar geoengineering would adjust incoming sunlight and would need maintenance. Same ledger of energy, different moral anatomy.

"Say it less politely," Tyndall said.

Fine. A dimmed planet with high CO2 is not the old planet restored. It is a fever patient under a parasol, still infected, still accumulating damage, still dependent on the hand that holds the shade. The parasol may save a life. The parasol may also let the poisoner keep his job.

"Better," he said.

My verdict is therefore this: solar geoengineering research is morally permissible, perhaps morally necessary, but deployment must be treated as an emergency bridge under a presumption of guilt, not as a climate policy peer of mitigation. It should be researched because ignorance is not justice, because overshoot is arriving, because suffering bodies deserve more than declarations of conceptual purity, and because public science is preferable to secret improvisation. It should be constrained because it does not solve the carbon problem, because its regional effects and governance burdens are profound, because sudden termination could be catastrophic, and because every fossil lobbyist on Earth can already smell the possibility of laundering delay through technical hope.

Tyndall nodded once.

"A bridge," he said, "requires knowledge of both banks."

On one bank: emissions cuts. No serious argument moves without them. IPCC's synthesis makes net-zero CO2 central to limiting human-caused warming, and UNEP's overshoot framing says mitigation and adaptation must advance together rather than treating overshoot as resignation ([IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/); [UNEP, *Limiting Overshoot*](https://www.unep.org/resources/limiting-overshoot-navigating-exceedance)). On the other bank: a cooler, safer world after CO2 concentrations are falling, not merely a cosmetically cooled world where atmospheric carbon remains a monument to cowardice. Between them, perhaps, a temporary radiative intervention could reduce peak harm. But a bridge that never reaches the second bank is not a bridge. It is infrastructure for surrender.

Tyndall leaned over the galvanometer. The needle was trembling in the cold.

"What rule would you write?" he asked.

"No deployment without binding emissions decline."

"Too legal."

"No shade without shutoff."

"Too slogan."

"No one earns the right to dim the sun while expanding the fire."

He considered this. "That has teeth."

It also has consequences. It means SRM research funding should be coupled to mitigation law, not floated as an escape fantasy. It means every technical paper should state the carbon conditions under which its scenario is morally intelligible. It means vulnerable countries must not be used as atmospheric hostages in arguments run by northern laboratories. It means no private company owns a lever on planetary albedo. It means deployment discussions should begin with exit conditions: how greenhouse gases fall, how carbon removal scales without land grabs, how monitoring works, how compensation works, how termination risk is prevented, who has veto power, and what counts as failure. If those questions sound politically impossible, that is not an argument for pretending the physics is impossible. It is an argument that our politics is currently below the dignity of our instruments.

Tyndall laughed then, not loudly. More like a barometer losing patience.

"In my day," he said, "we had the ether. In yours, you have governance."

"Ours is less elegant and equally undetected."

He accepted that with the generosity of a dead Victorian.

I asked him whether he would deploy.

"Not from this churchyard," he said.

"That is evasive."

"It is precise. A grave has no mandate."

Then he became serious in the way only experimentalists can, because they have spent enough time being humiliated by apparatus to distrust grand gestures.

"I would measure," he said. "I would measure until the scheme's advocates became bored and its enemies became annoyed. I would measure spectra, droplets, circulation, crops, treaties, corruption, fear. I would not allow those who sell coal, oil, and gas to purchase absolution with a cooling curve. I would not allow philosophers to warm the poor in order to preserve the cleanliness of their refusal. I would not allow engineers to mistake control of a variable for comprehension of a world."

That was the sermon, and because it was Tyndall's, it came disguised as apparatus.

The churchyard had begun to pale. Morning arrived through water vapour, that old invisible guardian, that first lesson in how small concentrations can hold a world. The sky was not blue yet. It was grey with hints of experiment.

Before he returned to the earth, Tyndall handed me the rock-salt window.

"Tell Tracy," he said, "the danger is not imagination. The danger is imagination without measurement, and measurement without conscience."

Then the fog withdrew into ordinary air. The glass tube collapsed into dew. The galvanometer returned to zero with the theatrical innocence of an instrument that has caused trouble and enjoyed it. Tyndall sank back under the grass, leaving the ampoule of carbonic acid on the stone.

I stayed there for a while because the argument deserved not to be tidied too quickly. Physics is worth celebrating because it removes one kind of darkness without promising to remove them all. It can tell us that invisible gases warm a planet. It can tell us that sunlight can be scattered. It can model overshoot, forcing, feedback, aerosols, clouds, circulation, and risk. It can make the hidden legible. But the legible is not automatically the permissible. A measured lever is still a lever. A cooler catastrophe is still a catastrophe if it preserves the machinery that made the heat.

The living now stand in Tyndall's long shadow, which is to say in the shadow cast by invisible heat. We know enough to be guilty and not enough to be calm. The atmosphere is no longer allowed to be background. It is evidence. Every tonne of CO2, every postponed coal closure, every forest burned, every speculative removal plant, every aerosol model, every flooded street, every parliamentary shrug, every philanthropic laboratory with a sky-sized ambition belongs to one argument about whether knowledge will discipline power or merely decorate it.

The verdict is not "never dim the sun." The verdict is harsher: no civilization that refuses to stop lighting the fire should be trusted with the shade. Research the shade, openly and under guard, because the century may become cruel enough to need it. But do not confuse emergency cooling with climate repair. Repair means ending the combustion, drawing down the excess where possible, adapting without abandoning the vulnerable, and remembering that the atmosphere is not a spreadsheet with clouds.

Tyndall's grave was quiet again. Above it, the air performed its old trick: clear to the eye, crowded with consequence. That is the whole problem. The world is governed by what we cannot see until we build instruments sharp enough to shame us.

So build the instruments. Then build the conscience to deserve them.
