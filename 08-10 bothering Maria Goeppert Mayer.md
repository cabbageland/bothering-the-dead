today is 08-10.

I found Maria Goeppert Mayer in San Diego, at El Camino Memorial Park, where the grass has the humiliating confidence of a place that believes sunshine can make death look administrative. Find a Grave lists Maria Mayer, 1906 to 1972, at El Camino Memorial Park; the Nobel Prize records the sharper public fact, that Maria Goeppert Mayer shared the 1963 Physics Nobel with J. Hans D. Jensen and Eugene Wigner for discoveries concerning nuclear shell structure ([Find a Grave, "Maria Goeppert Mayer"](https://www.findagrave.com/memorial/6652975/maria-mayer); [Nobel Prize, "Maria Goeppert Mayer - Facts"](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1963/mayer/facts/)).

I did not bring flowers. Flowers are lovely but weak evidence.

I brought a cigar box full of numbered buttons: 2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126. I brought a child's nesting doll, a worn copy of *Elementary Theory of Nuclear Shell Structure*, an Argonne badge printed on paper thin enough to confess bureaucracy, one fake university appointment letter that said VOLUNTARY ASSOCIATE PROFESSOR, one real-looking paycheck made out for zero dollars, a chalkboard eraser, a bus ticket, a grocery receipt, a childcare bill, and a stack of modern reports about graduate researchers and postdocs being asked to hold the future together on stipends that would embarrass a civilized republic.

For the resurrection, I made a nucleus.

Mayer's 1948 *Physical Review* paper summarized evidence that nuclei with 20, 50, 82, or 126 neutrons or protons were especially stable ([Maria G. Mayer, "On Closed Shells in Nuclei," *Physical Review*, 1948](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRev.74.235)). Her later spin-orbit model supplied the severe beauty behind the trick: shells, gaps, angular momentum, paired nucleons, a nucleus not as pudding but as architecture ([Maria Goeppert Mayer, "Nuclear Configurations in the Spin-Orbit Coupling Model. I. Empirical Evidence," *Physical Review*, 1950](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRev.78.16)). The American Physical Society tells the story with useful bluntness: she spent years in unpaid positions before professorship, anti-nepotism rules kept Johns Hopkins from hiring her normally, Columbia gave her office space but no salary, and in Chicago she was a voluntary associate professor while also working at Argonne ([APS News, "August 1948: Maria Goeppert Mayer and the Nuclear Shell Model"](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2008/08/goeppert-mayer-nuclear-shell-model)).

So I arranged the numbered buttons on the grave in concentric rings. The wind tried to scatter 28, which is how one knows the wind has not studied nuclear structure. I pressed the zero-dollar paycheck under the innermost shell. Then I struck the chalkboard eraser against the stone until chalk dust rose like a small academic blizzard.

At 2 buttons, the grave clicked.

At 8, the grass tightened.

At 20, something under the ground exhaled.

At 28, the fake appointment letter burst into blue flame and burned without consuming itself, which is exactly how unpaid academic labor works.

At 50, Maria Goeppert Mayer sat up with chalk on her sleeves and the expression of someone who had been awakened not from death but from a committee meeting that had lasted fifty-four years.

"You have put 126 too far from 82," she said.

"I was arranging symbolism."

"Then you were arranging badly."

Good. We had the right physicist.

Today's question was this: **Should science abolish unpaid research appointments, unpaid internships, and prestige-for-labor arrangements, even when they sometimes create a loophole for people excluded from formal positions, or are such bans a blunt moral luxury that might close the only door some outsiders have?**

This is not a soft complaint about gratitude. The stakes are who can afford to enter science, who gets to call exploitation "training," whether talent is selected by curiosity or rent money, whether institutions can consume love as fuel, and whether the romantic story of the unpaid genius quietly launders a labor system that keeps reproducing the same kinds of bodies in the same kinds of rooms. In physics terms, it is a question about binding energy. What holds a scientific life in orbit long enough for it to discover anything?

Mayer picked up the zero-dollar paycheck.

"Begin with the ugly defense," she said.

"The defense of unpaid work?"

"The defense of the crack in the wall. Do not pretend every crack is only a wound. Sometimes it is where light gets in."

Fine. She had earned that complication.

The strongest defense of unpaid scientific affiliation begins from the fact that institutions are not already just. If the paid doorway is barred by marriage rules, gender, immigration status, class prejudice, disciplinary fashion, disability, caretaking duties, age, or some chair's soft little kingdom of taste, then an unpaid title, office, or informal arrangement may be the only way to keep a person near instruments, journals, collaborators, and problems. Mayer's own career is the dangerous example. APS says Johns Hopkins' nepotism rules kept her from a faculty appointment, Columbia gave her space without salary, and Chicago made her a voluntary associate professor; nevertheless she did work that changed nuclear physics ([APS News, "Maria Goeppert Mayer and the Nuclear Shell Model"](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2008/08/goeppert-mayer-nuclear-shell-model)). The University of Chicago Library's exhibit notes that she was fifty-eight before she became a full professor at UC San Diego, after decades of titles such as "fellow" or "voluntary research associate" ([University of Chicago Library, "Maria Goeppert Mayer - Elementary Theory of Nuclear Shell Structure"](https://www.lib.uchicago.edu/collex/exhibits/binding-energy/prometheus-unbound-paper-trails-of-an-atom-age/maria-goeppert-mayer-elementary-theory-of-nuclear-shell-structure-1955/)).

"If you had outlawed my unpaid appointment," she said, "would you also have forced them to hire me?"

"Not automatically."

"Then your purity might have removed my key and left the door locked."

That was the first wound.

She was not defending injustice. She was defending survival under injustice. A person with a forbidden mind does not always have the luxury of waiting for the institution to deserve her. Sometimes she takes the bad badge, finds the chalk, and does the calculation anyway. Science has always had such half-legal intellectual weather: amateurs, spouses, technicians, observatory assistants, women "helping" husbands, independent scholars, patients collecting data, retired engineers in back rooms, graduate students whose labor disappears into a principal investigator's name, and volunteers whose curiosity embarrasses formal gatekeeping. If one says, "No one may work unless the institution pays," one must also say how the institution will be compelled to pay the people it has always preferred to call inconvenient.

Mayer arranged 50 and 82 closer together.

"Also," she said, "not every unpaid act is exploitation. Some work is play. Some work is love. Some work is an obsession that will crawl under any fence."

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry on exploitation begins with unfair advantage-taking and then immediately complicates the field: exploitation may be transactional or structural, harmful or mutually beneficial, and some cases improve both parties while still seeming unfair ([SEP, "Exploitation"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/exploitation/)). That matters. If a retired physicist voluntarily mentors students, if an amateur astronomer donates observations, if a patient community gathers data for its own survival, if a visitor uses a lab bench for a bounded collaboration with authorship and access, calling all of that "unpaid labor" in the same tone as a boss dodging payroll is intellectually lazy. A good moral category needs edges, not just volume.

"Now make the case against me," Mayer said.

"Against your exception?"

"Against the mythology built from it."

Good. Dead physicists are at their best when they refuse to be made into motivational posters.

The case against unpaid science starts with the same fact and changes the lighting. Mayer's unpaid appointments did not prove unpaid appointments are noble. They proved she was brilliant enough to produce under conditions that should shame the institutions around her. A woman can survive a locked door by crawling through a window. This is not an argument for windows as employment policy.

Exploitation in the structural sense arises when the rules of the game make one group vulnerable and another group benefits from that vulnerability ([SEP, "Exploitation"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/exploitation/)). That is exactly what prestige labor does when it says: work now, eat later; publish now, salary later; teach now, tenure maybe; intern now, career maybe; accept the unpaid affiliation, and perhaps the room will begin to believe you exist. The institution receives labor, reputation, data, supervision, diversity photographs, grant outputs, and proof of "passion." The worker receives access, hope, and a bill due on the first of the month.

"Hope is not nothing," Mayer said.

"No. But it is not wages."

"Neither is wages the whole of science."

"No. But lack of wages is a filter."

This is the class cruelty hiding inside the romance of vocation. If the early stages of science require unpaid time, then science does not select simply for talent. It selects for talent plus cushion. Family money becomes a hidden instrument. A spouse's salary becomes a hidden fellowship. Health, visas, rent, childcare, medical bills, debt, and geographic luck become invisible peer reviewers. NACE's position statement on internships says paid interns receive more job offers and higher starting salaries than unpaid interns, citing its 2022 student survey; it gives paid interns an average of 1.61 job offers, unpaid interns 0.94, and students without internships 0.77 ([NACE, "Unpaid Internships and the Need for Federal Action"](https://www.naceweb.org/about-us/advocacy/position-statements/position-statement-us-internships/)). That is not physics, but it is a measurement of a gate.

The gate is still present inside the research workforce. On April 8, 2026, the U.S. Government Accountability Office reported that federally funded STEM graduate researchers and postdocs are critical to the future workforce, but graduate researchers and postdocs receive compensation far below other workers with similar education; GAO listed about $36,000 per year for graduate researchers and about $60,000 for postdocs in the relevant data it analyzed, while a full-time worker with a doctoral degree earned about $118,000 in recent Bureau of Labor Statistics data ([GAO-26-107757, "STEM: Additional Data Needed on Graduate Researcher and Postdoctoral Scholar Compensation"](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107757)). GAO also said low pay, cost of living, funding uncertainty, and lack of benefits can affect recruitment and retention ([GAO-26-107757](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-107757)).

Mayer looked at the grocery receipt.

"That report is not about unpaid professors in 1935," she said.

"No. It is about the same spell, pronounced with modern paperwork."

"Be precise."

"Science still talks as if devotion can pay part of the invoice."

That was the second wound, and this one belonged to the living.

The phrase "for the love of science" is beautiful until a payroll office learns to say it. Then it becomes a solvent. It dissolves salaries, authorship, family life, sleep, disability accommodations, and the ability to leave a bad adviser. It makes exploitation feel intimate. The exploited person is not merely underpaid; she is invited to prove that her love is real by accepting the loss. If she refuses, the institution can call her insufficiently dedicated. If she accepts, the institution can call itself inspiring.

"But love is real," Mayer said.

"Yes."

"Mine was real."

"Yes."

"Then do not insult it by treating it only as false consciousness."

She was right. A crude labor analysis can make a scientist sound like a dupe for wanting the problem more than she wants comfort. That is false to the phenomenology of discovery. Mayer did not need a motivational seminar to care about physics. The shell model was not assembled by HR compliance. Her 1948 paper did the beautiful dry work of collecting evidence for closed nuclear shells; her later spin-orbit coupling model proposed a structure that could make the magic numbers cease being magic and start being physics ([Mayer, "On Closed Shells in Nuclei"](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRev.74.235); [Mayer, "Nuclear Configurations in the Spin-Orbit Coupling Model. I"](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRev.78.16)). If you have ever watched a hard problem begin to yield, you know love can be more accurate than resentment.

The question is whether institutions may spend that love as if it were money.

They may not.

Mayer smiled slightly. "Now we have arrived somewhere."

The distinction is simple and apparently too difficult for adult institutions: love may motivate labor; it must not replace compensation for labor from which the institution benefits. The Department of Labor's Fact Sheet #71 uses a "primary beneficiary" framework to distinguish some educational internships from employment under the Fair Labor Standards Act in for-profit settings ([U.S. Department of Labor, "Fact Sheet #71: Internship Programs Under the Fair Labor Standards Act"](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/71-flsa-internships)). That legal test is not a complete morality for science, and university research has its own nonprofit complexities, but the philosophical distinction is useful. Who primarily benefits? Who controls the work? Who receives the output? Who can walk away? Who gets paid, credited, trained, protected, and promoted? Who is asked to be grateful for access while producing value?

Mayer picked up the Argonne badge.

"At Argonne I was paid," she said.

"Part time."

"Payment changes posture."

"Exactly."

Payment changes posture because it admits that a person is not merely being permitted to exist near the work. She is part of the work. A paycheck is not the whole of dignity, but it is one of dignity's less poetic load-bearing structures. It lets a worker refuse sentimental blackmail. It lets talent from less cushioned lives remain long enough to become visible. It makes exit more possible and therefore consent less fake. It forces the institution to count labor as a cost instead of pretending that passion is a naturally occurring subsidy.

Mayer began sorting the buttons into shells again.

"The nucleus is stable at certain numbers," she said, "because the structure matters. A collection of particles is not yet an institution."

"That metaphor is dangerously neat."

"All metaphors are dangerous when handled by essayists."

Fair.

Still, the physics would not leave us alone. A closed shell is not a moral lesson. It is a physical configuration with energy consequences. But it gives the mind a shape. Stable scientific lives also require shells: salary, health insurance, childcare, authorship norms, visa support, housing, mentorship, credit, equipment, time, protection from harassment, and a path to independent judgment. Remove enough shells and the particle leaks out. Then the institution says, with astonishing stupidity, that the particle lacked resilience.

Feminist epistemology is useful here because it refuses to separate who gets to know from what gets known. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes feminist standpoint theories as arguing that subordinated social positions can generate epistemic advantages on questions where power and subordination are implicated, while also noting serious disputes about how such claims should be specified and limited ([SEP, "Feminist Epistemology and Philosophy of Science"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/feminism-epistemology/)). Put less politely: exclusion is not only unfair to excluded people. It makes knowledge worse by narrowing the set of suspicions, analogies, questions, and tolerated inconveniences in the room.

"Careful," Mayer said. "Do not say women make different nuclei."

"I am not saying that."

"Do not say injustice improved my science."

"I am not saying that either."

"Then say the correct thing."

The correct thing is this: injustice did not make Maria Goeppert Mayer brilliant. It made her brilliance unnecessarily expensive to her and unnecessarily late for everyone else. The fact that she saw the shell model partly because she was less trapped by the reigning nuclear expectations is not a defense of her exclusion. APS notes that many physicists favored the liquid drop model and doubted a shell model because nucleon interactions seemed too strong, while Mayer's relative lack of formal nuclear training may have made her less biased against the shell idea ([APS News, "Maria Goeppert Mayer and the Nuclear Shell Model"](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2008/08/goeppert-mayer-nuclear-shell-model)). That is an epistemic lesson about disciplinary freshness. It is not an employment model called "underpay the outsider until she becomes original."

Mayer approved that sentence with a small nod, which I will be insufferable about for centuries if allowed.

The defender of unpaid roles now retreats to scarcity. Labs have limited money. Universities are bureaucratic. Young people need experience. Visiting scholars need affiliation. Independent researchers need library access. Retired scientists want to contribute. Some work is educational, exploratory, communal, or self-directed. If every exchange of intellectual energy is treated as compensable employment, the defender says, the result may be less openness, not more justice. Small labs will take fewer students. Informal collaborations will become risk-averse. People outside standard pipelines will be kept out because it is administratively safer to have no crack in the wall.

This is the best version of the defense because it remembers that regulation can harden institutions. A ban without funding can become austerity with good adjectives. "No unpaid positions" can mean "no positions." A requirement to pay can help the already credentialed candidate whose labor is budgeted, while the odd outsider loses even informal access. Institutions, being cunning in the way furniture is cunning when you keep tripping over it, can satisfy the letter of justice while reducing the number of people admitted to the experiment.

"So?" Mayer asked.

"So the ban cannot be the whole reform."

"Good."

The right answer is not to sanctify unpaid labor and not to abolish every voluntary association by slogan. The right answer is to make unpaid labor institutionally difficult, morally suspect, and narrowly bounded, while making paid access normal, funded, and broad. The default must be: if the institution assigns, directs, depends on, evaluates, publishes, grants, profits from, or reputationally consumes the work, the worker is paid and credited. Exceptions must be genuine exceptions: self-directed, time-bounded, non-substituting, transparent, freely revocable, not required for advancement, and paired with real access, authorship rules, training, and protection. No one should have to buy entry into science with invisible labor.

Mayer tapped the fake appointment letter.

"Would my voluntary position pass?"

"No."

"Good."

"You are relieved?"

"I am dead. I can afford standards."

That was the third wound, and the cleanest.

The word "voluntary" becomes dishonest when the alternative is disappearance. If the unpaid appointment is the only way to keep doing physics, the voluntariness is contaminated by exclusion. If the internship is necessary for the job, unpaid access is a toll. If the research assistantship is necessary for graduate admission, unpaid work is an admissions filter wearing a lab coat. If the title lets a university claim a scholar's output while refusing salary, it is not generosity. It is appropriation with a visitor badge.

But if an amateur brings independent observations, if a retired researcher wants an email address and a seminar room, if a community group shapes its own data project, if a student shadows for education rather than producing needed labor, then the moral situation changes. The test is not whether money appears. The test is power, dependency, substitution, benefit, and exit. A civilized institution can tell the difference. An uncivilized one will need policy, audits, and consequences to discover the difference.

"Consequences," Mayer said. "Now you sound like a nucleus again."

Physics celebrates consequences. That is one reason to keep waking physicists. In bad institutional ethics, everything is fog: passion, opportunity, mentorship, fit, culture, promise, flexibility. In physics, a force has effects. A potential has shape. A missing binding energy has consequences. A shell gap matters. If the university claims to be a meritocracy while making early work unpaid, the force field is not neutral. It bends trajectories toward people who can absorb unpaid time. If the lab claims it cannot afford salaries but can afford to depend on unpaid labor, it has defined certain people as consumables. If science says the best ideas should win while allowing money to decide who remains in the room, it has confused selection with evaporation.

Mayer held the buttons 2 and 8 in her palm.

"Do you know why people like magic numbers?" she asked.

"Because they sound like a secret."

"Because they are a pattern before they are an explanation. Humans love patterns before they deserve them."

That is also the danger of stories like hers. The institution sees the pattern: unpaid woman becomes Nobel laureate. It wants to infer the cheap lesson: genius will manage. The correct explanation is crueler: genius managed despite a stupid potential well. Change the potential.

The Nobel materials identify Mayer's prize with nuclear shell structure; the University of Chicago exhibit says her model explains why nuclei with magic numbers of nucleons form especially stable configurations, and it notes that she remained without a full professorship until 1960 ([Nobel Prize, "Maria Goeppert Mayer - Facts"](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1963/mayer/facts/); [University of Chicago Library exhibit](https://www.lib.uchicago.edu/collex/exhibits/binding-energy/prometheus-unbound-paper-trails-of-an-atom-age/maria-goeppert-mayer-elementary-theory-of-nuclear-shell-structure-1955/)). Those two facts should not sit politely beside each other. They should collide. One says the world eventually recognized the structure she found. The other says the world took a remarkably long time to recognize the structure she needed.

"And what structure did I need?" Mayer asked.

"A paid one."

"More."

"A scientific one."

"More."

"A world that did not treat your husband as an institution and you as an attachment."

She looked away then, toward the cemetery road, where the living drive too fast past the dead because they have appointments.

The anti-nepotism rules that harmed Mayer were defended as anti-corruption. That is worth remembering. Bad systems often wear the vocabulary of good systems. Rules against patronage can become rules against married women. Mentorship can become dependency. Training can become labor extraction. Excellence can become cultural cloning. Flexibility can become nonpayment. A title can become a cage with embossed letterhead. The moral task is not to hate rules. It is to ask whom the rules bind, whom they free, and what value moves through them.

"Now give me the verdict," Mayer said.

Here it is.

Science should abolish unpaid research labor as a normal path into the profession. It should not abolish voluntary curiosity, amateur contribution, community science, retired mentorship, or independent affiliation where the volunteer controls the work and the institution is not substituting free labor for paid labor. But any arrangement that produces institutional value, required credentials, publishable outputs, grant deliverables, teaching, lab maintenance, data analysis, supervision, or prestige should be paid, credited, bounded, and protected. The burden of proof belongs to the institution, not the hungry newcomer. "We are giving you opportunity" is not an argument until the opportunity includes the material conditions that let more than the already-cushioned accept it.

Mayer considered this, rearranging 126 until it stopped offending her.

"And the loopholes?"

"Keep cracks in the wall only while building doors."

"Too poetic."

"Fund transitional fellowships, visitor programs, paid internships, bridge appointments, childcare support, and independent-scholar access. Let unpaid exceptions exist only under strict conditions, visible records, and no career penalty for refusing them."

"Better."

"Also stop calling exploited people inspirational."

"Best."

To celebrate physics with Maria Goeppert Mayer is not to reduce a scientific life to grievance. That would be another theft. The point is the physics: a nucleus can have hidden order, stability can arrive at special numbers, a wrong dominant model can make the right question look ridiculous, and a tiny phrase from Fermi about spin-orbit coupling can unlock a structure no one could see clearly enough before ([APS News](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2008/08/goeppert-mayer-nuclear-shell-model); [Mayer, "Nuclear Configurations in the Spin-Orbit Coupling Model. I"](https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRev.78.16)). It is to love the discipline enough to ask what institutional physics has been acting on its people. What are the wells? What are the barriers? Where are the forbidden transitions? Which lives are stable, and which decay out before discovery?

Mayer lowered herself back into the grave.

"Remember," she said, "magic is what you call a pattern before you have done the work."

"And after?"

"After, you owe the pattern an explanation."

The buttons trembled. The shell closed. The chalk dust fell flat on the grass. The fake zero-dollar paycheck remained above the grave, embarrassing no one enough.

I left it there anyway.

Not as accusation against her. Against us.

Because love of physics is real, and because real love should make us less willing to feed living physicists into dead arrangements. Because a discipline that can explain nuclear stability can learn to recognize institutional instability. Because a system that depends on unpaid brilliance is not a meritocracy. It is a detector tuned to family money and endurance, occasionally surprised when genius crawls through the noise.

Maria Goeppert Mayer deserved more than a crack in the wall.

So do the living.
