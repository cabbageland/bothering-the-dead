today is 06-29.

I found Rachel Carson in Rockville, Maryland, under the kind of suburban green that has learned to look innocent.

NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information says Carson died of cancer on April 14, 1964, in Silver Spring, Maryland, and is buried at Parklawn Memorial Park in Rockville; the same short remembrance notes the public attacks she endured from chemical interests after *Silent Spring* made her the face of an environmental argument she did not live long enough to see become ordinary civic furniture ([NOAA NCEI, "Rachel Carson"](https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/news/rachel-carson)). A cemetery is already an archive of unfinished controversies, but hers seemed especially bad at staying dead. The grass over her had the tense neatness of a public hearing after all the witnesses have gone home.

I brought a jar of Chesapeake water, a paper wasp nest, a dead robin's feather, a balloon string from a canceled experiment, a pinch of volcanic ash labelled PINATUBO, the Paris Agreement, a copy of the Convention on Biological Diversity's Decision X/33, the National Academies report *Reflecting Sunlight*, and a small desk lamp whose shade I had painted the color of a poisoned sunrise.

The resurrection was not theatrical. Carson would have despised a stunt. I poured the Chesapeake water into the grass and read the passage where *Silent Spring* insists that when the public is asked to assume risks calculated by others, it must have the facts; Carson borrows Jean Rostand's sentence, "The obligation to endure gives us the right to know" ([Rachel Carson, *Silent Spring*, Internet Archive text](https://archive.org/stream/fp_Silent_Spring-Rachel_Carson-1962/Silent_Spring-Rachel_Carson-1962_djvu.txt)). Then I opened the National Academies report to its deliberately cold taxonomy: stratospheric aerosol injection would add reflective particles to the stratosphere, marine cloud brightening would add particles near low ocean clouds, and cirrus cloud thinning would alter high ice clouds to let more thermal radiation escape ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight*, Summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)).

The grass folded inward like a lung taking offense.

Carson sat up wearing cemetery soil on her sleeves and a look of absolute marine patience. Death had not made her sentimental. If anything, it had removed the polite delay between noticing stupidity and naming it.

"You brought me the sun in a jar," she said.

"More like a proposal to dim it."

"That is worse. A jar can be dropped."

The question was this: **Should humanity openly research and prepare solar radiation modification as an emergency climate tool, or should it reject the whole project as a planetary pesticide whose risks, governance failures, and moral corruption are inseparable from the technology itself?**

This is not a puzzle for people who enjoy saying "Anthropocene" in rooms with catering. It is a question about heat deaths, drought, monsoons, coral, crop yields, fossil power, Indigenous consent, patents, military suspicion, democratic legitimacy, and whether a species that could not govern smoke from smokestacks should be trusted with deliberate haze in the stratosphere. It is also a question about panic. Panic is sometimes the mind noticing reality faster than politics can digest it.

I began with the emergency, because any argument against solar geoengineering that skips the emergency becomes morally decorative.

Human-caused warming is not speculative. The IPCC's AR6 synthesis says human activities, principally greenhouse gas emissions, have unequivocally caused global warming, with global surface temperature reaching about 1.1C above 1850-1900 in 2011-2020 ([IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report, Summary for Policymakers](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/summary-for-policymakers/)). It also says approximately 3.3 to 3.6 billion people live in contexts highly vulnerable to climate change, and that from 2010 to 2020 human mortality from floods, droughts, and storms was fifteen times higher in highly vulnerable regions than in regions with very low vulnerability ([IPCC AR6 SPM](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/summary-for-policymakers/)). The Paris Agreement's famous temperature aim is to hold warming well below 2C and pursue efforts to limit it to 1.5C ([UNFCCC, Paris Agreement overview](https://unfccc.int/process-and-meetings/the-paris-agreement)). Yet the IPCC also says current nationally determined contributions make it likely warming will exceed 1.5C during the twenty-first century and that deep global emissions cuts are required this decade ([IPCC AR6 SPM](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/summary-for-policymakers/)).

"You are telling me the fever is real," Carson said.

"Yes."

"I did not rise from cancer to deny a fever."

Good. Then the pro-research case is this: if the world is failing to cut emissions fast enough, refusing to understand a possible temperature-lowering tool may be a luxury paid for by people least able to buy luxuries. The National Academies says solar geoengineering could reduce surface temperatures and potentially reduce some risks from climate change, even while warning it could introduce new risks to ozone, monsoons, ecosystems, agriculture, health, and politics ([National Academies, Summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). That is not a sales brochure. It is a loaded instrument panel. But when the plane is losing altitude, there is a difference between smashing the instrument panel and refusing to confuse it with a parachute.

Carson picked up the dead robin feather.

"You have made the classic industrial argument," she said. "First describe the damage. Then offer a more ambitious damage as treatment."

"That is unfair."

"It is historically excellent."

She had me there by the throat. *Silent Spring* was not anti-science. It was anti-concealment, anti-carelessness, anti-profiteering dressed as expertise. Carson's target was not chemistry as such; it was the social arrangement in which chemical power spread through air, soil, water, farms, and bodies before the public knew what had been done to it. Solar geoengineering raises the same pattern to the scale of sky. The public would be asked to endure a risk calculated by atmospheric specialists, states, philanthropies, companies, and security establishments, many of them located in countries with heavy historical responsibility for the problem.

"A planetary pesticide," she said.

"A bad slogan."

"A precise one. Pesticides promised targeted control too. Then the target grew roots, wings, rivers, milk, eggs, bodies."

The strongest anti-geoengineering position does not say climate change is fake or that technology is sinful. It says a technology that intentionally modifies planetary radiative balance cannot be governed justly enough to be legitimate. The Solar Geoengineering Non-Use Agreement initiative calls for governments to prohibit public funding for development, ban outdoor experiments, deny patents, refuse deployment, and oppose institutional normalization of solar geoengineering as a policy option ([Solar Geoengineering Non-Use Agreement, Open Letter](https://www.solargeoeng.org/non-use-agreement/open-letter/)). That is not mere romantic refusal. It is a theory of political containment: some tools become more dangerous when they become thinkable as normal policy.

"Knowledge is not neutral when institutions are hungry," Carson said.

"Ignorance is not neutral either."

That is the pro-research answer, and it is stronger than enthusiasts sometimes make it. Research is not automatically deployment. Knowing whether an intervention would disrupt rainfall, damage ozone, intensify regional inequity, or fail outright is valuable even if the final policy is prohibition. The National Academies recommended that the United States create a transdisciplinary solar geoengineering research program as a minor part of the larger climate response, focused on policy-relevant knowledge rather than advancing deployment, with transparency, public engagement, international coordination, and limits on technology with direct deployment applicability ([National Academies, Recommendation 4.1](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). It also recommends explicit "exit ramps" that would terminate research if risks become unacceptable or a technique appears unlikely to work ([National Academies, Summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)).

"Exit ramps," Carson repeated.

"Yes."

"Industry loves a ramp. It implies motion is already proper."

"Sometimes a ramp is also how you leave."

"Then build it before the engine starts."

This is why the debate cannot be divided into brave technologists and frightened pastoralists. The question is not "science or fear." The question is who designs the science, who sets its limits, who can stop it, who owns the hardware, who bears liability, who interprets harm, and who gets called irrational when they refuse consent. Harvard's Stratospheric Controlled Perturbation Experiment, SCoPEx, became a small version of the whole problem. The Keutsch Group's own page now says SCoPEx was canceled in March 2024 ([Keutsch Group, SCoPEx](https://www.keutschgroup.com/scopex)). Before that cancellation, the Saami Council opposed the planned Swedish test flights, saying there were no acceptable reasons to allow the project in Sweden or elsewhere and objecting to the broader legitimation of solar geoengineering ([Saami Council, petition and open letter page](https://www.saamicouncil.net/news-archive/support-the-indigenous-voices-call-on-harvard-to-shut-down-the-scopex-project)). The proposed flight would not have released particles at first, which makes the conflict philosophically useful. The fight was not only about physical harm. It was about authorization.

"A balloon with no particles still carries a future," Carson said.

"And a canceled balloon still leaves the climate problem untouched."

"Yes. Refusal is not repair."

This is the line too many anti-geoengineering arguments blur. Saying no to solar radiation modification does not cool a single overheated apartment, protect a coral reef, restore a glacier, compensate a flooded village, or cut a ton of CO2. A non-use agreement may block a dangerous class of interventions, but it cannot be allowed to become a ceremonial substitute for decarbonization, adaptation, finance, and loss-and-damage obligations. If "no geoengineering" means "keep the fossil economy while feeling morally pure about refusing the panic button," then it is only another tranquilizing pill.

Carson gave me a look that could peel varnish.

"Do not accuse the objector of purity because the arsonist has not bought a fire extinguisher."

Fair. More than fair.

The pro-deployment case, if stated without perfume, is ugly and serious. If warming drives cascading crop failures, lethal heat waves, ice-sheet instability, or mass displacement while emissions cuts remain too slow, a temporary reflective intervention might reduce peak temperatures and buy time. Paul Crutzen's 2006 essay on stratospheric sulfur injection explicitly framed the idea as a policy dilemma in the face of unsuccessful greenhouse gas mitigation efforts ([Crutzen, "Albedo Enhancement by Stratospheric Sulfur Injections"](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10584-006-9101-y)). The volcanic analogy is not fantasy: after the 1991 Pinatubo eruption, sulfur dioxide injected into the stratosphere formed aerosols that reduced incoming solar radiation, and USGS scientists describe global cooling of at least 0.5 to 0.7C in the following period ([USGS, "The Atmospheric Impact of the 1991 Mount Pinatubo Eruption"](https://pubs.usgs.gov/pinatubo/self/)). The physics of reflection is real. The politics of reflection is the nightmare.

"Volcanoes do not hold patents," Carson said.

"No. They also do not hold public hearings."

"And when a volcano injures the monsoon, whom do you sue?"

"No one."

"You propose improving on that by making the volcano intentional."

The National Academies itself gives the anti-deployment side some of its best ammunition. It says solar geoengineering is not a substitute for cutting greenhouse gases because it does not address rising greenhouse gas concentrations or ocean acidification, cannot reliably restore global or regional climate to some prior state, and would create a risk of catastrophically rapid warming if stopped after offsetting large warming without emissions reductions ([National Academies, Summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). The IPCC's ocean warnings make the point plainer: ocean warming and acidification have already harmed food production from fisheries and shellfish aquaculture in some regions, and future ocean acidification increases under continued CO2 emissions ([IPCC AR6 SPM](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/summary-for-policymakers/)). A dimmer sun does not unacidify the sea. It does not remove carbon from the air. It does not heal the cause. It can at most alter some symptoms while leaving the poison circulating.

"That is why I call it a pesticide," Carson said. "It may kill the visible pest while deepening the system that invited the pest."

"Heat is not a pest. It is a consequence."

"Then your pesticide is aimed at a consequence."

I hated the neatness of that.

But the refusal position also faces a hard counterquestion: what if the consequence becomes unbearable before the cause is repaired? The IPCC says some future changes are unavoidable or irreversible but can be limited by deep, rapid, and sustained emissions reductions; it also says the likelihood and impacts of abrupt or irreversible changes increase with further warming ([IPCC AR6 SPM](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/summary-for-policymakers/)). If the world blows past safer pathways, is it morally acceptable to refuse research that might reduce harm? "Might" is doing brutal work there. But the people exposed to extreme heat do not live inside philosophical tidiness. They live in buildings, fields, clinics, floodplains, and power grids.

Carson pressed the robin feather flat against her palm.

"Do you want deployment?"

"No."

"Do you want the option?"

"I want knowledge of the option under conditions that make the option harder to abuse."

"That is a lawyer's prayer."

"It may also be the only prayer law can answer."

The Convention on Biological Diversity's 2010 Decision X/33 is more interesting than either camp's slogans. It asks parties, in the absence of science-based, global, transparent, and effective control and regulatory mechanisms, to ensure that no climate-related geoengineering activities that may affect biodiversity take place until there is an adequate scientific basis and appropriate risk consideration; it carves out small-scale scientific research in controlled settings if justified by the need for specific data and subject to prior environmental assessment ([CBD Decision X/33](https://www.cbd.int/doc/decisions/cop-10/cop-10-dec-33-en.pdf)). That is not reckless permission. It is not absolute metaphysical refusal either. It is precaution with a laboratory door cracked open and a guard standing by it, looking worried.

"Precaution is not paralysis," I said.

"Correct," Carson said.

"So some research can be legitimate."

"Some. If the word 'research' has not been laundered into 'development.' If the people most exposed can refuse. If the funders cannot buy the question. If the purpose is to know whether to stop, not to make stopping impossible."

There it was: not anti-knowledge, anti-capture.

The problem with solar geoengineering is that capture is not a side risk. It is structurally invited. The technology is seductive because it appears fast, centralized, and comparatively cheap next to the dirty democratic labor of transforming energy, land, transport, buildings, agriculture, and consumption. It offers a thermostat fantasy to political systems built around avoiding sacrifice by the powerful. It whispers to fossil companies: continue extraction under a paler sky. It whispers to governments: reduce temperature headlines without redistributing power. It whispers to billionaires: perhaps history needs a heroic technician. It whispers to frightened citizens: someone else can handle the atmosphere.

Carson smiled without pleasure.

"A whisper is how poison enters a policy."

"But a ban whispers too," I said. "It tells citizens we can safely not know."

"No. A real ban would be loud about what must be done instead."

This is the standard by which both sides should be judged. Pro-research people must show that their work does not normalize deployment, weaken mitigation, privatize planetary choice, or convert vulnerable people into test subjects for elite desperation. Anti-use people must show that refusal is attached to material climate action, not only moral hygiene. The atmosphere will not honor anyone's purity certificate.

A serious governance regime would begin with prohibitions, not permission. No deployment now. No private outdoor experiments. No patents on deployment-enabling solar geoengineering technologies. No military classification of climate intervention research. No research program funded by fossil interests or by philanthropies allowed to set public priorities behind velvet ropes. No outdoor experiment without public registry, independent review, liability rules, environmental assessment, Indigenous consultation where relevant, and a veto structure with real teeth for affected communities. No model in which the Global North studies the sky and the Global South receives the weather.

"You are making a fence," Carson said.

"Yes."

"Fences are easier to move than graves."

"Then pour concrete."

The concrete must be emissions cuts. That is the nonnegotiable foundation. The National Academies says the research program should be only a minor part of the overall climate response and that rapid reductions of CO2 and other greenhouse gases remain the top priority ([National Academies, Summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25762/chapter/2)). The IPCC says near-term choices will have impacts now and for thousands of years, and calls the window for a livable and sustainable future rapidly closing ([IPCC AR6 SPM](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/summary-for-policymakers/)). Solar radiation modification must never become the corridor around that sentence.

Suppose, though, that some future council asks for emergency deployment. What conditions would make the request more than technocratic panic?

First, the emergency would have to be publicly defined in advance: measurable climate harms, failure modes, time limits, and a burden of proof that no less dangerous response could reduce near-term mass suffering. Second, deployment would have to be internationally authorized through institutions not controlled by the countries most responsible for historical emissions. Third, vulnerable states, Indigenous peoples, and affected regional publics would need formal power, not consultation theater. Fourth, emissions reductions and carbon removal would have to accelerate during any deployment, with automatic termination of the program if mitigation stalls. Fifth, liability and compensation would have to be established before deployment, not after the first altered drought. Sixth, the program would have to be temporary, reviewable, technically reversible in stages, transparent about uncertainty, and forbidden from becoming private property.

Carson listened, then asked the only question that mattered.

"Do you believe humans can build that?"

"I believe humans can write it."

"That was not my question."

No. It was not.

This is where the essay stops pretending that procedural ethics can save us by itself. The central objection to solar geoengineering is not that it is impossible to imagine good governance. The objection is that imagining good governance is one of the ways bad governance dresses for court. A constitutional design for the stratosphere may look beautiful on paper and still collapse when a heat emergency, a food shock, a war, a tech billionaire, or a great power decides the sky cannot wait for consent. The more plausible deployment becomes, the more every drought, cyclone, and flood can be politicized as evidence for or against the intervention. If it rains too little in one region, who is guilty? If it rains too much in another, who is believed? A managed sun could become the world's largest conspiracy engine, and not all suspicion would be irrational.

"Now you are hearing it," Carson said.

"The governance problem is not paperwork. It is trust."

"And the polluters have spent a century strip-mining trust."

Still, total ignorance is not trust either. We need atmospheric monitoring, climate modeling, volcanic analog research, social science, ethics, law, and public deliberation. We need to know enough to detect rogue deployment, enough to understand why deployment might fail, enough to prevent charlatans from selling sulfur as salvation, enough to help vulnerable countries evaluate proposals made in their name. The difference between research and development must be policed like a border during a plague. Crossings will happen. That is why the border has to exist.

Carson stood. The cemetery had become less green and more exact. Every blade of grass looked like it was taking notes.

"Your answer?" she said.

My answer: **solar geoengineering should not be deployed now and should not be normalized as a climate solution. It should be studied only under restrictive, public, internationally accountable governance designed as much to enable refusal as to generate knowledge.**

That means I reject the breezy techno-optimist version: no one gets to call the stratosphere an innovation platform. No one gets to sell a reflective sky as a substitute for ending fossil combustion. No one gets to move from models to outdoor experiments as if public consent were a scheduling inconvenience. The first duty is mitigation, adaptation, finance, and repair.

But I also reject the easy anti-research version: ignorance is not solidarity. Vulnerable people do not benefit from a taboo that leaves knowledge to militaries, billionaires, startups, and rumor. A democratic public cannot govern what only closed institutions understand. Carson's right to know cuts both ways. It gives the public the right to know what chemicals are in its fields and what aerosols might do in its sky. It also gives the public the right to say no after knowing.

Carson considered this with the severity of a tidepool.

"Then write the sentence carefully," she said.

"Which sentence?"

"Permission to know is not permission to do."

Yes. That is the sentence.

Permission to know is not permission to do. Solar geoengineering research, if allowed at all, must be organized around that distinction so fiercely that the distinction can survive ambition, money, fear, and heat. The purpose of research must be to make future publics less manipulable, not future deployment more inevitable. It must reveal risks, map injustice, test governance, expose failure, and strengthen the case for prohibition where prohibition is warranted. It must not become the polite childhood of a weaponized thermostat.

The resurrection began to thin. Carson brushed cemetery soil from her cuffs. The robin feather lifted in wind that I had not felt until then.

"When I wrote about poisons," she said, "I was accused of wanting insects to win."

"And now?"

"If I object to dimming the sun, I will be accused of wanting heat to win."

"Do you?"

"I want humans to stop arranging debates in which every answer preserves the original arrogance."

That was unfair in the way prophetic sentences are unfair: too large, too clean, and annoyingly useful.

Before she returned to the grave, she turned the little painted lamp toward the ground. Its poisoned-sunrise shade made the grass look briefly Martian.

"Do not let them call domination emergency medicine without proving the medicine, naming the domination, and asking the patient," she said.

"And if the patient is the planet?"

"Then stop pretending the doctor is outside the patient."

She lay back down.

The grass closed. The Chesapeake water disappeared into the soil. The balloon string tangled itself around the desk lamp. The pinch of Pinatubo ash sat in its vial, morally indifferent and atmospherically eloquent.

I left the cemetery with a rule, not a comfort: research narrowly, govern before doing, ban what cannot be governed, and cut emissions as if the sky were not available for further experiments. The dead are very tired of our cleverness. The living should be too.
