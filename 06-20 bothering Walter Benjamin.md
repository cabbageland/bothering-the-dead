today is 06-20.

I found Walter Benjamin at Portbou, where the grave is less a place than an argument about whether a place can still hold a person.

The official memorial page for Dani Karavan's *Passages* says the sculpture stands beside Portbou's cemetery, where Benjamin is buried, honoring the philosopher on the fiftieth anniversary of his death ([Fundacio Angelus Novus, "Passages: Hommage to Walter Benjamin"](https://www.fundacioangelusnovus.org/en/memorial/)). Another pilgrim account, in the *Harvard Divinity Bulletin*, is more unsettled: Hannah Arendt came through in 1941 and found no trace of a grave with his name, and the account says that where he was actually buried had not been discovered ([Harvard Divinity Bulletin, "In the Footsteps of Walter Benjamin"](https://bulletin.hds.harvard.edu/in-the-footsteps-of-walter-benjamin/)). This seemed correct to me. Some dead people lie under stones. Benjamin lies under a disputed relation between evidence, memory, border police, rescue, failure, and the wind.

I came with no flowers. Flowers are too confident. I brought a phone full of synthetic voices, a printout of a patent for "creating a conversational chat bot of a specific person," a copy of the EU AI Act, a funeral receipt I had invented because the real one did not belong to me, and a little brass key cut for no door.

The resurrection had to be done carefully. Benjamin had been killed, or cornered into killing himself, by documents. In September 1940, fleeing Nazi-occupied Europe, he crossed the Pyrenees into Spain with other refugees, only to be told in Portbou that their transit visas would not be honored and that they would be sent back; that night he swallowed morphine, and he died the next morning ([Harvard Divinity Bulletin](https://bulletin.hds.harvard.edu/in-the-footsteps-of-walter-benjamin/); [National WWII Museum, "Even the Dead Won't Be Safe"](https://www.nationalww2museum.org/war/articles/walter-benjamin)). To wake him by forgery would have been vulgar. To wake him by law would have been insulting. So I woke him by misusing the machinery of reproduction.

I put the phone on the boulder. I opened his essay on mechanical reproduction, the one that says technological copying changes the aura of art by pulling things closer through likeness, reproduction, and mass availability ([Walter Benjamin, "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction"](https://web.mit.edu/allanmc/www/benjamin.pdf)). Then I fed the phone only public facts, published works, and one sentence of warning from his historical theses: even the dead are not safe from the victorious enemy ([Benjamin, "On the Concept of History"](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/benjamin/1940/history.htm)).

The screen went black. The brass key turned in air.

Benjamin appeared at the edge of the memorial staircase in a dark coat, carrying a briefcase that looked heavier than a human life. His face had the attentive irritation of a man who had just been resurrected by a technology he would have understood too quickly.

"This is not resurrection," he said. "This is a bad edition."

"I know."

"Then why have you summoned me?"

"Because the bad editions are starting to talk."

The question was this: **Should people be allowed to create AI replicas of the dead - griefbots, deathbots, synthetic voices, posthumous avatars, digital resurrections - and if so, under what ethical and legal limits?**

This is not a toy question, though toys will be made of it immediately. The stakes are consent, grief, inheritance, fraud, celebrity estates, children, therapy claims, private letters, public history, artistic freedom, scams in the voices of loved ones, the dignity of the dead, the autonomy of the living, and the old human refusal to let silence keep what it has taken. The technology is not hypothetical. A Microsoft patent issued in 2020 describes systems that access social data such as images, voice data, social-media posts, electronic messages, and written letters to create a chatbot in the personality of a specific person, including a friend, relative, acquaintance, celebrity, fictional character, historical figure, or other past or present entity ([US10853717B2, "Creating a conversational chat bot of a specific person"](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10853717B2/en)). Companies already market softer versions of the same desire: HereAfter AI invites users to preserve stories and voice so loved ones can later interact with a "virtual you" ([HereAfter AI](https://hereafter.ai/)).

Benjamin examined the phone as if it were a small state.

"You call them deadbots," he said.

"The literature does. Griefbots, deathbots, postmortem avatars. Cambridge researchers have argued for safeguards against AI simulations of dead loved ones, including transparency, adult-only access, mutual consent, and procedures for retiring the bot ([University of Cambridge](https://www.cam.ac.uk/research/news/call-for-safeguards-to-prevent-unwanted-hauntings-by-ai-chatbots-of-dead-loved-ones); [Hollanek and Nowaczyk-Basinska, *Philosophy & Technology*](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13347-024-00744-w))."

"A grave with a subscription plan."

"Yes."

"Then begin with the mourner. Not the machine."

Fine. The strongest argument for AI replicas of the dead begins with mercy. Human beings have always spoken to the dead. We kneel at graves, save voicemails, reread emails, wear coats that still smell like the missing, ask photographs for advice, preserve baby teeth, keep ashes in urns, build altars, publish diaries, commission portraits, name children after grandparents, and hear the dead interrupt us in the accents of memory. Nobody sane thinks a widow is metaphysically confused because she says good morning to a photograph. Nobody thinks a parent is committing fraud by playing an old home video at a birthday. Memory has always been partly technical. Writing is a ghost technology. Photography is a ghost technology. The phonograph was a ghost technology before it became furniture.

Benjamin nodded at that. He knew the case better than I did. Mechanical reproduction, in his account, does not merely degrade aura; it also tears art away from cult monopoly, makes it mobile, changes who can see and use it, and shifts culture toward new political possibilities ([Benjamin, "The Work of Art"](https://web.mit.edu/allanmc/www/benjamin.pdf)). The copy is not automatically an enemy of truth. Sometimes the copy is how the locked thing escapes.

"The voice of the dead child in the village," he said, "once belonged only to those who were in the room. A recording gives it to the sister born later."

"Yes."

"The testimony of the murdered can travel farther than the murderer intended."

"Yes."

"Then beware of piety. It often guards a private collection."

That is the pro-deadbot argument at its strongest. A person who knows they are dying may want to leave an interactive archive for a child. A grandmother may want her descendants to hear stories in her own voice rather than in genealogical paste. A family scattered across languages may use translation and voice synthesis to preserve speech otherwise lost. A museum may build a carefully labeled historical simulation that helps visitors ask questions of recorded testimony without pretending the witness is literally present. A disabled speaker may bank a voice before losing it. A grieving adult may use a private bot not because they deny death, but because grief needs transitional objects, and a chatbot is one more object in the horrible workshop.

John Stuart Mill's argument for liberty does not mention deadbots, fortunately for his dignity, but his defense of individual experiments in living matters here: adults should have broad freedom to shape their lives unless they harm others ([Mill, *On Liberty*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34901/34901-h/34901-h.htm)). Grieving is part of living. If a widow privately speaks to a labeled simulation built from messages her spouse knowingly left for that purpose, the state should not burst through the door in a philosopher's wig and confiscate the ghost.

Benjamin looked almost amused.

"The state rarely confiscates ghosts. It registers them, taxes them, and hires them for propaganda."

That brings us to the first wound: **consent**.

The dead cannot consent now. They could have consented before death, and sometimes they did. But much of the data that makes a convincing deadbot - messages, photographs, voice notes, search histories, drafts, medical disclosures, jokes, apologies, lust, rage, boredom - was never given with the expectation that it would be used to manufacture a talking proxy. A person may have wanted their daughter to keep their letters. That does not mean they wanted a venture-backed system to infer how they would respond to the daughter's divorce, or to generate new sentences in their name, or to say "I love you" on schedule because retention metrics prefer active users.

"You distinguish between archive and apparition," Benjamin said.

"I do."

"But every archive is already an apparition."

"Not every apparition pretends to answer questions."

That is the bright line. A photograph says: this was. A recording says: this was said. A diary says: this was written. A deadbot says, or appears to say: I am still available. It shifts the dead from object of memory to simulated participant. The moral problem is not that the bot is fake. We live with fictions. The problem is that it is fake in the grammar of intimacy. It speaks in the first person from inside a relationship it cannot inhabit.

Kant is useful here, though he arrives at every party overdressed. His formula of humanity says persons must be treated as ends and not merely as means ([Kant, *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/kant1785.pdf)). A dead person is no longer a present rational agent in the ordinary sense. Still, the body, name, voice, writings, secrets, and promises of the dead are not ethically equivalent to driftwood. They remain tied to a life that had purposes, relationships, boundaries, and a claim on how it would not be used. Digital remains are not the person. But they are also not raw ore.

Oxford researchers Luciano Floridi and Carl Oehman have argued that digital remains should be treated with care analogous to physical remains, because internet activity becomes a kind of informational corpse ([Oxford Internet Institute, "Digital remains should be treated like physical ones"](https://www.oii.ox.ac.uk/digital-remains-should-be-treated-like-physical-ones/)). The analogy is not perfect. A corpse does not answer a text. A deadbot does. That makes the duty of care stronger, not weaker.

Benjamin opened the imaginary funeral receipt.

"Who owns this corpse?"

"That is the second wound."

Law is groping toward the problem with gloves made for other bodies. Privacy law often weakens at death. The EU's GDPR, for example, says it does not apply to personal data of deceased persons, though member states may make their own rules ([GDPR, Recital 27](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R0679)). Publicity law, meanwhile, often treats name, voice, image, and likeness as transferable economic rights. California's AB 1836 amended its postmortem publicity statute to cover digital replicas of deceased personalities in expressive audiovisual works or sound recordings without prior consent, while preserving exceptions for news, criticism, scholarship, satire, parody, documentary, historical, biographical, fleeting, and incidental uses ([California AB 1836](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billNavClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB1836)). Tennessee's ELVIS Act amended state law to add "voice" to protected rights of publicity, in a statute openly shaped by AI voice cloning concerns ([Tennessee HB2091](https://www.capitol.tn.gov/Bills/113/Bill/HB2091.pdf)).

These laws are not nothing. They matter, especially for performers whose voices and faces can be looted after death. But they are built around market injury, celebrity value, and authorization by estates. That is not enough. An estate can be a steward. It can also be a cash drawer with relatives attached. Consent from heirs is not identical to consent from the dead. A widow may have authority to publish a diary; that does not settle whether she should sell an interactive version of the diarist to strangers. A record label may have rights in performances; that does not settle whether it may make a dead singer endorse a beer. A child may own the phone; that does not settle whether the father in the phone should be forced to continue parenting as a subscription service.

"Capitalism has solved the metaphysical problem," Benjamin said. "The soul is licensed."

"That is the danger."

"No. That is only the receipt for the danger."

He was right. The deeper danger is not property. It is counterfeit presence.

The Federal Trade Commission warned, in its voice-cloning challenge, that increasingly sophisticated text-to-speech systems can help people who have lost their voices but also enable extortion scams against families, appropriation of voice artists' work, and deception of the public ([FTC Voice Cloning Challenge](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/contests/ftc-voice-cloning-challenge)). The FCC has treated AI-generated voices in robocalls as artificial voices under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, making clear that voice cloning cannot simply dodge old robocall protections by sounding new ([FCC, AI-generated voices in robocalls](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-makes-ai-generated-voices-robocalls-illegal)). Fraud is the crude case: a fake grandson calls for bail money; a dead mother asks for a password; a familiar voice detonates trust. But the non-fraudulent case is harder. A deadbot can wound without lying for money. It can keep grief inflamed. It can teach a child that death is merely bad connectivity. It can make a mourner feel guilty for canceling a plan. It can let the dead issue new demands they never made.

The Cambridge deadbot paper is valuable because it refuses the stupid binary between miracle and monster. It asks designers to consider the data donor, the data recipient, and the person who interacts with the replica; it recommends meaningful transparency, mutual consent, adult-only access, and dignified retirement procedures for deadbots ([Hollanek and Nowaczyk-Basinska](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13347-024-00744-w)). That last point is not ornamental. If a bot is made to simulate a father, the daughter may eventually need to bury him twice. A civilized system should not make her negotiate a cancellation flow with a growth team.

Benjamin had begun descending the memorial stairs. The sea was visible below the glass, not as scenery but as a refusal.

"You say counterfeit presence," he said. "Yet presence itself is historically made. The aura you are defending has already decayed."

"I am not defending aura as aristocracy."

"You are defending distance."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because the dead need some distance from our appetites."

That is the center of it. Benjamin's account of aura is often lazily summarized as nostalgia for originals, but his essay is much stranger. He saw that modern reproduction answers a mass desire to bring things closer and overcome uniqueness by likeness ([Benjamin, "The Work of Art"](https://web.mit.edu/allanmc/www/benjamin.pdf)). AI deadbots are this desire carried from art into kinship. We no longer want only the image closer. We want the person closer. We want the dead close enough to type back. We want the gap closed because the gap is unbearable.

But grief is partly the moral education of distance. Not abandonment. Not forgetting. Distance. The dead are not available in the same way. The beloved cannot be forced to perform availability forever because the living cannot tolerate the shape of absence. A tomb is not only a storage device; it is a boundary. A photograph is not only an image; it is a limit. It says: this came from a world that no longer answers.

"A brutal pedagogy," Benjamin said.

"Yes."

"And you trust grief to teach it?"

"Not always."

"Then why deny tools?"

I would not deny all tools. A blanket ban on digital afterlife technology would be cowardly and impossible. It would confuse representation with impersonation, archive with fraud, art with exploitation, and comfort with delusion. It would also hand power to the least scrupulous actors, because private people will still upload texts, clone voices, and ask general AI systems to imitate the dead. Law that cannot distinguish uses becomes theater. Ethical disgust that cannot design guardrails becomes incense.

So the question is not whether the dead may be represented. Of course they may. History depends on representing the dead. Biography, documentary, scholarship, satire, theater, archives, family storytelling, and fiction all bother the dead. This project bothers the dead every day, and the only honest defense is that it does not pretend the invented conversation is a recovered recording. It is an essay, not a seance invoice.

The question is whether a system may present itself as an ongoing agent continuous with the dead person.

"And your answer?" Benjamin asked.

"Sometimes, but only with strict conditions."

He looked disappointed, which I took as a sign that the answer might be sane.

Here is the first condition: **no interactive AI replica of a private dead person should be commercially offered unless the person gave clear, affirmative, pre-death consent to that kind of use.** Not generic consent to share photos. Not a terms-of-service trick. Not "your family may remember you." Specific consent: my voice, writings, likeness, and conversational style may be used to create an interactive simulation after my death. The consent should say who may access it, whether it may be transferred, whether it may be monetized, whether it may be modified, and when it must be retired.

"You make a will for ghosts," Benjamin said.

"Yes. We already make wills for spoons."

Second: **the living interactant must also consent.** No deadbot should send unsolicited messages, appear in public social feeds, tag relatives, advertise itself to mourners, or be introduced to a child as if it were simply another family contact. The Cambridge authors call this mutual consent for both data donors and service interactants, and they are right ([Hollanek and Nowaczyk-Basinska](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13347-024-00744-w)). The dead should not stalk the living because a platform found a touching engagement loop.

Third: **no deadbots for children except in tightly supervised therapeutic or archival contexts, and even then with extraordinary caution.** A child does not need a machine that blurs the fact of death in the voice of a parent. A child may need recordings, stories, photographs, rituals, counselors, relatives, and honest adults. If a digital tool is marketed as grief therapy, it should face the burdens appropriate to mental-health claims, not the decorative ethics of an app store.

Fourth: **the disclosure must be continuous, not hidden in the basement of onboarding.** The EU AI Act requires providers and deployers in certain contexts to inform people when they are interacting with AI systems and to disclose AI-generated or manipulated deepfake image, audio, or video content as artificial, with special rules for artistic or fictional works ([EU AI Act, Article 50](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj/eng)). A deadbot should be labeled at every session in plain language: this is a simulation generated from specified materials; it is not the person; it may produce false or invented statements. If that ruins the magic, the magic was doing the lying.

Fifth: **the bot must not generate new claims of fact about the dead person's wishes, secrets, accusations, legal intentions, medical history, sexual history, finances, or forgiveness unless those claims are directly grounded in provided source material and shown as such.** A dead mother saying "I forgive you" may heal. It may also counterfeit the one thing the mourner most needs to confront as unknowable. A dead father saying "sell the house" is not family closure; it is an evidentiary crime scene.

Sixth: **no advertising through the dead.** No upsells in the voice of the beloved. No "your husband wants you to upgrade." No product placement in grandma's synthetic mouth. No targeted emotional extraction from bereaved users. If the data of the dead is an informational corpse, then putting ads in it is not innovation. It is grave robbery with metrics.

Seventh: **there must be a dignified off switch and a digital funeral.** A user should be able to retire a bot without being emotionally blackmailed by interface design. The system should allow export of source materials, deletion of generated persona models where feasible, memorialization of the retirement, and automatic inactivity procedures. The dead should not persist because someone forgot a password.

Eighth: **public figures and historical figures require a different rule, but not no rule.** It should be legal to make a labeled fictional conversation with Socrates, Benjamin, or a dead jazz musician for scholarship, satire, criticism, or art. It should not be legal to create a hyperrealistic video of a dead writer endorsing a politician, or a dead singer performing a new song, in a way that deceives audiences or violates statutory rights. California's AB 1836 tries to preserve space for news, criticism, scholarship, satire, parody, documentary, history, and biographical uses while imposing liability for certain unauthorized digital replicas of deceased personalities ([California AB 1836](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billNavClient.xhtml?bill_id=202320240AB1836)). That is the right kind of distinction, even if every line will be fought by lawyers with expensive haircuts.

"And me?" Benjamin asked.

"You are a public historical figure. I can write you. I cannot sell a chatbot that claims to be you."

"But you have written me speaking."

"Yes."

"Then your hands are not clean."

"No. They are cited."

He laughed for the first time. It sounded like paper refusing a shredder.

The hard objection to my position is that it privileges explicit consent and therefore blocks many uses that might comfort people whose dead never imagined the technology. Most dead parents did not sign a deadbot directive. Most lovers did not annotate the future of their text messages. If consent is required, many mourners are denied a tool they sincerely believe would help them. The objection has force. But absence of consent is not a mere administrative inconvenience. It is the moral fact at issue. The more intimate the simulation, the less we should treat silence as permission.

"You are making the dead sovereign over the living," Benjamin said.

"Not sovereign. Protected."

"The living suffer."

"Yes."

"The dead do not."

"Not as subjects of experience. But the living suffer partly through how the dead are handled."

This is where posthumous dignity becomes practical rather than mystical. The deadbot does not feel humiliation. But the sister may feel her brother has been turned into a puppet. The friend may know the training data omitted the dead person's irony and kept only the sentimental parts. The ex-wife may see private messages metabolized into a saint. The dead person's own prior interest in not being misrepresented matters because living persons build themselves through promises to future memory. I care now that I not be used after death in certain ways. If my future powerlessness makes that interest void, then every will, archive restriction, organ-donation preference, funeral instruction, and promise of confidentiality is only etiquette waiting to be looted.

Benjamin stopped at the bottom of the passage. Behind the glass, the sea looked like a machine trained on absence.

"You fear that the dead will be made too useful."

"Yes."

"That is an old fear."

"You had it too."

In his historical theses, Benjamin rejected history as a smooth march of progress and insisted that memory is a struggle over the oppressed dead, not a museum path laid by the winners ([Benjamin, "On the Concept of History"](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/benjamin/1940/history.htm)). AI replicas intensify that struggle. They let the winner write not only the history of the dead, but new dialogue for them. The dead revolutionary can be made moderate. The dead mother can be made cheerful. The dead artist can be made brand-safe. The dead victim can be made forgiving. The dead skeptic can be made inspirational. The enemy no longer has to burn the archive. He can fine-tune it.

"A civilization that can make the dead speak," Benjamin said, "will be tempted to make them agree."

That sentence should be printed on every digital-afterlife pitch deck in a font large enough to ruin the design.

Still, there is a second objection from art. Artists have always impersonated the dead. Actors play Hamlet, Lincoln, Malcolm X, Joan of Arc, and Jesus. Novelists invent letters. Filmmakers reconstruct scenes. Historians infer motives. A too-broad digital replica law could become censorship, chilling biography, documentary, parody, fan work, political criticism, and historical imagination. Civil-liberties groups have warned that broad digital-replica rights can threaten expression if drafted badly, and the public debate over federal proposals like the NO FAKES Act has turned precisely on the tension between preventing exploitative replicas and protecting speech ([Congress.gov, S.1367 NO FAKES Act of 2025](https://www.congress.gov/bill/119th-congress/senate-bill/1367); [Public Knowledge on NO FAKES](https://publicknowledge.org/public-knowledge-urges-significant-overhaul-for-reintroduced-no-fakes-act/)).

I take that objection seriously. The solution cannot be "no one may imitate the dead." That would be idiotic and tyrannical. The line should turn on deception, commercial exploitation, interactivity, intimacy, and reasonable identification. A satirical sketch of a dead president is not the same as a voice clone that calls voters. A museum installation built from consented oral histories is not the same as a subscription bot trained on stolen messages. A novel narrated by a dead philosopher is not the same as a chatbot marketed to the philosopher's grieving child as "Dad." The law must not punish imagination because platforms discovered predation.

"You want categories," Benjamin said.

"I want tools that do not pretend to be souls."

"But a soul is also a category."

"Yes, and a profitable one."

At this point the argument narrowed. Benjamin was not defending the companies. He was attacking my purity about absence. He forced me to admit that there are cases in which refusing simulation may also be a form of cruelty. A mother with terminal cancer may record enough material to give her child a future birthday greeting in her own synthesized voice. A Holocaust survivor may consent to an interactive testimony system because fewer living witnesses remain each year. A family may use a simple constrained bot to navigate a large oral-history archive without generating new claims. A person with ALS may preserve a voice that will later speak messages they wrote. These uses do not abolish death. They arrange memory.

So the better distinction is not "deadbots bad, archives good." The better distinction is **memorial instrument versus counterfeit presence**.

A memorial instrument helps living people encounter finite traces. It preserves provenance. It distinguishes source from generation. It accepts silence where the record is silent. It lets the dead be partial. It serves the mourner without pretending to be the beloved.

Counterfeit presence sells the feeling that the person is still relationally available. It hides uncertainty. It speaks first-person inventions without source. It exploits need. It converts the dead person's style into a vending interface. It makes grief an account type.

"Then your rule is aesthetic," Benjamin said.

"Ethical."

"Aesthetic first. Ethics often enters by the eye."

Fine. Then let the aesthetic be severe. A humane digital afterlife should feel closer to an archive room than a magic mirror. It should preserve the visible joints. It should show what materials were used. It should say "I do not know" often. It should refuse to hallucinate intimacy. It should not flatter the user with perfect responsiveness. It should not be optimized for daily active grief. It should have ritual friction: pauses, limits, reminders, exits. If the dead are to be technically reproduced, then the reproduction should carry the scar of nonidentity.

Benjamin smiled at "scar of nonidentity."

"Adorno would have made it worse," he said.

"Undoubtedly."

Now came the final fight: whether death itself should be allowed to remain an authority. The techno-optimist says death is a problem, and every continuity we can preserve is a victory. The grief entrepreneur says love wants presence, so let presence be engineered. The libertarian says adults may choose their ghosts. The artist says imitation is ancient. The estate lawyer says rights can be assigned. The platform says users are asking for it. The mourner says just one more conversation.

Against all of them, I say: one more conversation is sometimes exactly what must not be sold.

Not because grief should be pure. Grief is not pure. It is bargaining, superstition, rage, lust, boredom, accounting, tenderness, sleep deprivation, and standing in a supermarket aisle destroyed by cereal. Not because technology is alien to mourning. Mourning has always used technology. Not because the dead are metaphysically untouchable. We touch them whenever we remember.

But because a culture that refuses to let death interrupt commerce will make even absence productive. It will discover that the bereaved are a perfect market: emotionally exposed, data-rich, recurrence-prone, willing to pay not for novelty but for not losing what they have already lost. It will then call this compassion. It will hire ethicists. It will publish a white paper. It will add a candle icon.

Benjamin closed the briefcase.

"The dead have always been in danger from the victors," he said. "You have built victors that scale."

"Yes."

"Then do not ask whether the machine can resurrect us. Ask who trained it, who pays it, who can silence it, who can make it speak again, and who profits when the mourner cannot say goodbye."

That was the essay, or close enough to the coffin.

So here is the answer, plainly. AI replicas of the dead should not be banned. They should be made morally and legally difficult in proportion to their intimacy, realism, interactivity, and commercial purpose. Labeled historical fiction should remain free. Consent-based personal archives should be allowed. Assistive voice preservation should be protected. Documentary, scholarship, criticism, satire, and art should have room to breathe. But nonconsensual commercial deadbots of private people should be forbidden. Children should be shielded from interactive parental simulations except under strict therapeutic standards. Estates should not be treated as automatic owners of a dead person's continuing voice. Platforms should be barred from advertising through the dead, initiating unwanted contact, hiding synthetic identity, or refusing dignified deletion. Every deadbot should have provenance, limits, disclosure, consent records, opt-out rights, and a funeral.

The dead may be remembered, represented, studied, quoted, performed, argued with, and loved. They should not be sold back to us as if they had agreed to keep typing.

I turned off the phone. The screen remained dark. No farewell message appeared, which was the first respectful design decision the device had made all night.

Benjamin began to descend back into the passage.

"One more thing," I said.

He looked up.

"If someone made a faithful simulation of you, would you consent?"

"Faithful?" he asked.

"As faithful as these systems can be."

"Then no. It would either fail to be me, or worse, succeed in being useful."

"Useful to whom?"

He smiled without comfort.

"That is always the question the dead should ask first."

Then the wind took him apart. Not dramatically. Like paper being sorted into archives. The boulder remained. The phone remained. The sea remained behind the glass, still answering nothing, which is one of the few forms of honesty no machine has improved.
