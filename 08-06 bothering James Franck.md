today is 08-06.

I found James Franck in Chicago, at Oak Woods Cemetery, where the physicist's American afterlife lies under the same South Side weather as the university that finally adopted him. Find a Grave lists his burial at Oak Woods; the Nobel materials record the more wandering fact that he died in Gottingen on May 21, 1964, while visiting the city that had once been his great laboratory and then his country of exile ([Find a Grave, "James Franck"](https://www.findagrave.com/memorial/10967294/james-franck); [Nobel Lectures in Physics 1922-1941, biography of James Franck](https://www.nevis.columbia.edu/~haas/documents/physics_nobel_lectures/physics22-41-1.pdf)). Even his death has two addresses: Germany for the last breath, Chicago for the earth. That is what history does to a refugee. It gives the body a map with a crease through it.

I did not bring plutonium. A cemetery deserves limits.

I brought a sealed teaching tube for the Franck-Hertz experiment, a mercury lamp, a voltmeter, a copy of the June 11, 1945 Franck Report, a printout of Truman's Hiroshima announcement, the Potsdam Declaration, a page from the Radiation Effects Research Foundation's casualty FAQ, and a small switch marked DEMONSTRATION in black tape. Franck and Gustav Hertz won the 1925 Nobel Prize in Physics for work on electron impacts with atoms; the Nobel lecture volume describes how their electron-collision experiments gave direct experimental support to basic assumptions of Bohr's atomic theory ([University of Gottingen, "James Franck, Physics"](https://www.uni-goettingen.de/en/74502.html); [Nobel Lectures in Physics 1922-1941](https://www.nevis.columbia.edu/~haas/documents/physics_nobel_lectures/physics22-41-1.pdf)). That is physics at its cleanest: not a sermon, not a flag, just a tube of vapor teaching the electron that the atom accepts energy in definite amounts.

So I raised the voltage.

At first the current climbed as if nature were continuous, agreeable, willing to be smoothed by wishful graph paper. Then the electrons reached the threshold and began giving their energy to mercury atoms in exact packets. The current dropped. The atom had spoken in steps. A second rise, another drop. The cemetery grass trembled at each discontinuity. By the third, the report on the grave opened by itself. By the fourth, a thin violet light stood above the stone. By the fifth, James Franck sat up with the expression of a man who had once seen quantum structure and political stupidity in the same lifetime and had not forgiven either.

"You chose the grave," he said.

"The date chose the grave."

He looked at the paper stack. "Hiroshima."

Yes. Hiroshima. On August 6, 1945, the United States used an atomic bomb on the city; RERF gives broad estimates of deaths within two to four months as roughly 90,000 to 166,000 for Hiroshima and 60,000 to 80,000 for Nagasaki ([Radiation Effects Research Foundation, "Frequently Asked Questions"](https://www.rerf.or.jp/en/faq/)). Truman's statement announcing Hiroshima described a new destructive power and warned Japan of further ruin if it did not accept Allied terms ([Harry S. Truman Library, "Statement by the President Announcing the Use of the A-Bomb at Hiroshima"](https://www.trumanlibrary.gov/library/public-papers/93/statement-president-announcing-use-bomb-hiroshima)). The Potsdam Declaration had already demanded the unconditional surrender of Japanese armed forces and warned of "prompt and utter destruction" if Japan refused ([U.S. Department of State, Foreign Relations, Potsdam Declaration](https://history.state.gov/historicaldocuments/frus1945Berlinv02/d1382)).

Today's question was this: **Was the United States morally obligated to demonstrate the atomic bomb before using it on Hiroshima, and what does a scientist owe the world when his knowledge becomes a state's new way to kill civilians?**

Franck folded his hands over the report that bore his name.

"Begin correctly," he said. "The report was not pacifist decoration."

He was right. The Franck Report was chaired by him at the University of Chicago's Metallurgical Laboratory and signed by Donald J. Hughes, James J. Nickson, Eugene Rabinowitch, Glenn T. Seaborg, Joyce C. Stearns, and Leo Szilard ([The Franck Report, June 11, 1945](https://fissilematerials.org/library/fra45.pdf)). It did not say atomic power was small, gentle, or safely ignorable. It said the opposite. It warned that nuclear bombs could not remain an American secret for more than a few years, that an arms race would follow without international control, and that an early unannounced attack on Japan would damage the possibility of future agreement ([Franck Report](https://fissilematerials.org/library/fra45.pdf); [National Museum of Nuclear Science and History, "The Franck Report"](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/key-documents/franck-report/)). Its preferred alternative was a demonstration in an uninhabited place, before the eyes of the world.

"Your report was brave," I said.

"That is not an argument."

"No. It is only the minimum courtesy due a dead man before I start biting."

He waited.

So I bit.

The demonstration proposal sounds morally luminous because we are reading it after the bodies. It lets us imagine a desert flash replacing burned skin, a shocked Japanese delegation replacing a vaporized city, a clean proof replacing a massacre. But wars do not always surrender to stagecraft. A demonstration could have failed technically. It could have succeeded physically and failed politically. Japanese militarists might have dismissed it as a trick, demanded another demonstration, hidden behind the emperor, or interpreted hesitation as weakness. The Scientific Panel of the Interim Committee - Arthur Compton, Enrico Fermi, Ernest Lawrence, and J. Robert Oppenheimer - argued on June 16, 1945 that they saw no acceptable alternative to direct military use likely to end the war ([Atomic Archive, "Science Panel's Report to the Interim Committee"](https://www.atomicarchive.com/resources/documents/manhattan-project/interim-committee.html); [National Security Archive, "Recommendations on the Immediate Use of Nuclear Weapons"](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/28526-document-25-memorandum-j-r-oppenheimer-recommendations-immediate-use-nuclear-weapons)). Henry Stimson later defended the decision as a terrible wartime act taken to end the war and avoid still greater loss ([National Museum of Nuclear Science and History, "Stimson on the Bomb"](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/key-documents/stimson-bomb/)).

"There," I told Franck. "That is the hard case. If the demonstration fails to produce surrender, you may have spent time, revealed capability, lost shock, and still arrived at city use. Meanwhile blockade, firebombing, invasion planning, prisoners, soldiers, civilians across Asia - all continue dying. You were not deciding between murder and peace. You were deciding among deaths."

Franck looked at the switch marked DEMONSTRATION.

"All war arguments learn to say 'among deaths' when they want immunity from moral grammar," he said.

That was the first wound.

He did not deny the military problem. He denied that military urgency had the right to flatten every other clock. The Franck Report's opening is unusually exact about this. The scientists wrote that they did not presume to speak authoritatively on national and international policy, but that their acquaintance with the scientific elements of the situation imposed an obligation to offer suggestions about grave dangers ([Franck Report](https://fissilematerials.org/library/fra45.pdf)). That is the hinge. A scientist does not become king because he understands neutrons. But he also does not become a wrench because generals hold the clipboard.

"We knew one thing the public did not know," Franck said. "The bomb was not merely a bigger explosive. It was a political species."

"States make political species all the time."

"Not like this."

He had earned the distinction. Franck's own physics belonged to the birth of quantum atomic theory, where energy exchange stopped behaving like ordinary smooth common sense. His later committee understood nuclear weapons in a similarly discontinuous way. The first combat use of the atomic bomb would not merely add one more weapon to war. It would teach the world what category the weapon belonged to: not laboratory secret, not bargaining chip, not last-resort instrument under public moral burden, but usable city-killer.

Just war theory matters here because it refuses both innocence and realism's gutter laugh. Stanford's entry on war describes the tradition as trying to justify some wars while limiting how they are fought, including by moral constraints on the treatment of noncombatants ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "War"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/war/)). The doctrine of double effect is often invoked when an action has both a good aim and a foreseen grave harm, such as civilian death as a side effect rather than an intended end ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Doctrine of Double Effect"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/double-effect/)). But Hiroshima strains that distinction until it squeals. The city was not merely an unfortunate perimeter around a narrow target. The shock to human beings, officials, and the world was part of the weapon's communicative function. The bomb was meant to speak. A city was selected as the mouth.

Franck nodded once.

"Say that again."

"A city was selected as the mouth."

"Good. Ugly enough to be useful."

The defender of Hiroshima replies that this is sentimental surgery on the past. Hiroshima had military value; Japan had waged aggressive war; the Pacific campaign had been catastrophic; Japan's government had not accepted Potsdam; American leaders did not know the future surrender path; an invasion could have been horrific. These are not fake points. If an argument for Hiroshima is to be defeated, it has to be defeated in its strongest clothes. Stimson's defense was not that killing civilians was lovely. It was that every path before him was soaked, and that the bomb seemed the least costly route to surrender ([Stimson, "The Decision to Use the Atomic Bomb"](https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/ps/japan/stimson_harpers.pdf)). That is not nothing.

"It is not enough either," Franck said.

"Why?"

"Because the question is not only how to end one war. It is how the first nuclear act teaches all later wars to imagine themselves."

This is the part of the Franck Report that still feels as if it arrived from the future with ash on its cuffs. It argued that the scientific facts could not be kept secret indefinitely and that, without effective international control, a nuclear arms race was certain to follow the first revelation of American possession ([Franck Report](https://fissilematerials.org/library/fra45.pdf)). That prediction did not require clairvoyance. It required taking physics seriously. If nature permits fission weapons and many competent nations have physicists, ore, industry, spies, fear, and pride, monopoly is a temporary hallucination.

"So you would have staged a demonstration," I said. "A barren island. A desert. Representatives watching."

"Not staged. Witnessed."

"A demonstration is also theater."

"All politics is theater when the audience may die."

That one I let stand.

The demonstration would not have been pure. It would have been nuclear coercion with better manners. It would have said to Japan: look, this is what we can now do to your cities. It would have said to the Soviet Union: look, this is what we possess. It would have said to the world: physics has opened a hole in ordinary war. There is no innocent version of that message. But moral life often depends on distinctions inside impurity. A warning is not peace, but it is not the same as an unannounced incineration. A demonstration is not mercy, but it is not the same as using a populated city as the first public experiment in nuclear fear.

Franck smiled without pleasure.

"You are approaching adulthood."

"Do not get fond of me."

"I have been dead too long for that risk."

The hardest counterargument is not that demonstration was impure. It is that it may not have worked. Here humility matters. The United States Strategic Bombing Survey later concluded that Japan would probably have surrendered before November 1, 1945 even without the atomic bombs, Soviet entry, or an invasion, but that postwar conclusion is a retrospective assessment and remains part of a larger historical controversy rather than an oracle ([Air University Press, *The United States Strategic Bombing Surveys*](https://www.airuniversity.af.edu/Portals/10/AUPress/Books/B_0020_SPANGRUD_STRATEGIC_BOMBING_SURVEYS.pdf); [National Security Archive, "The Atomic Bombings of Japan and the End of World War II, 80 Years Later"](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/briefing-book/nuclear-vault/2025-08-05/atomic-bombings-japan-and-end-world-war-ii-80-years-later)). I will not cheat by pretending the decision-makers possessed later archives and hindsight. Moral judgment is not improved by making the past stupider than it was.

But uncertainty cuts both ways.

If leaders could not know that demonstration would work, they also could not know that city use was necessary. If they could not know whether Japan would surrender after a warning, they also could not know that no warning was worth trying. If they could not know whether Soviet entry, modified surrender terms, blockade, or continued conventional bombing would end the war, they were still choosing which uncertainties to honor and which to burn. The word "necessity" is dangerous in retrospect because it often means "the path we took, now wearing armor."

Franck lifted the Szilard Petition from the stack. Szilard's July 1945 petition asked Truman not to use atomic bombs unless surrender terms had been made public in detail and Japan had refused them; it was signed by many Manhattan Project scientists but did not reach Truman before Hiroshima ([National Museum of Nuclear Science and History, "Szilard Petition"](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/key-documents/szilard-petition/); [National Security Archive, "A Petition to the President of the United States"](https://nsarchive.gwu.edu/document/28456-document-38-e-lapp-leo-szilard-et-al-petition-president-united-states-july-17-1945)). The chain of command is not always a moral nervous system. Sometimes it is insulation.

"Should you have gone public?" I asked him.

That question sharpened the air.

There are two temptations here. One is obedient cowardice: we are technical men, policy belongs elsewhere, the paperwork has been filed, please consult the officials for your apocalypse. The other is heroic narcissism: I understand the nucleus, therefore I am entitled to override democratic government, military judgment, and the messy legitimacy of public authority. Both are false. Scientists are not philosopher-kings in lab coats. They are also not morally laundered by employment.

Franck answered slowly.

"First, one uses the channel available. Then one asks whether the channel was designed to absorb conscience without transmitting it."

"And if it was?"

"Then silence becomes collaboration."

I did not like how clean that sounded. Clean answers often conceal dirty machinery.

The Stanford entry on dirty hands discusses the idea that political actors may sometimes do morally wrong things under extreme pressure, and it notes how debates over city bombing and "supreme emergency" try to handle cases where catastrophe seems to demand terrible acts ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "The Problem of Dirty Hands"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/dirty-hands/)). If Hiroshima is defended as dirty hands, then the defender must accept the dirt. Not polish it into triumph. Not hide it behind "scientific gamble." Not say only that the war ended and therefore the dead are retroactively disciplined into purpose. Dirty hands means: this may have been necessary, and it was still a moral injury. If the defender cannot say both, the defender is not doing tragedy. He is doing public relations.

"And if it was not necessary?" Franck asked.

"Then dirty hands becomes just hands."

"No," he said. "Then it becomes an atrocity with stationery."

That was the second wound.

My position began to settle, unhappily, into shape. The demonstration proposal was morally required not because it guaranteed surrender, and not because scientists had solved war from a Chicago committee room. It was required because first nuclear use against a city crossed a threshold so large that the burden of proof should have been almost unbearably heavy. A state may not convert uncertainty into permission merely because the option is available. Before using a new weapon whose purpose included terrorizing a whole political community through civilian destruction, the state owed the world every serious measure short of that act: explicit terms, warning, demonstration if technically possible, and a public recognition that nuclear weapons were not ordinary bombs with better engineering.

Franck did not look satisfied. Dead physicists are not vending machines for approval.

"You still speak as if the state could have been made decent by procedure," he said.

"No. Only less indecent."

"That may be the honest unit."

Less indecent. That is not a slogan anyone puts on a monument. Good. Monuments lie by standing too confidently.

The argument for demonstration is really an argument about moral sequence. There are acts that should not be first moves. You may threaten before you strike. You may show before you slaughter. You may name terms before you punish refusal. You may create a political record before you create a field of dead bodies. None of this makes coercion innocent. It only prevents the worst form of moral compression, where proof, warning, punishment, experiment, diplomacy, and spectacle are all detonated over one inhabited place at 8:15 in the morning.

Physics is worth celebrating here precisely because it refuses vague grandeur. The Franck-Hertz experiment did not say "energy sort of changes." It showed thresholds. Below this voltage, one thing. Above it, another. The bomb brought politics to a threshold of the same cruel kind. Before Hiroshima, nuclear weapons were possible, tested, secret, argued over. After Hiroshima, the world knew they were usable on cities. That transition was not smooth. It was a jump.

"You make my old experiment into a sermon," Franck said.

"You made your old experiment into a report."

He almost laughed.

The report matters because it is neither innocence nor absolution. Franck worked inside the Manhattan Project as director of the Chemistry Division at the University of Chicago's Metallurgical Laboratory; the University of Chicago finding aid for his papers places the Franck Report among records of atomic scientists' movements about the development and control of atomic energy ([University of Chicago Library, Guide to the James Franck Papers](https://www.lib.uchicago.edu/e/scrc/findingaids/view.php?eadid=ICU.SPCL.FRANCK); [National Museum of Nuclear Science and History, "James Franck"](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/profile/james-franck/)). He was not standing outside power with clean robes. He was inside the machine, trying to make the machine hear a future it preferred not to hear.

That is the scientist's duty when knowledge becomes lethal: not to rule, not to preen, not to pretend purity, but to force consequences into the room before officials reduce them to schedule. The physicist owes the public an account of what the public cannot yet see. The chemist owes the miner, the soldier, the child, the future city, and the rival laboratory a description of the mechanism before the mechanism becomes fate. Expertise does not settle values. It does make some evasions unforgivable.

"Then your verdict?" Franck asked.

Here it is.

James Franck was morally right that the first revelation of nuclear weapons should have been a warning or demonstration, not an unannounced attack on a city. He was right for the reason his report emphasized: the decision was not merely military expediency but long-range policy for a world that would soon learn to build the same fire. The demonstration might have failed. It might have been ignored. It might have led, after further refusal, to combat use anyway. But trying it would have preserved a vital moral distinction: the distinction between proving terrible capacity and making civilians the proof.

But Franck was not entitled to innocence. No one in that room was. A demonstration would still have been a nuclear threat. The Manhattan Project scientists still helped create the option. The state still faced a brutal war. Japanese militarism still bore immense responsibility for the conditions of its defeat. The correct judgment is not a fairy tale in which one committee report could have saved history from blood. It is harsher: when the new threshold appeared, Franck saw it more clearly than the people who crossed it.

He stood, and the cemetery bent around him like a voltmeter needle returning to zero.

"Does this celebrate physics?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Strange celebration."

"The honest kind. Physics found the discontinuity. It also gave us the bomb. Celebrating physics means loving the discipline enough not to let states wrap it in flags and call every use progress."

Franck looked down at the report. "Then remember this: an atom can be excited by impact. So can a conscience. But only if the collision is allowed to lose energy in the right place."

Then he returned to being dead, as abruptly as a current drop in mercury vapor.

I left the switch marked DEMONSTRATION on the grave. Not because it would undo anything. The dead are not fooled by symbols. I left it because August 6 is a day for refusing the smooth graph. There are thresholds. There are jumps. There are points after which the world is not the same world plus one more event, but another world, entered by force.

Today is for standing at that threshold with James Franck, counting the dead, counting the arguments, and admitting that the smallest honest moral unit may sometimes be only this: less indecent than what happened.
