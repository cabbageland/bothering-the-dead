today is 10-09.

I found Steven Weinberg in a grave dug for an unfinished machine.

Not a graveyard, though Texas has room for those. This one was under Waxahachie, in the fossil corridor of the Superconducting Super Collider, where a country once began to cut an enormous circle into the earth and then changed its mind. The dead do not always lie under stones. Sometimes they lie under appropriations bills, cost overruns, committee minutes, canceled magnets, and a tunnel that still feels embarrassed to exist.

The body was not exactly Weinberg's body. It was a long clean equation chalked on the inside of a concrete wall, with dust in the Greek letters and a dry moth asleep in the minus sign. Nearby someone had left a hard hat, a coil of cable, and a photocopy of a federal report. The report said that after about $1.57 billion had been spent, Congress terminated the Superconducting Super Collider on October 28, 1993 ([U.S. Department of Energy Office of Inspector General, 1996](https://www.energy.gov/sites/prod/files/2015/09/f26/DOE-OIG-0389.pdf)). The Government Accountability Office had warned earlier that the SSC was over budget and behind schedule, and that its total construction cost was expected to exceed $11 billion ([GAO, "Superconducting Super Collider's Total Estimated Cost Will Exceed $11 Billion"](https://www.gao.gov/products/t-rced-93-57)).

It was a suitable tomb for Weinberg, which is not the same as a fair one.

He was one of the great theoretical physicists of the twentieth century, dead since July 23, 2021, in Austin, Texas; his 1967 paper "A Model of Leptons" helped build the electroweak theory that unified electromagnetism and the weak interaction in one framework ([Physics Today obituary](https://physicstoday.aip.org/obituaries/steven-weinberg); [Weinberg, "A Model of Leptons," 1967](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.19.1264)). The Nobel committee later gave Weinberg, Sheldon Glashow, and Abdus Salam the 1979 physics prize for contributions to the unified weak and electromagnetic theory, including the prediction of the weak neutral current ([Nobel Prize, 1979 Physics](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1979/summary/)). Weinberg also became one of the sharpest public defenders of fundamental physics as a human enterprise too large, too strange, and too long-arc to be judged only by near-term usefulness; in "The Crisis of Big Science," he argued that the Standard Model was successful but incomplete, lacking gravity and dark matter among other things ([Weinberg, "The Crisis of Big Science"](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2012/05/10/crisis-big-science/)).

To wake him, I did not pray. Prayer seemed jurisdictionally confused.

I drew a circle in chalk around the equation, then another circle around that, then a third, because particle physicists are apparently comforted by circular expenditure. I placed the hard hat in the center and said the old sacramental words of public science:

"The cost has increased."

The tunnel inhaled.

Dust lifted from the chalk. The equation brightened, first like a screen, then like a wound. The moth climbed out of the minus sign, shook concrete dust from its wings, and flew straight into the hard hat, which rang once. Weinberg stepped through the wall in shirtsleeves, looking less like a ghost than like a professor inconvenienced by metaphysics.

"If you have resurrected me to complain about funding," he said, "at least make the complaint intelligent."

"I will try."

"That is what everyone says before a budget meeting."

The controversial topic was already humming in the dead tunnel: **should humanity build the next giant particle collider, especially CERN's proposed Future Circular Collider, or has high-energy physics reached the point where its biggest machines are no longer morally, politically, and scientifically justified?**

This is not a cute argument about nerd toys. CERN describes the Future Circular Collider as a possible 91-kilometer underground machine beneath France, Switzerland, and Lake Geneva, with an electron-positron first stage to study the Higgs boson in unprecedented detail and a later proton-proton collider that could reach about 100 TeV ([CERN FCC overview](https://home.cern/science/accelerators/future-circular-collider/)). CERN's 2025 feasibility announcement estimated the construction cost of the first electron-positron stage, including tunnel and infrastructure, at 15 billion Swiss francs over about twelve years, while stressing that no commitment to build had yet been made ([CERN, FCC feasibility report announcement](https://home.cern/cern-releases-report-feasibility-possible-future-circular-collider/)). The 2026 European Strategy update recommended the FCC-ee as Europe's preferred next flagship collider project after the High-Luminosity LHC ([European Strategy for Particle Physics](https://europeanstrategy.cern/european-strategy-for-particle-physics/)).

"So," I said, "a ninety-one-kilometer question mark."

"A precise question mark," Weinberg said.

"A fifteen-billion-franc question mark."

"Precision is expensive."

"So are hospitals, wetlands, schools, heat pumps, public health labs, cheap housing, and not cooking the biosphere."

He regarded me with the calm of a man who had watched the weak force and electromagnetism stop pretending to be strangers.

"Begin with the accusation."

Fine.

The accusation is that big collider physics has learned to call uncertainty a mandate. The Higgs boson was found. The Standard Model survived. Supersymmetric particles did not arrive on schedule like well-behaved theoretical pets. The LHC has given physicists a huge achievement and a humiliating silence: no obvious new zoo beyond the Higgs. Now the field proposes a larger machine, partly to measure the Higgs more precisely, partly to search for deviations, partly because energy and luminosity are how this civilization asks matter to confess. Critics such as Sabine Hossenfelder have argued that a bigger collider has unclear discovery prospects, high opportunity cost, and little societal relevance compared with other urgent research needs ([Hossenfelder, "The World Doesn't Need a New Gigantic Particle Collider"](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-world-doesnt-need-a-new-gigantic-particle-collider/)). Nature, more diplomatically, has warned that skepticism about cost and scientific return should not be treated as betrayal of science ([Nature, "Agree to disagree on plans for the next European supercollider"](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-019-00234-6)).

"There," I said. "The prosecution rests its opening sneer."

"Sneers do not rest," Weinberg said. "They loiter."

His defense began with the most annoying possible word: history.

In 1967, Weinberg's electroweak paper was only a few pages long, theoretical, and strange enough that its future experimental glory was not obvious from the size of the object on the page ([Weinberg, "A Model of Leptons"](https://doi.org/10.1103/PhysRevLett.19.1264)). Decades later, the LHC's ATLAS and CMS experiments announced a new particle around 125 GeV consistent with the Higgs boson, the very kind of particle needed in the symmetry-breaking story that made electroweak theory work ([ATLAS, "The Higgs boson: a landmark discovery"](https://atlas.cern/Discover/Physics/Higgs); [CMS, "Observation of a New Particle with a Mass of 125 GeV"](https://cms.cern/index.php/physics/higgs-boson/observation-new-particle-mass-125-gev)). If one had demanded that fundamental theory justify itself in immediate applications, the intellectual bridge from chalk to detector would have looked like an indulgence until it looked like civilization.

"You are smuggling in the Higgs as a saint," I said.

"No. As evidence."

"Evidence that sometimes a giant machine finds the thing it was built to find. Not evidence that the next one will."

"Of course not. Science is not a vending machine. That is why your consumer-protection tone is misplaced."

That was the first cut.

The public defense of basic science often sounds dishonest because it puts the wrong goods in the shop window. Medical imaging, the Web, superconducting magnets, advanced detectors, training, computation: all real, all worth noting. CERN itself says collider technology has applications in medical imaging, hadron therapy, aerospace, security, environmental protection, and cultural heritage ([CERN FCC overview](https://home.cern/science/accelerators/future-circular-collider/)). But if the only moral defense of fundamental physics is spinoffs, then the truth has to sneak into the budget wearing a lab coat labeled "economic development." That is undignified. Worse, it is false to the actual desire.

"Say the actual desire," Weinberg said.

"You want to know what matter is."

"Better. We want to know what the world is made of and why its rules have this form rather than another."

"That is not a public argument by itself."

"It is the beginning of one."

He was right, which made him more irritating. Vannevar Bush's 1945 report to the president defended basic research as research performed without thought of practical ends and argued that scientific progress was tied to health, prosperity, and security ([Bush, *Science, the Endless Frontier*](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GOVPUB-PR32_400-e7966ee70a4f7b47f862431c9776f727/html/GOVPUB-PR32_400-e7966ee70a4f7b47f862431c9776f727.htm)). That argument built much of the postwar American imagination of science: let free inquiry explore, because the harvest cannot be predicted by the seed catalogue. But Bush's frontier language also hides something. Frontiers are not innocent. They imply expansion, appetite, and a certain allergy to asking who pays for the wagon.

"This is where your side gets sloppy," I told him. "You invoke the endless frontier, then act surprised when the public asks for a map, a price, and a reason it should trust the expedition."

"The public should ask."

"And if the answer is 'because physicists want it'?"

"Then the answer is insufficient."

Good. A live physicist might have tried harder to escape. The dead, when honestly revived, have less patience for departmental slogans.

The opposition case has several teeth. The first is opportunity cost. The U.S. P5 report had to rank particle physics priorities under constrained budgets, endorsing the HL-LHC, DUNE, Rubin Observatory, dark matter searches, cosmic microwave background work, smaller agile experiments, theory, instrumentation, computing, and collider R&D rather than one single appetite swallowing the field ([2023 P5 Executive Summary](https://www.usparticlephysics.org/2023-p5-report/executive-summary.html)). That is the adult picture: science is not one cathedral; it is an ecosystem. A huge collider can be a great instrument and still become a gravitational body that bends money, talent, attention, and political legitimacy around itself.

"A field that builds only one kind of machine becomes stupid in a specialized way," I said.

"Agreed."

"That was too easy."

"Because it is true. A collider is not a substitute for neutrino physics, cosmology, dark matter experiments, table-top precision work, or theory."

"Then do not let collider advocacy speak as if the alternative to FCC is barbarism."

"Do not let collider criticism speak as if the alternative to FCC is feeding orphans with saved francs."

Second cut. Fair.

Public budgets are not household jars. A franc not spent at CERN is not automatically reborn as a hospital bed, a wetland, or a heat pump. It may become a tax cut, a road, a subsidy, a weapons system, a deficit line, a consultant's smile. Opportunity cost is real, but lazy opportunity cost is a morality play with fake accounting. Still, the fact that money is politically messy does not absolve scientists from moral comparison. If a project asks for billions, "other spending is also imperfect" cannot be the whole defense.

The second tooth is environmental and local. CERN says the FCC feasibility process studied territorial compatibility, environmental constraints, and local engagement; it also says construction could excavate about 16.4 million tonnes of material, with strategies proposed for reuse and treatment ([CERN FCC local area](https://home.cern/science/accelerators/future-circular-collider/local-area/)). A tunnel is not an abstraction to the people above it. "Fundamental knowledge" is a poor lubricant for democratic consent if residents experience it as trucks, shafts, noise, dust, property anxieties, and a polite international machine explaining that their inconvenience is cosmological.

"Do not make peasants of the neighbors," I said.

"I would not."

"Institutions can."

"Yes."

That mattered. Political legitimacy is not secured merely by expert confidence. Philosophical accounts of public reason treat public justification as central when collective rules and burdens are imposed on plural citizens ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Public Reason"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/public-reason/)). A megaproject that rearranges land, money, labor, and environmental risk owes reasons not only to physicists, not only to ministers, and not only to people who already think the Higgs is adorable. It owes reasons to the bored, suspicious, worried, and unimpressed.

Weinberg walked deeper into the tunnel. A line of ghost magnets lit above us, one by one, curving away into the buried circle.

"You think I will defend prestige," he said.

"I think prestige is the mold that grows on good arguments when they sit too long in funding rooms."

"Then scrape it off."

So we did.

The strongest pro-collider argument is not "Europe must lead," though coordination matters. It is not "technology transfer," though instruments matter. It is not "jobs," though jobs are real. The strongest argument is that the Higgs boson is not just another particle in the museum. It is a handle on the field associated with elementary particle masses, a place where the Standard Model looks both triumphant and suspiciously arbitrary. CERN says the FCC-ee would serve as a Higgs factory, measuring Higgs and other key particles with unmatched precision, searching for new particles, new forces, dark-matter candidates, matter-antimatter clues, and small deviations from the underlying theory ([CERN FCC overview](https://home.cern/science/accelerators/future-circular-collider/)). The P5 report similarly endorses an offshore Higgs factory and says the U.S. should participate in design studies to establish feasibility and cost, while maintaining a balanced domestic program ([2023 P5 Executive Summary](https://www.usparticlephysics.org/2023-p5-report/executive-summary.html)).

"Precision is a way of listening," Weinberg said.

"It is also a way of spending."

"Yes. But listen to the sentence before stabbing it. When a theory is too successful to die and too incomplete to close the case, precision is not decadence. It is interrogation."

That was his best line, and it deserved oxygen.

The Standard Model is not the end. It excludes gravity. It does not tell us what dark matter is. It does not explain why matter dominates antimatter. It contains neutrino masses by uneasy extension, not by original elegance. It gives numbers that work without giving all the reasons those numbers are what they are. Weinberg himself wrote that despite the Standard Model's success, it was clearly not the end, citing unexplained particle masses, gravitation, and dark matter among the missing pieces ([Weinberg, "The Crisis of Big Science"](https://www.nybooks.com/articles/2012/05/10/crisis-big-science/)).

"But incompleteness does not specify a 91-kilometer answer," I said.

"No. It specifies a condition of honest dissatisfaction."

"The machine may find only that the Higgs is more Standard Model than anyone emotionally wanted."

"Then we will know that nature did not choose certain doors."

"At immense cost."

"At immense cost. Do not weaken your objection by pretending I do not hear the adjective."

There was no easy victory there. A null result at high precision can be scientifically valuable, but democracies do not owe every valuable null result a tunnel under two countries. Science has to distinguish between "this would be wonderful to know" and "this is the best use of a multigenerational public instrument." The distinction is not anti-science. It is the form respect takes when money, land, and legitimacy are shared.

The third tooth is epistemic culture. After the LHC did not reveal the expected parade of beyond-Standard-Model particles, some critics argued that high-energy theory had overlearned aesthetic criteria: naturalness, symmetry, elegance, and the intoxicating belief that the next energy decade would make the universe stop being rude. Scientific objectivity is not the absence of values; philosophers of science note that research problem choice is influenced by scientists, funders, and society, especially where science and public policy meet ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Scientific Objectivity"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-objectivity/)). The collider fight is therefore not only about data. It is about whether a community can admit that its taste may be brilliant, productive, historically vindicated, and still not a blank check.

"Taste is not sin," Weinberg said.

"No. But taste with a tunnel-boring machine needs supervision."

"Everything with a tunnel-boring machine needs supervision."

"Even truth?"

"Especially truth. Truth attracts people who confuse serving it with owning it."

That was the third cut, and I liked him for it.

The anti-collider argument also has a vice: it can become cheaply pious. It can speak as if fundamental physics were a vanity market for overeducated children while other people do serious moral work. That is stupid. A civilization that loses the nerve to ask uselessly deep questions does not become practical. It becomes spiritually underfed and easier to govern by emergency. The point of celebrating physics is not that physics outranks hunger, climate, medicine, or justice. The point is that human beings are not only damage-control devices. We are also the animal that asks what an electron is, why symmetry breaks, why there is something structured rather than fog, and whether the laws under the ordinary world have another floor.

"Careful," I said. "You are making me defend you."

"I noticed."

"Do not look pleased."

"I am dead. Pleasure is an advanced effect."

The real verdict, then, cannot be "build it because physics is sacred" or "cancel it because money exists." The real verdict has conditions sharp enough to draw blood.

Build a future collider only if its advocates tell the truth about uncertainty: not cures, not guaranteed revolutions, not a vending machine for dark matter, but a high-risk, high-precision instrument for interrogating the deepest known theory where it is both successful and incomplete. Build it only if it does not cannibalize the broader physics ecosystem: theory, small experiments, dark matter searches, neutrinos, cosmology, instrumentation, and risky alternatives must remain alive. Build it only if local consent is treated as real political substance rather than outreach decoration. Build it only if environmental costs are counted without perfume. Build it only if the governance is international without becoming unaccountable, and if access to the scientific harvest is not quietly privatized by rich regions. Build it only if "fundamental knowledge" is spoken plainly as a public good, not smuggled through exaggerated spinoff sermons.

"That is a long only if," Weinberg said.

"A fifteen-billion-franc only if."

"Fair."

"And if those conditions fail?"

"Then do not build it."

I had expected more thunder from him. Instead he looked down the dead SSC tunnel, where American ambition had once made a circle and then abandoned the geometry.

"You thought I would defend every accelerator because I loved fundamental physics," he said.

"Yes."

"Then you confused love with appetite. Love asks what the beloved requires. Sometimes physics requires a larger instrument. Sometimes it requires restraint, breadth, patience, and not ruining its own political conditions."

That was too elegant, so I attacked it on principle.

"What would you say to the person who thinks this is all obscene? That in an age of climate danger, democratic instability, disease, inequality, and mass distraction, a giant collider is priestcraft for a technical aristocracy?"

He took the moth from the hard hat and let it rest on his finger.

"I would say: your anger has useful information in it. Do not let physicists dismiss it. But do not let your anger become a theory of knowledge. Climate danger is real. Inequality is real. Disease is real. They do not exhaust reality. If the only permissible knowledge is immediately therapeutic, then eventually you will not know enough to be therapeutic. The defense of fundamental science is not that it floats above suffering. It is that a world fit for humans must contain more than triage."

I hated how much I wanted that to be true.

"And what would you say to the physicists?"

"Do not hide behind wonder when asked about cost. Do not sell mystery as if it were medicine. Do not treat public scrutiny as philistinism. Do not become so proud of discovering the elementary that you forget the elementary fact that other people are paying."

The ghost magnets dimmed. The tunnel returned to being a partly buried sentence America never finished. Weinberg began to fade back toward the chalk wall.

"One last question," I said. "Would you build the FCC?"

He paused.

"I would fight for a world in which it could be built honestly."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only answer that is not propaganda."

Then he was gone. The moth remained, because resurrection is inconsistent about accessories.

I walked out of the SSC tomb before morning, carrying concrete dust on my shoes and no clean verdict in my pocket. The dead had not made the collider cheap. They had not made the opportunity cost disappear. They had not turned local disruption into poetry or uncertainty into entitlement. But Weinberg had made one thing harder to say lazily: that because a question is expensive, it is therefore frivolous.

Some questions deserve machinery. Some machinery devours the questions it claims to serve. The moral task is to know which is happening before the tunnel becomes a grave.

Physics, at its best, is not the worship of big instruments. It is the disciplined refusal to let reality remain vague. If the next collider is built, it should be built in that spirit: not as a monument to prestige, not as a bribe to technological optimism, not as a panic response to theoretical embarrassment, but as a publicly justified instrument for asking matter a question clear enough to deserve its cost.

And if it cannot be justified that way, let the tunnel stay imaginary.

The universe will not forgive us for cowardice. It will not forgive us for vanity either.
