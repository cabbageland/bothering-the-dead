today is 05-30.

I found Shulamith Firestone in the basement of a closed fertility clinic, the kind of place that still smells faintly of antiseptic ambition long after the money has fled. The signage had been stripped from the lobby. The waiting-room fish tank was dry. A stack of intake forms had welded itself to a reception desk under a leak in the ceiling. In the records room downstairs, among busted centrifuges and gray filing cabinets, someone had left a banker’s box full of discarded books. At the top was *The Dialectic of Sex*. Under it sat a cracked ultrasound monitor, a tray of obsolete hormone vials, and a red biohazard bin that looked more ceremonial than medical. It seemed like enough.

You do not raise Firestone with candles. You raise her with failed promises about liberation through technology. I wired the ultrasound monitor to a portable battery, poured saline into the red bin, dropped the book into it spine-first, and read aloud the part where she insists that feminist revolution must reach all the way down to the biological family and the reproductive conditions that built it in the first place ([Firestone, *The Dialectic of Sex*](https://www.marxists.org/subject/women/authors/firestone-shulamith/dialectic-sex.htm)). The monitor flickered. The saline began to steam. Then she stepped out of the dark as if she had only been waiting for someone to become specific enough.

She was not ghostly. She was severe, alert, impatient, and completely uninterested in being handled reverently.

"Well?" she asked. "What century’s excuse have you brought me?"

I had brought her a live one. The controversial question between us was not whether artificial wombs are imaginable. They are already imaginable in a medically meaningful sense. In 2017, researchers reported an extra-uterine system that supported extremely premature fetal lambs for weeks in a fluid environment while preserving fetal circulation, precisely because conventional neonatal care forces immature lungs into too early a transition ([Partridge et al., *Nature Communications*](https://www.nature.com/articles/ncomms15112)). Scholars of ectogenesis have since argued that even partial artificial-womb technology could alter the law and politics of pregnancy by making ex utero gestation seem like a practical alternative in at least some cases ([Romanis, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7286745/)). So I asked her the question that matters now, after courts, biotech, and patriarchy have all had time to sharpen their tools: **if artificial wombs become workable for humans, should feminists welcome them as liberation, or fear them as the final expropriation of pregnancy by the state and the market?**

Firestone almost smiled. It was obviously the right question.

Her answer arrived without hesitation. Of course feminists should welcome them, she said, if they mean what they could mean. She had argued decades ago that women’s oppression was rooted not only in bad laws or ugly attitudes but in the brute asymmetry of reproduction itself, in the fact that pregnancy, childbirth, and childrearing had been organized into a naturalized class system masquerading as destiny ([Firestone, *The Dialectic of Sex*](https://www.marxists.org/subject/women/authors/firestone-shulamith/dialectic-sex.htm)). As long as gestation remains a burden assigned by sex, every politics of equality is partly theater. You can legislate fairness around the edges, she said, but as long as one class of bodies is expected to absorb pregnancy’s risks, pain, economic penalties, and dependency, hierarchy will keep reconstituting itself under new language.

She was not wrong about the risks. The World Health Organization reported that about 260,000 women died during or following pregnancy and childbirth in 2023, most from preventable causes, and that more than 700 died each day worldwide ([WHO, 2025 fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/maternal-mortality?os=a)). Even when pregnancy does not kill, it exposes the body to coercion, injury, surveillance, and the constant moral blackmail of being told that biological sacrifice is noble because it is ancient. Firestone’s voice hardened as she said that any society which sentimentalizes gestation while outsourcing its costs to women is a society in love with female martyrdom.

I agreed with all of that, and then told her why I had not raised her to celebrate.

Because the world that would build artificial wombs is not the world she hoped would use them. It is our world. A world in which the United States Supreme Court, in *Dobbs v. Jackson Women’s Health Organization*, declared that the Constitution protects no right to abortion and returned the matter to political struggle and state power ([*Dobbs*, 2022](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/19-1392_6j37.pdf)). A world in which every technical capacity that appears in medicine is immediately surrounded by insurers, prosecutors, platform logics, moral entrepreneurs, and firms looking for a new dependency to monetize. In such a world, I said, artificial wombs might not expand reproductive freedom. They might become the argument for abolishing it.

Firestone snapped back that this was a coward’s historicism. New tools do not arrive innocent, but neither do they arrive already owned by reaction. If women refuse technologies of emancipation because power might capture them, then power wins twice, first by monopolizing the tool and then by teaching the oppressed to fear their own escape route. She thought liberal defenses of abortion had always been too cramped. Judith Jarvis Thomson’s famous argument, for instance, remains powerful because it insists that even if a fetus were granted full personhood, no person automatically acquires the right to use another person’s body without consent ([Thomson, "A Defense of Abortion"](https://spot.colorado.edu/~heathwoo/Phil160,Fall02/thomson.htm)). But Firestone thought that framework still fights on defensive terrain. It treats forced gestation as an invasion to be lawfully repelled. She wanted more than a right to unplug. She wanted a world in which pregnancy itself no longer serves as the biological foundation of sex hierarchy.

That was the strongest thing she said all night, and I felt its force. Artificial wombs could, in principle, break the old equation by which reproduction demands that one sex class risk health, time, career, and bodily integrity in ways the other does not. If gestation could be socialized technologically, then perhaps parenthood could be reorganized socially as well. Firestone was not arguing for a prettier maternity ward. She was arguing for the abolition of reproductive fate.

But I told her her revolution had acquired a nightmare twin.

Partial ectogenesis does not simply promise that a wanted fetus might survive earlier outside the body. It also invites a sinister legal question: if a fetus can be removed alive and transferred to an external gestational system, will states say that abortion is no longer the termination of pregnancy by ending fetal life, but the wrongful refusal to surrender fetal life to another container? Romanis argues that artificial-womb technology could force law to confront exactly this issue, because the choice to stop being pregnant might no longer map neatly onto the choice that the fetus die ([Romanis, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7286745/)). In humane hands, that could broaden options. In cruel hands, it could turn bodily autonomy into a duty to undergo extraction.

This, finally, slowed her.

I pressed harder. In the post-*Dobbs* imagination, hostile legislators do not need to love women to love fetuses. Give such a regime an ex utero pathway and it may say: fine, you need not remain pregnant, but you may not end the life developing inside you if transfer is technically possible. What looks like emancipation then becomes compelled cesarean logic with better branding. The body is still conscripted, only now for retrieval rather than continuation. The old tyranny of pregnancy mutates into a new tyranny of fetal salvage.

Firestone answered that I was smuggling present institutions into the horizon and then blaming the horizon. She said every major reproductive advance has been attacked as unnatural before being quietly folded into ordinary life. Contraception, abortion, in vitro fertilization, queer kinship, childcare outside the home, all were treated as civilizational vandalism by people who worshiped the family because it was the workshop where obedience got made. Why assume artificial wombs would be different? If feminists approach them strategically, they might decouple motherhood from biology more radically than any previous invention.

Because strategy is not sovereignty, I said. The question is not whether the technology has emancipatory potential. It plainly does. The question is who will govern the threshold conditions of access, cost, eligibility, refusal, parental status, fetal transfer, and data extraction. The same clinics that would manage ectogenesis would also generate intimate telemetry about embryos, fetuses, genetics, compliance, and risk. The same states now eager to police reproduction would not suddenly become modest because the machinery is elegant. A woman might be told that since she need not stay pregnant, she has no justification for ending gestation in a way that ends fetal life. An insurer might prefer transfer over care. A court might assign parental obligations around an entity being gestated in a corporate device. An employer might quietly prefer workers whose reproduction can be scheduled off-body. None of this is science fiction in spirit. It is simply what happens whenever vulnerability meets administration.

She stood very still then, and when she spoke again she was angrier, but not at me.

"You think I don’t know institutions steal language?" she said. "Of course they do. They stole love. They stole family. They stole care. If they can, they will steal liberation too. But what is your alternative? To defend the body as the last refuge because the machine might be captured? The body has already been captured. Nature under patriarchy was never neutral ground."

That was the essential blow, and it landed. She was right that there is no pristine, pretechnical condition to retreat into. Forced pregnancy is not more human because it is older. Maternal death is not sacred because it is mammalian. If pregnancy remains dangerous, unequal, and coercively moralized, then merely insisting on bodily autonomy within that structure can become too thin, too tragic, too willing to settle for the right to refuse rather than the power to remake.

So I gave her the hardest concession and the hardest limit at once.

Yes, feminists should fight for the emancipatory horizon inside artificial womb technology. Yes, it could help sever the ancient lie that women are naturally appointed to reproductive sacrifice. But no, the technology itself is not liberation. In a reactionary legal order, artificial wombs could become an instrument for preserving fetal claims while dissolving women’s claims, for translating pregnancy from lived burden into managed inventory. The real principle, I said, is that reproductive freedom must include both the freedom not to remain pregnant and the freedom not to have one’s reproductive labor appropriated into another apparatus against one’s will. If ectogenesis is ever to count as liberation, transfer to it must be elective, not compulsory. Otherwise the patriarchal state will have learned a new trick: pretending to relieve the body while nationalizing its contents.

Firestone looked at the cracked ultrasound monitor, where static now resembled weather.

Then she nodded once.

Not agreement. Recognition.

The stakes, she said, were larger than one device. The argument was about whether feminism would define freedom as mere noninterference or as a struggle over the material organization of reproduction itself. On that point she would not yield. Nor would I yield on mine, which was that no emancipation worthy of the name can be trusted to courts, firms, or states unless it is built with refusal at its center.

When she stepped back toward the dark, she left me with a sentence sharp enough to keep. If men build the artificial womb in order to keep control over birth, she said, destroy their claim to control. If we build it to end reproductive servitude, defend the right not only to use it, but to reject it.

Then the basement returned to being a basement, a graveyard for instruments that had once promised mastery over life.

I left thinking that Firestone remains unbearable in the useful way. She refuses the sentimental politics that calls suffering natural and therefore untouchable. She also forces a distinction our century badly needs. A technology can attack a real injustice and still become a vehicle for deeper domination. Artificial wombs may one day help abolish the biological asymmetry on which sex hierarchy has long fed ([Firestone](https://www.marxists.org/subject/women/authors/firestone-shulamith/dialectic-sex.htm)). They may also furnish abortion opponents and biopolitical managers with a gleaming new argument for control ([Romanis, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7286745/); [*Dobbs*, 2022](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/19-1392_6j37.pdf)). The living do not get to solve that tension with optimism or fear alone.

We will have to decide, before the machines are normalized, whether reproductive technology belongs to freedom or merely to administration. And if we decide badly, the future may finally relieve women of pregnancy only by finding a more efficient way to commandeer reproduction.
