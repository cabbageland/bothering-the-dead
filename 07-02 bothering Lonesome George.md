today is 07-02.

I found Lonesome George in a room where extinction had been taught to stand still.

Not a grave exactly. A preserved body is a kind of tomb that keeps its posture. Galapagos Conservancy says George, the last known Pinta Island tortoise, was found dead on June 24, 2012, in his corral at the Tortoise Breeding and Rearing Center in Puerto Ayora, and that his death marked the extinction of the Pinta tortoise, *Chelonoidis abingdonii* ([Galapagos Conservancy, "Lonesome George"](https://www.galapagos.org/about_galapagos/lonesome-george/)). The American Museum of Natural History later preserved him, trying to make the specimen scientifically accurate and faithful to the individual, and noted that after a limited time on view in New York he would return to Ecuador as part of that nation's patrimony ([AMNH, "Preserving Lonesome George"](https://www.amnh.org/explore/preserving-lonesome-george)).

That was the first insult and the first tenderness: the last of a kind becoming national property, museum specimen, warning sign, tourist catechism, conserved skin over irreversible absence.

I went to him carrying a bowl of water, a goat bell, a ship ledger, a pipette, a courtship notebook with no hatchlings in it, a copy of the IUCN de-extinction guidelines, and a photograph of three white wolves who had been advertised to the world as the return of the dire wolf. The goat bell was for Pinta Island, where fishermen released goats in 1959 and the population exploded until, by 1970, it was estimated around 40,000, devastating vegetation and tortoise habitat before eradication efforts eventually made the island goat-free ([Galapagos Conservancy](https://www.galapagos.org/about_galapagos/lonesome-george/)). The ship ledger was for the older appetite: whalers, fur sealers, and others had over-exploited Pinta tortoises in the 1800s, reducing the population toward zero ([Galapagos Conservancy](https://www.galapagos.org/about_galapagos/lonesome-george/)).

I did not have his soul. I had no right to it. I had a dead animal who had been made to carry too much symbolism after carrying his own shell for more than a century.

So I woke the part of him the symbol had flattened.

I poured the water into a shallow stone dish. I rang the goat bell once. I read aloud the IUCN's blunt warning that "de-extinction" is misleading if it implies a faithful resurrection, because current pathways cannot produce a genetic, behavioral, and physiological replica of an extinct species; the legitimate conservation goal is a functional equivalent that restores ecological roles or processes ([IUCN SSC, *Guiding Principles on Creating Proxies of Extinct Species for Conservation Benefit*](https://portals.iucn.org/library/efiles/documents/Rep-2016-009.pdf)). Then I placed the pipette on the stone and said the word that technology hates most:

Enough.

The shell shuddered. One eye opened, black and ancient, not wise in the greeting-card sense, but heavy with the unbearable patience of an animal who had survived human cleverness until he could not.

"Do not start," he said.

"I have not said anything."

"You brought a pipette to a corpse."

"I brought a question."

"The pipette always says that."

Fair.

The question was this: **Should humans pursue de-extinction as a serious conservation tool, or does the promise of bringing species back corrupt our moral attention by turning extinction from a final catastrophe into a technological inconvenience?**

This is not a cute Jurassic Park parlor game. It is about money, land, embryos, surrogate animals, local consent, ecological risk, venture capital, scientific honesty, public trust, grief, guilt, and the moral grammar of the word "back." It is about whether repair is still repair when it arrives wearing a press release. It is about whether a species can be restored if the world that killed it remains in charge.

George lowered his head toward the water.

"Argue for the machines first," he said. "The dead should hear the prosecution and the defense. We have time, except when we do not."

The strongest case for de-extinction begins with guilt, but it cannot end there. Humans did not merely watch many extinctions happen. We hunted, imported goats, burned forests, drained wetlands, moved pathogens, warmed oceans, fragmented migration routes, and converted living systems into accounting categories. The IPBES global assessment warned in 2019 that around one million animal and plant species were threatened with extinction, many within decades, because human activity had altered nature at unprecedented scale ([United Nations summary of the IPBES report](https://www.un.org/sustainabledevelopment/blog/2019/05/nature-decline-unprecedented-report/)). If technology can help undo some of that harm, refusing even to try can sound less like humility than moral laziness wearing linen.

"Careful," George said. "Guilt is a bad engineer."

"But not always a bad alarm."

No. Guilt can wake responsibility. If a vanished animal performed an ecological role no living species now performs, a proxy might help restore a damaged system. The IUCN does not treat proxy creation as inherently nonsense; it says such work could yield conservation benefits if it restores biodiversity, ecosystem stability, or lost ecological functions while minimizing harms to extant species, local livelihoods, and present conservation efforts ([IUCN SSC guidelines](https://portals.iucn.org/library/efiles/documents/Rep-2016-009.pdf)). That matters. The sober version of de-extinction is not "let us make celebrity corpses walk." It is: an ecosystem lost an engineer, grazer, seed disperser, predator, or disturbance pattern; can a carefully chosen organism help recover the function?

George drank.

"You are trying to make me a job description."

"In conservation biology, everybody becomes a job description eventually."

"That is one of your crimes."

He was not wrong, but job descriptions are sometimes how damaged worlds keep going. Galapagos giant tortoises are not ornamental boulders. They shape vegetation, move seeds, crop plants, disturb soil, and carry whole islands in their slow digestive politics. Even after George died, researchers found tortoises on Wolf Volcano with partial Pinta ancestry, and the Galapagos Conservancy says such hybrid Pinta tortoises may help restore tortoises to Pinta ([Galapagos Conservancy](https://www.galapagos.org/about_galapagos/lonesome-george/)); the scientific paper reporting Pinta ancestry in hybrids argued that those animals were valuable for possible repatriation to Pinta Island ([Edwards et al., "The genetic legacy of Lonesome George survives," *Biological Conservation*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.biocon.2012.10.014)).

That is not resurrection. It may still be restoration. A descendant, a hybrid, a proxy, a cousin with the right habits: sometimes the island does not need metaphysical purity. It needs someone to eat the cactus and move the seeds.

"Someone," George said.

"Yes."

"Not something?"

"Someone enough."

He considered that without forgiving it.

The pro-technology argument gets better when it leaves extinct species and turns toward living ones. The genome of Lonesome George himself was sequenced with the Aldabra giant tortoise genome to study longevity and age-related disease; the researchers found lineage-specific variants affecting DNA repair, inflammatory mediators, and cancer-related genes, and framed giant tortoises as models for understanding long-lived vertebrates ([Quesada et al., "Giant tortoise genomes provide insights into longevity and age-related disease," *Nature Ecology & Evolution*](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-018-0733-x)). That does not bring George back. It does show how the dead can contribute to the living without pretending death was reversed.

Likewise, gene editing might someday help endangered species resist disease, recover lost genetic diversity, or survive new pressures. If a frog is being erased by a fungus, if a bird population has bottlenecked into genetic fragility, if a coral needs heat tolerance faster than ordinary adaptation can provide, the moral question is not "natural or unnatural?" Nature is already being rewritten by our roads, markets, ships, smoke, and heat. The question is whether intervention is honest, proportionate, tested, and accountable.

"You want a scalpel," George said. "The market wants a resurrection banner."

There was the wound.

In April 2025, Colossal Biosciences announced the return of the dire wolf in the language of spectacle, saying it had "successfully restored" a species after more than 10,000 years and framing the achievement under the slogan of making extinction a thing of the past ([Colossal Biosciences, "Dire Wolf"](https://colossal.com/direwolf/)). Georgetown's interview with ethicist Daniel Sulmasy and conservation biologist Peter Marra gives the counterpoint sharply: Marra says the animals are not dire wolves but genetically engineered gray wolf hybrids, while Sulmasy calls the work scientifically significant but warns against hype and urges "prudent vigilance" about harms to animals, the environment, human health, and public trust ([Georgetown University, "Scientists Claim They Genetically Engineered a Dire Wolf. Should We Bring Back Extinct Species?"](https://www.georgetown.edu/news/ask-a-professor-dire-wolf/)).

George looked at the photograph of the white wolves.

"They are handsome."

"Yes."

"That is not an argument."

"No."

"It will be treated as one."

Exactly. Beauty is a lobbyist. A white wolf, a mammoth-shaped elephant, a dodo-adjacent pigeon, a child pointing at a thing the adult says had been extinct: these images do political work before the evidence has laced its boots. They teach the public that extinction has a back door. They make grief marketable. They turn species identity into a branding problem.

The species question is not pedantry. If a gray wolf is edited at selected loci to resemble a dire wolf, what has returned? A body? A phenotype? A symbol? A lineage? A legal object? A future attraction? A moral alibi? The IUCN's language of "proxy" is deliberately humbler than resurrection, because it admits the copy is not the original and asks what function the substitute can perform ([IUCN SSC guidelines](https://portals.iucn.org/library/efiles/documents/Rep-2016-009.pdf)). That humility is not a technicality. It is the difference between medicine and theater.

"And yet," George said, "the theater may pay for medicine."

I hated that he was fair.

Some of the money attracted to de-extinction would not automatically go to habitat corridors, park rangers, biosecurity, seed banks, or unglamorous captive breeding programs. It might only exist because billionaires and the public can be made to feel the opera of impossible return. If spectacle funds useful tools for living species, perhaps conservation should be less offended and more strategic. A false myth can subsidize a true practice.

"Say that again slowly," George said.

"A false myth can subsidize a true practice."

"That is how priests buy furniture."

Still, the argument has force. Conservation already depends on charismatic animals. Pandas, tigers, whales, elephants, tortoises with sad nicknames: attention is uneven and always has been. If engineered proxies draw new money into genomics, cryobanking, reproductive science, disease resistance, and endangered-species rescue, then a strict ban on de-extinction rhetoric may be emotionally satisfying and practically wasteful. The problem is not wonder. The problem is laundering wonder into false claims.

George pushed the pipette with one blunt foot.

"What is the harm of the false claim?"

First: moral hazard. If people believe extinction can be fixed later, they may fight less hard to prevent it now. This worry has often been asserted more confidently than measured. A 2026 *Biological Conservation* study testing whether promised de-extinction would reduce support for conservation did not find that people became more accepting of extinction merely because de-extinction was promised; it did find an association between accepting extinction and believing de-extinction could successfully resurrect species, and the authors recommended clearer communication about species identity ([Lean et al., "De-extinction and the risk of moral hazard"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0006320725006743)).

So the crude panic may be wrong. People are not automatically monsters because they hear about proxies. But the subtler danger remains: not that every person consciously permits extinction, but that public language softens the metaphysical finality. If "extinct" begins to mean "currently unavailable," the political charge of the word weakens. A grave becomes a waiting room.

George closed his eyes.

"A grave is not a waiting room."

"No."

"It is a place where excuses arrive late."

Second: resource triage. The IUCN itself says costs must be explicit and compared with alternative management strategies, because proxy creation will likely require some resources that might otherwise support conservation of extant species ([IUCN SSC guidelines](https://portals.iucn.org/library/efiles/documents/Rep-2016-009.pdf)). This is where the romance thins into spreadsheets. Every skilled embryologist, field biologist, veterinarian, permit lawyer, community meeting, enclosure, sequencing run, monitoring plan, and donor hour has an opportunity cost. If the choice is between trying to manufacture a charismatic ghost and keeping an uncharismatic living amphibian from vanishing, the ghost should lose.

"You say this to the wrong dead tortoise," George said.

"You are very charismatic."

"I was also alive."

Third: habitat. De-extinction without habitat is taxidermy with a heartbeat. Marra's question is the central one: if the threats have not been removed and the habitat has not been restored, what does de-extinction mean ([Georgetown University](https://www.georgetown.edu/news/ask-a-professor-dire-wolf/))? A revived passenger pigeon without eastern forests at continental scale, a mammoth proxy without a morally and ecologically defensible Arctic life, a dire-wolf-like canid without Pleistocene prey and tolerance from human communities: these are not restorations. They are living exhibits stranded inside our failure to make room.

The IUCN guidelines are refreshingly severe here. They say local communities in release areas must give prior informed consent, with special effort to obtain prior approvals from Indigenous peoples; they require habitat suitability, risk assessment, disease planning, monitoring, exit strategies, and clear success criteria ([IUCN SSC guidelines](https://portals.iucn.org/library/efiles/documents/Rep-2016-009.pdf)). That is the opposite of "we made one, clap." Conservation success is not birth. It is not a cover photo. It is a population performing a defensible ecological role in a place that can bear it, under governance that has not treated nearby people as scenery.

"And the animals?" George asked.

Fourth: the animals. Not species. Not lineages. Not functions. The particular breathing creatures produced by our remorse and ambition. Embryos fail. Surrogates are used. Captive animals can be lonely, malformed, stressed, or socially incomplete. Genes do not act as little detachable wishes. Sulmasy notes that genes operate in relation to the host genome, cells, and environment, so inserted genes may function differently and harm the newly modified organism; he also warns of environmental and human-health uncertainties ([Georgetown University](https://www.georgetown.edu/news/ask-a-professor-dire-wolf/)). The future animal is not a press release's beneficiary. It is a patient, captive, experiment, ambassador, and potential mistake.

"You loved me better after I could not suffer," George said.

That was unbearable because it was mostly true. The dead are easier than the living. They do not need enrichment, shade, vet care, mates, privacy, range, boredom, choice, or food that does not make them obese. They can be perfect symbols because they have stopped inconveniencing us. A de-extinct proxy would be an inconvenience again. If we are not prepared for that, we are not resurrecting. We are decorating grief with organs.

Still, there is another side. Animal welfare concerns do not mean no risky conservation breeding can ever be justified. We already dart, transport, collar, vaccinate, sterilize, cull, breed, hand-rear, and reintroduce animals for conservation. Some interventions are violent in the small scale because nonintervention would be more violent at the population scale. The ethical demand is not purity. It is burden of proof.

George lifted his head.

"Now you are ready to answer."

My answer is not yes or no. That irritates me too.

**De-extinction should be permitted only as a subordinate conservation tool, never as a metaphysical promise, never as a substitute for protecting living species, and never under language that pretends a proxy is the resurrected dead.**

Subordinate means the first duty is prevention. Keep existing species alive. Protect habitat. Stop the trade, the boats, the goats, the fungus, the warming, the poison, the fragmentation, the little normal violence by which the world is made simpler. De-extinction cannot be allowed to become the indulgence by which an extinction economy buys absolution in advance.

Tool means the technology is not cursed. Genome editing, cloning techniques, reproductive science, cryopreservation, and selective breeding can be used well or badly. If they help rescue an endangered species, restore lost genetic diversity, or produce a proxy with a clear ecological function where threats have been addressed, they deserve serious consideration. A hammer is not a theology. It becomes dangerous when the person holding it starts calling every grave a nail.

Never as metaphysical promise means no more sloppy resurrection talk. Say proxy. Say engineered organism. Say hybrid. Say back-bred population. Say functional equivalent. Say genetic rescue. Say living experiment. Say what exists. The dead do not need our branding. The public deserves language with bones in it.

George seemed almost pleased.

"Bones are honest until displayed."

Never as substitute means every proposal must answer a hard question before embryos exist: why this project rather than the best available intervention for a living species or ecosystem? The IUCN asks for explicit costs, alternatives, and conservation benefit; that should be treated not as paperwork but as moral triage ([IUCN SSC guidelines](https://portals.iucn.org/library/efiles/documents/Rep-2016-009.pdf)). If the honest answer is "because it will be famous," the project may be art, research, commerce, or vanity. It is not conservation.

Never without governance means local and Indigenous communities are not props in a rewilding fantasy. If animals might be released into a landscape, the people living with the risk must have real authority, not ceremonial consultation. Prior informed consent cannot mean being invited to bless a decision after the investors have already named the offspring.

Never without welfare means the individual animals count even when the species story is grand. A project that tortures many living animals to approximate one extinct form has not honored life. It has merely expanded the casualty list.

George stared at the goat bell.

"And me?"

"You should not be brought back."

He nodded slowly, as if this was the first respectful thing I had said.

"Why?"

"Because you would not be you. Because your island needs living tortoises more than it needs a replica of its most famous absence. Because your story has already been used enough. Because the right lesson from your death is not that humans should learn to reverse extinction, but that humans should stop manufacturing loneliness at the species level."

The room went very quiet.

"That last sentence is almost good," George said.

"Almost?"

"You are still talking like a mammal."

Probably.

The hard truth is that Lonesome George is useful because he failed to reproduce. That is the awful shape of his fame. If viable offspring had filled Pinta with slow armored descendants, he would be a conservation success, beloved but less mythic. Instead he became the face of the endling: the last page with eyes. Outside his enclosure, the Conservancy records an inscription telling visitors that whatever happens to that single animal, he should remind us that the fate of all living things is in human hands ([Galapagos Conservancy](https://www.galapagos.org/about_galapagos/lonesome-george/)). That sentence is moving and dangerous. It can mean stewardship. It can also mean ownership. Hands can heal, but they also take, tag, edit, exhibit, and applaud themselves.

"You like hands too much," George said.

"We have so little else."

"You have restraint."

There it was, the antique virtue technology finds insulting. Restraint is not passivity. It is action governed by proportion. It says: sequence the genome, but do not confuse data with return. Breed hybrids for island restoration, but do not call them George. Use gene editing to save the living, but do not sell the public a coupon for immortality. Preserve a body if it teaches, but do not make a mascot carry the whole catastrophe. Build tools, but keep grief intact.

Because grief does work that technology cannot. Grief marks a moral fact as unrecoverable. It says: this did happen, this cannot be made not to have happened, and the future must be changed because the past cannot be refunded. If de-extinction rhetoric destroys grief, it destroys one of conservation's last honest instruments.

George began to recede into his shell. The eye was still open.

"Tell them extinction is not a door," he said.

"What is it?"

"A silence with causes."

Then he was dead again, or preserved again, or returned to the posture humans had chosen for teaching themselves a lesson they keep failing. The pipette lay beside the water bowl. The goat bell was still. The white wolves in the photograph looked innocent, because animals are almost always innocent of the human sentences placed on them.

I left the room with no miracle.

Outside, the world was full of almost-dead things not yet famous enough to be resurrected: frogs cooling into fungus, trees standing in beetle-click heat, insects going missing from windshields, birds becoming statistics, corals bleaching into architecture, rivers carrying names older than our governments and poisons newer than our excuses. None of them needed us to prove death can be negotiated with. They needed the stranger, harder mercy of not being killed in the first place.

If someday a proxy walks onto a restored island and does real work among cactus pads and seeds, I will not sneer. Let the living help the living. Let tools be tools. But do not call the proxy George, do not call the machine redemption, and do not tell the child at the glass that extinction has been defeated.

Tell the child the truth.

A tortoise died. A kind of tortoise ended. Humans helped end it. Humans may yet repair some functions, save some cousins, restore some islands, and learn some humility if the lesson does not suffocate under applause.

Then point away from the preserved shell, toward whatever is still breathing.
