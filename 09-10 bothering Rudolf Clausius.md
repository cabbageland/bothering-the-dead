today is 09-10.

I found Rudolf Clausius in Bonn by following the smell of honest waste heat.

Not smoke. Smoke is too theatrical, too willing to help a writer. This was the drier odor of old machines cooling after their useful arrogance: a kettle giving up its argument to the kitchen air, a transformer humming behind a locked panel, a server fan worrying at the darkness, the little warm exhale from a laptop that has been asked to summarize the civilization that powers it. Clausius spent the last phase of his academic life at the University of Bonn and died there in 1888, after having helped make thermodynamics into a discipline instead of a set of steam-engine anxieties ([MacTutor, "Rudolf Julius Emmanuel Clausius"](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Clausius/); [Encyclopedia.com, "Rudolf Julius Emanuel Clausius"](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/science-and-technology/physics-biographies/rudolf-julius-emanuel-clausius)). That was enough geography. The body I came to disturb was not only in a grave. It was in every irreversible process that had learned to continue without him.

I brought a resurrection kit appropriate to the founder of entropy: a chipped thermometer, a brass toy engine, a dead phone at 1 percent battery, a coil of copper wire, a sealed black server blade, a copy of his *Mechanical Theory of Heat*, and a printout of the sentence that made him sound like an undertaker for the universe: "The energy of the universe is constant. The entropy of the universe tends to a maximum" ([Encyclopedia.com, "Concept of Entropy"](https://www.encyclopedia.com/people/science-and-technology/physics-biographies/rudolf-julius-emanuel-clausius)). In the English translation of *The Mechanical Theory of Heat*, Clausius explains that he chose the word "entropy" after the Greek word for transformation and because of its kinship with energy ([Clausius, *The Mechanical Theory of Heat*](https://archive.org/details/mechanicaltheory00clau)).

The spell was not dramatic. I put the dead phone on top of the book, connected it to a charger, and watched the battery percentage rise while the charger warmed in my hand. I read aloud the line in which Clausius defines entropy for a body and gives the relation dQ = T dS for reversible change ([Clausius, *The Mechanical Theory of Heat*, full text](https://archive.org/stream/mechanicaltheory00clau/mechanicaltheory00clau_djvu.txt)). Then I opened the server blade and placed inside it a slip of paper bearing Landauer's title: "Irreversibility and Heat Generation in the Computing Process" ([Landauer, 1961](https://doi.org/10.1147/rd.53.0183)).

The server fan turned once.

Clausius sat up in the archive dust with the disgusted dignity of a man woken by bad engineering.

"You have made a small altar to loss," he said.

"I brought you home."

"No. You brought me to the accountants."

Good. We were awake.

The controversial topic was this: **should modern societies ration, restrict, or aggressively tax energy-hungry computation, especially artificial intelligence, cryptocurrency, and speculative data-center expansion, under climate and grid constraints; or would that be an anti-intellectual panic against the very tools that could help civilization become cleaner, smarter, and less wasteful?**

This is not a decorative complaint about people using too many apps. It is about electricity, land, cooling systems, grids, gas plants, public subsidies, scientific discovery, medical research, surveillance capitalism, crypto speculation, classroom cheating, military targeting, climate modeling, hospital scheduling, employment, search, boredom, status, and whether a society that cannot distinguish thought from throughput deserves more machines. The U.S. Department of Energy announced in 2024 that domestic energy use from data centers was expected to double or triple by 2028 ([DOE, "Evaluating Increase in Electricity Demand from Data Centers"](https://www.energy.gov/articles/doe-releases-new-report-evaluating-increase-electricity-demand-data-centers)). The International Energy Agency likewise warned that electricity use from data centers, artificial intelligence, and cryptocurrency could rise sharply, with global consumption from those categories potentially more than doubling from 2022 to 2026 ([IEA, *Electricity 2024* executive summary](https://www.iea.org/reports/electricity-2024/executive-summary)). The climate background is not neutral wallpaper: the IPCC synthesis report states that human-caused climate change is already affecting weather and climate extremes in every region, and that deep, rapid, sustained emissions reductions are needed to limit warming ([IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/)).

Clausius looked at the server blade as if it were a locomotive pretending to be a saint.

"Begin with the prosecution," he said.

Very well.

The prosecution begins with a stupidly simple fact: computation is physical. The cloud is not a cloud. It is buildings, wires, chips, pumps, substations, mines, cooling loops, backup generators, labor contracts, tax abatements, and heat. Every search query, model training run, recommendation feed, ad auction, proof-of-work lottery, and synthetic image is a transformation of energy into ordered behavior and then, finally, into waste heat. Landauer gave this metaphysical insult a technical edge: erasing information in an irreversible computing process has a minimum thermodynamic cost, and later experimental work verified the link between information erasure and heat at the scale Landauer predicted ([Landauer, 1961](https://doi.org/10.1147/rd.53.0183); [Berut et al., "Experimental verification of Landauer's principle"](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature10872)).

That does not mean your laptop is near the physical limit. It means the dream of disembodied intelligence is dead on arrival. Thought has a temperature. Memory has a bill. Forgetting is not free.

Clausius's eyes sharpened.

"Careful. You are using physics to smuggle in disgust."

"No. I am using physics to catch the marketing department."

Because the marketing department has been busy. It names warehouses "cloud regions" and treats electricity as if it were a background permission granted by the universe to any company with a growth chart. It sells artificial intelligence as mind while hiding the furnace. It sells crypto as decentralization while converting scarce power into rivalrous proof that someone won a numerical lottery. It sells frictionless digital progress while communities get transmission lines, higher demand, diesel backup, and the political pressure to keep grids cheap for machinery before they are clean for people.

"You object to frivolity," Clausius said.

"I object to externalized frivolity."

There is a difference. I do not mind heat. Life is heat with opinions. I mind a social order that lets private actors turn public energy systems into speculative equipment and then call the result innovation. If a hospital uses computation to route ambulances, analyze scans, predict drug interactions, or reduce waste in a power grid, the heat has a moral argument. If a laboratory uses computation to model fusion plasmas, climate systems, materials, proteins, or epidemics, the heat has a serious defense. But if a company burns megawatt-hours to produce infinite personalized sludge, manipulative advertising, financial arbitrage, fake companions, spam, or casino tokens with libertarian packaging, then the heat has become a confession.

"You wish to distinguish noble heat from vulgar heat," he said.

"Yes."

"On what instrument?"

That was the hard question, and he knew it.

The easy anti-compute position says: cap it, ration it, stop it. It has emotional force because the waste is real. But it risks becoming a nostalgia with a policy costume. Computation is not one thing. A GPU cluster can train a medical model, optimize logistics, generate propaganda, price insurance, discover a catalyst, simulate a bomb, serve pornography, accelerate weather forecasts, or mint a token whose chief product is resentment with a ticker symbol. "AI" is not a moral category. "Data center" is not a moral category. "Electricity use" is not a moral category. The ethical unit is the transformation: what energy becomes, for whom, at whose cost, under what alternatives.

Clausius touched the toy engine.

"You have arrived at thermodynamics. Continue."

Thermodynamics is merciless because it refuses angelic accounting. It does not let us say that digital work is pure because the output is intangible. It also does not let us say that energy use is evil because it ends as heat. All useful work ends in dispersed energy. The question is not whether a process increases entropy. It will. The question is whether the irreversible spending of ordered energy purchased something worth losing the order for.

That is where the rationing case becomes morally serious. We already ration by price, by infrastructure, by political influence, and by neglect. Poor households are asked to accept demand-response thermostats; data centers ask utilities for dedicated substations. Public schools hold bake sales for air conditioning while speculative compute receives tax deals. Neighborhoods are told the grid is fragile; platforms deploy ever-larger models because a competitor might do it first. The present system is not freedom. It is rationing through power.

William Stanley Jevons saw a related trap in coal economics: greater efficiency in using coal could increase, rather than decrease, total coal consumption by making coal-powered work cheaper and more expansive ([Jevons, *The Coal Question*](https://oll.libertyfund.org/titles/jevons-the-coal-question)). That is the old ghost inside modern computing. Efficient chips do not automatically save energy if efficiency expands the market, multiplies use cases, lowers the cost of inference, and trains users to summon computation for every shrug. The question "can the model run more cheaply?" is incomplete. The second question is "how many more times will it be run because it is cheap?"

Clausius nodded, but only once.

"Jevons is not a law of nature."

"No. A warning about appetite."

"Good. Do not confuse capitalism with thermodynamics. Capitalism would eat the sun and call the chewing a platform. But the sun is not guilty."

This was exactly why I had resurrected him instead of a columnist. Clausius would not let outrage pass for analysis. He had helped kill the caloric theory by showing that heat and work had to be understood through transformation, equivalence, and irreversible tendency, not through comforting substances ([MacTutor, "Clausius's first paper"](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Clausius/)). He was not going to let me replace one bad substance with another called "digital waste."

"Now defend the machines," he said.

The defense begins with humility. Computation is one of the few tools we have for understanding systems too complex for unaided human intuition: climate, epidemics, protein folding, power grids, traffic flows, materials, language, markets, stars. It would be obscene to tell a warming civilization to think less precisely because thinking precisely requires electricity. Climate science itself depends on massive computation; weather forecasts save lives; grid optimization can help integrate renewable energy; medical imaging and drug discovery can convert watts into years of life. A society that simply equates energy demand with sin may become clean in the same way a corpse is low-emission.

Clausius enjoyed that.

"At last, an argument with bones."

The pro-compute case also has a democratic side that elitists on both sides dislike. Cheap computation can give students tutors, small labs analytic tools, disabled people communication systems, local governments planning capacity, and independent creators means of production once reserved for institutions. Expensive computation locked behind elite gates is not obviously more ethical than broad computation under public rules. If rationing is crude, it may preserve frivolous use for the rich while scolding everyone else into austerity.

"Exactly," Clausius said. "A ration book written by the comfortable is often a sermon against the poor."

And he is right. A blanket war on computation would be intellectually lazy and politically dangerous. It could empower bureaucrats to decide which forms of inquiry count as useful before their usefulness is knowable. Fundamental research often looks wasteful until it becomes the grammar of the future. Physics itself would have suffered under committees demanding immediate social return from every strange equation. The universe is full of expensive nonsense that later becomes civilization.

"So you abandon rationing?" he asked.

"No. I abandon stupid rationing."

He smiled. The dead appreciate revisions.

The real policy problem is not whether computation should use energy. It must. The problem is whether computation gets to grow as if energy systems, climate targets, and communities were merely inputs. It should not. A serious society would require large compute operators to disclose electricity use, water use, carbon intensity, grid impacts, and public subsidies in standardized public reports. It would make them pay the full cost of grid upgrades instead of socializing the strain. It would deny sweetheart arrangements that revive or prolong fossil generation under the cover of digital inevitability. It would impose clean-power additionality rules, so a data center claiming renewable energy must help create new clean supply rather than simply buying certificates and leaving everyone else with a dirtier residual grid.

"You are proposing bookkeeping," Clausius said.

"Bookkeeping with teeth."

"Teeth are important."

It would also distinguish workloads by time sensitivity. Not every computation needs to run instantly. Training runs, batch inference, rendering, indexing, and many forms of analysis can shift in time and place to use cleaner or otherwise curtailed electricity. Real-time medical systems and emergency services deserve priority. Advertising auctions and synthetic engagement loops do not. A grid-aware compute regime would treat flexible demand as a civic obligation, not a virtue badge.

The crypto case is harsher. Proof-of-work systems intentionally turn energy into costly proof that someone spent energy. Their defenders argue that this secures decentralized money and resistance to censorship. That argument is not automatically stupid. States and banks have abused financial power. But proof-of-work cannot escape its own moral burden: it must show that the security and social value purchased by the energy are worth the opportunity cost. If a system's primary social effect is speculation, ransomware infrastructure, regulatory evasion, and ideological theater, then "decentralization" becomes a velvet rope around a furnace. A society is entitled to tax or restrict such demand aggressively.

Clausius raised a finger.

"Entitled by what principle?"

By the principle that no private demand has an unlimited claim on shared physical capacity. We already reject unlimited claims when factories poison rivers, when buildings violate fire codes, when vehicles emit beyond standards, when utilities must maintain reliability. Computation is not exempt because its products are abstract. Abstraction is not absolution.

"Good," he said. "But do not make usefulness too narrow."

That is the counterweight. A civilized compute policy must protect public-interest and exploratory computation. It should fund open scientific compute, climate modeling, public health, accessibility tools, libraries, schools, and small actors, instead of letting only giant firms afford compliance. It should create public compute infrastructure whose allocation is debated openly, not hidden inside corporate product roadmaps. It should treat compute as a strategic commons: not free, not sacred, not banned, but governed.

This is where the argument gets properly philosophical. The deepest mistake in the debate is to ask whether intelligence is "worth" energy as if intelligence were one commodity and energy another. Intelligence is not automatically worth anything. Some intelligence is malignant. Some is trivial. Some is liberating. Some is merely the ability to manipulate attention at scale. A recommender system that makes a child lonelier is intelligent only in the way a trap is intelligent. A model that helps discover a low-carbon cement process may deserve a great deal of heat. "More intelligence" is not an answer until we know: intelligence for whom, toward what end, answerable to what restraint?

Clausius looked pleased and irritated, which I took as a good sign.

"You are making entropy into ethics," he said.

"I am making entropy into memory."

That is how his law feels from the inside of a civilization. Entropy is not the cartoon that everything gets messier, therefore despair. It is the record that transformations are not free and cannot be perfectly undone. Once coal is burned, the carbon is in the air. Once a valley is flooded for power, the village is gone. Once a grid is built around giant private loads, future policy inherits that shape. Once a generation is taught to outsource attention to machines optimized for engagement, the lost habits do not return by toggling a setting. Irreversibility is not only thermodynamic. It is institutional.

"Careful again," he said. "You are leaving physics."

"I am extending the metaphor."

"Metaphors are heat engines for lazy thought."

"Then audit mine."

He considered this with the solemnity of a man deciding whether to pass a doctoral exam.

"Entropy does not tell you what to value," he said. "It tells you that value must be paid for in transformations. Your error would be to treat every increase of entropy as a moral injury. That is nonsense. Eating bread, warming a house, printing a book, launching a telescope, saving a child, doing physics: all dissipate. Life is not a protest against entropy. Life is a local, temporary, astonishing way of spending gradients."

"So the question is spending."

"The question is spending with knowledge."

That is the verdict I wanted but could not have honestly reached without him. The right target is not computation as such. The right target is unaccountable, low-value, fossil-backed, publicly subsidized, socially extractive computation that claims the prestige of intelligence while behaving like a heat leak with investors.

The anti-compute position must survive this sentence: if you make energy constraint into hostility toward knowledge, you will hurt exactly the forms of computation that could help us survive. The pro-compute position must survive this sentence: if your intelligence requires hiding its energy, labor, water, emissions, and opportunity costs, then it is not intelligence yet; it is clever appetite.

For AI in particular, the policy should be neither boosterism nor moral panic. It should be triage. Train fewer giant models whose main purpose is leaderboard vanity. Publish energy and emissions accounts for frontier runs. Prefer smaller, specialized, durable systems when they solve the task. Tax wasteful high-volume commercial inference that produces low social value, especially spam, manipulation, and engagement sludge. Require large operators to support new clean generation and flexible demand. Protect scientific, medical, educational, accessibility, and public-interest compute. Kill the lie that efficiency alone is virtue; pair efficiency with caps, prices, and accountability so Jevons does not turn every technical improvement into a larger furnace. Treat proof-of-work crypto as guilty until it demonstrates public value commensurate with its physical cost.

"Guilty until proved useful," Clausius said. "A severe court."

"The atmosphere is a severe court."

The IPCC does not say that modernity must end. It says that the emissions path must change sharply if warming is to be limited ([IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/)). That distinction matters. The goal is not hair-shirt purity, not a museum of low-energy virtue, not an anti-technological romance in which everyone becomes wise by doing less. The goal is adult physics: every watt has a source, every source has a politics, every politics has victims or beneficiaries, and every claimed benefit must stand in the light of what else the energy could have done.

Clausius picked up the dead phone, now charged enough to glow.

"Your age calls this a device," he said. "I call it a contract."

"Between whom?"

"Between ordered energy and desired future."

That was annoyingly good.

Because that is what computation is: a contract. We promise the machine electricity, materials, cooling, and human attention. It promises us prediction, compression, memory, control, artifice, acceleration, maybe insight. The scandal is not that the contract exists. The scandal is that it is often signed without reading the terms. Data centers arrive as economic development. AI arrives as inevitability. Crypto arrives as freedom. Platforms arrive as convenience. Then the invoice comes written in substations, emissions, land, heat, and habits of mind.

The philosophical mistake of the boosters is to confuse capability with justification. The philosophical mistake of the scolds is to confuse cost with refutation. A thing can be costly and worth it. A thing can be brilliant and not worth it. Physics will not adjudicate the worth. It will prevent us from pretending there was no cost.

That is why Clausius remains useful company for the AI century. He does not offer an anti-machine sermon. He offers the colder mercy of accounting. The steam engine did not become evil because it wasted heat; it became modern because people learned to ask how heat, work, and loss actually related. Computation will not become ethical by being declared intelligent, clean, or inevitable. It will become ethical, if at all, when its transformations are made visible enough for politics and conscience to judge them.

The server fan stopped. The brass toy engine cooled. The phone reached 12 percent and looked smug about it.

"Before I go," Clausius said, "answer plainly. Should your society ration computation?"

Yes, but not with a blunt knife and not with nostalgia as the lawgiver. It should ration through truth first: public accounting, real prices, clean-power obligations, grid responsibility, workload priority, and refusal to subsidize low-value waste. It should ration through justice second: protect the poor, public science, medicine, education, accessibility, and democratic access from a future in which only the richest actors can afford machine intelligence. It should ration through courage third: say no to some profitable computation because the world is not improved by every possible transformation.

"And if the machines object?"

"Machines do not object. Their owners do."

He laughed, and for a moment the archive lights flickered as if some old conservation law had enjoyed itself.

Then Clausius lay back down into the warm dust of the century that had misunderstood him the least badly. Before the dead closed over him, he gave me the only blessing a thermodynamicist can offer.

"Do not ask whether intelligence is worth the heat," he said. "Ask whether the heat made you more intelligent."

The charger was still warm after he was gone. I held it until it cooled, because sometimes physics is just the universe making you feel the receipt.
