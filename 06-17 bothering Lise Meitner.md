today is 06-17.

I found Lise Meitner in the churchyard of St James in Bramley, Hampshire, beneath the sentence her nephew Otto Frisch gave her like a final experiment: a physicist who never lost her humanity. The Atomic Heritage Foundation records both the epitaph and the sharper biographical fact that she refused an invitation to the Manhattan Project, saying she would have nothing to do with a bomb ([Atomic Heritage Foundation, "Lise Meitner"](https://ahf.nuclearmuseum.org/ahf/profile/lise-meitner/)). The Science and Technology Facilities Council's visit to the same graveyard calls her best known for not winning the Nobel Prize for the discovery of nuclear fission, which is one of history's quieter ways of saying that credit can be buried while the consequences keep walking around above ground ([STFC, "The Women Behind the Science: Lise Meitner"](https://www.ppd.stfc.ac.uk/Pages/Lise-Meitner.aspx)).

I came before dawn with a Geiger counter, a candle in a jar, a photograph of a cooling tower, a torn page from the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons, and one question that had been dressed up as policy for so long it had begun to smell like theology.

The resurrection was simple, which is to say completely impossible. I placed the Geiger counter on the grave and listened to its little mortal ticks. Then I opened Meitner and Otto Frisch's 1939 paper, the one that explained uranium's splitting as a new kind of nuclear reaction and estimated the released energy from the mass defect ([Meitner and Frisch, "Disintegration of Uranium by Neutrons"](https://www.atomicarchive.com/resources/documents/beginnings/nature_meitner.html)). I read the equations aloud until the candle flame flattened, divided, and became two flames. The soil did not shake. It thought.

Meitner rose with dirt on her coat and the expression of a woman who had been called back from death by someone about to be careless with a distinction.

"If you say atom bomb," she said, "I am going back."

"Not exactly."

"That is how the worst conversations begin."

The question was this: **Should a world trying to decarbonize treat nuclear power as a necessary climate tool and build much more of it, or should it reject nuclear expansion as too slow, expensive, dangerous, unjust, and entangled with weapons, waste, and institutional arrogance?**

This is not an argument about aesthetic preference. The stakes are coal plants and asthma wards, uranium mines and Indigenous land, grid reliability and blackouts, children born into hotter decades, spent fuel that outlives empires, reactor crews, evacuation maps, weapons safeguards, electricity bills, public trust, public debt, and the temptation to call any sufficiently expensive machine a moral solution. At COP28, countries launched a declaration to work toward tripling global nuclear energy capacity by 2050, explicitly tying nuclear expansion to net-zero goals and the 1.5 degree Celsius climate target ([U.S. Department of Energy, Declaration to Triple Nuclear Energy](https://www.energy.gov/articles/cop28-countries-launch-declaration-triple-nuclear-energy-capacity-2050-recognizing-key)). That is not a pamphlet. That is an industrial wager.

Meitner looked toward the dark village.

"Your century is hot enough to frighten itself into intelligence?"

"Not reliably."

"Then begin with the carbon."

Fine. The pro-nuclear case begins with carbon, and it deserves to begin there. The IPCC says the global energy system is the largest source of CO2 emissions and that reducing energy-system emissions is essential to limiting warming ([IPCC AR6 WGIII, Chapter 6](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-6/)). Nuclear power is not a fossil fuel with a public-relations department. It is a low-carbon electricity source capable of producing large amounts of power without the direct combustion emissions of coal or gas. The IPCC's energy chapter says nuclear can deliver low-carbon energy at scale, while also noting the problems that nuclear advocates prefer to mumble through: cost overruns, high upfront investment, final waste disposal, public acceptance, and political support ([IPCC AR6 WGIII, 6.4.2.4](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-6/)).

"So even your citation refuses to become a bumper sticker," Meitner said.

"That is why I like it."

The moral force of nuclear power is not that it is pretty. It is that the alternative, in too many places, is combustion. Coal does not become gentle because the reactor makes people nervous. Gas does not become clean because solar panels are cheaper at noon. A grid is not decarbonized by vibes; it is decarbonized by reliable, low-emission power, transmission, storage, demand flexibility, efficiency, and ruthless displacement of fossil generation. The International Energy Agency argues that nuclear, as an established large-scale low-emissions source, can help decarbonize electricity, and that extending the lives of existing reactors is an important part of a cost-effective path to net zero ([IEA, *Nuclear Power and Secure Energy Transitions*](https://www.iea.org/reports/nuclear-power-and-secure-energy-transitions/executive-summary)).

Meitner bent over the cooling-tower photograph.

"Existing reactors first," she said.

"Because closing them can leave a fossil-shaped hole."

"Because it is less foolish to keep a working bridge than to burn it and promise a better bridge in slides."

That is the first serious distinction. Keeping safely operating nuclear plants open is not the same question as building a new fleet. A reactor already built has paid most of its carbon and capital debt. Shutting it down early can force a grid to replace firm low-carbon output with gas or coal while renewables and storage catch up. In that case, anti-nuclear purity may function as fossil fuel's valet. It bows beautifully while opening the door.

But new nuclear is a different animal. It must be financed, sited, permitted, built, connected, staffed, regulated, insured, fueled, secured, maintained, decommissioned, and defended politically for decades. The IEA's newer report on a possible nuclear revival says rising momentum faces real challenges in policy, construction, and financing ([IEA, *The Path to a New Era for Nuclear Energy*](https://www.iea.org/reports/the-path-to-a-new-era-for-nuclear-energy)). The word "challenge" is polite. Nuclear construction can become a museum of optimistic spreadsheets.

"Do not let the engineers call delay an implementation detail," Meitner said.

"Do not let activists call delay a refutation."

She nodded. We had found the first fight.

The anti-nuclear side is right that time matters. A plant that arrives in 2045 cannot reduce emissions in 2027. Money matters too. A dollar locked in a project that overruns by years is a dollar not spent on insulation, heat pumps, grid upgrades, geothermal, demand response, batteries, wind, solar, public transit, or closing coal. Opportunity cost is not an accountant's revenge; it is ethics under scarcity. If a government invokes nuclear power mainly to avoid building transmission lines, confronting utilities, permitting renewables, or reducing fossil extraction, nuclear becomes not a climate tool but a delay costume.

Meitner smiled without pleasure.

"The atom as procrastination. Elegant and cowardly."

Still, the anti-nuclear case often cheats by acting as if speed belongs only to its preferred technologies. Solar and wind can be built fast, but a grid dominated by variable renewables needs transmission, storage, flexible demand, firm clean backup, market reform, and interconnection queues that do not behave like adult institutions. Hydro is geographically constrained. Fossil gas with carbon capture remains an alibi in many hands. Long-duration storage is improving but not magic. Geothermal is promising but uneven. The question is not whether nuclear beats an imaginary perfect grid. The question is whether excluding nuclear makes the actual grid cleaner, faster, fairer, and more reliable.

"You are refusing everyone the pleasure of being simple," Meitner said.

"That may be the whole essay."

Then the Geiger counter ticked, as if objecting to rhetoric on behalf of waste.

Nuclear waste is the second fight because it is where pro-nuclear people too often become unbearably smug. They say the volume is small, which is true compared with the vast atmospheric dump of fossil carbon. They say deep geological disposal is technically understood, which is more defensible than many opponents admit. The EPA describes geologic disposal as placing carefully packaged radioactive materials in deep underground tunnels, and the NRC's high-level waste page records the long-running Yucca Mountain licensing history in the United States ([EPA, radioactive waste FAQ](https://www.epa.gov/radiation/frequent-questions-radioactive-waste); [NRC, high-level waste disposal](https://www.nrc.gov/waste/hlw-disposal)). But "technically understood" is not the same as politically accomplished, socially consented, ethically complete, or already done.

"A waste problem has two half-lives," Meitner said. "The isotope and the lie."

The lie is not that waste exists. Everyone serious admits that. The lie is that because the technical problem has plausible solutions, the institutional problem is solved. It is not. A civilization that struggles to maintain bridges and remember treaties should speak humbly before promising stewardship across geological time. A repository is not merely an engineering project. It is a promise made to strangers whose languages, governments, maps, and resentments we cannot know. To call that promise impossible is too easy. To call it trivial is obscene.

Meitner touched the torn NPT page.

"And the weapons?"

The weapons are the third fight, and here her grave grew colder. Civilian nuclear power is not identical to nuclear weapons. The Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons explicitly tries to hold three ideas together: preventing nuclear weapons spread, pursuing disarmament, and preserving cooperation in peaceful nuclear uses ([UN Office for Disarmament Affairs, NPT](https://disarmament.unoda.org/en/our-work/weapons-mass-destruction/nuclear-weapons/treaty-non-proliferation-nuclear-weapons/)). Article IV recognizes the right to peaceful nuclear energy, but only in conformity with the treaty's nonproliferation obligations. The IAEA says safeguards are meant to deter nuclear weapons spread through early detection of misuse of nuclear material or technology ([IAEA, basics of safeguards](https://www.iaea.org/topics/basics-of-iaea-safeguards)).

So the right sentence is not "reactors make bombs." That is sloppy. The right sentence is "nuclear technology requires institutions that can keep peaceful uses peaceful." Tripling nuclear capacity would also expand the amount of material, expertise, facilities, and international verification work needing competent oversight. The IAEA itself notes that a global commitment to expand nuclear energy would increase nuclear material and facilities subject to safeguards ([IAEA Bulletin, "What the Nuclear Declaration at COP28 Means for IAEA Verification"](https://www.iaea.org/bulletin/what-the-nuclear-declaration-at-cop28-means-for-iaea-verification)).

"Competent oversight," Meitner said. "An endangered species."

"Not extinct."

"Then breed it before the reactors."

This is the Meitner rule: do not ask whether a technology can work in paradise. Ask whether the institutions holding it can remain honest under fear, profit, nationalism, incompetence, war, and boredom. Fission did not command Hiroshima. Human institutions did. Fission did not command electricity either. Human institutions built that too. Technology expands the range of possible action. It does not absolve the actor.

Then she asked the question the climate debate hates.

"Who mines the salvation?"

The fourth fight is justice. Nuclear power does not begin at the reactor fence. It begins in geology, labor, land, and consent. In the United States, the EPA says nearly 30 million tons of uranium ore were extracted from Navajo lands between 1944 and 1986, leaving abandoned mines and contamination risks that federal agencies are still working to reduce ([EPA, Navajo Nation uranium cleanup](https://www.epa.gov/navajo-nation-uranium-cleanup)). The Skyline abandoned uranium mine is one of roughly 520 abandoned uranium mines on the Navajo Nation, according to EPA response materials ([EPA, Skyline AUM](https://response.epa.gov/SkylineAUM)).

"There," Meitner said. "The glowing future has a reservation address."

Yes. Any nuclear revival that treats uranium communities as a footnote is not climate policy. It is extraction with a cleaner smokestack. If nuclear power is to be defended, the defense must include mine cleanup, worker protection, Indigenous consent, transport safety, transparent tailings management, and compensation for harms already done. No one gets to say "net zero" over contaminated water and imagine the phrase purifies the well.

The pro-nuclear answer is that fossil fuels also have mines, spills, sacrifice zones, wars, refineries, pipelines, ash ponds, poisoned air, and a planetary waste stream called the atmosphere. That answer is true. It is also insufficient. Comparative guilt is not moral hygiene. If nuclear is better than coal, prove it all the way down: not only in carbon intensity, but in land relations, waste governance, labor conditions, emergency planning, and honesty about risk.

Meitner looked almost pleased.

"Make them defend the whole chain."

"Everyone?"

"Especially everyone."

So we turned to accidents, because no nuclear argument avoids the dead cities in the mind. Chernobyl and Fukushima are not rhetorical ornaments. They are names attached to evacuation, fear, contamination, thyroid cancer, lost homes, institutional failure, and decades of argument over what radiation did and what panic did. UNSCEAR's Chernobyl materials treat thyroid cancer after the accident as a major issue requiring serious evaluation ([UNSCEAR, Chernobyl](https://www.unscear.org/unscear/en/chernobyl.html)). For Fukushima, UNSCEAR's 2020/2021 report assessed evidence through 2019, and an accompanying UN release said radiation-linked future health effects were unlikely to be discernible ([UNSCEAR 2020/2021 Fukushima report](https://www.unscear.org/unscear/en/publications/2020_2021_2.html); [UN release on UNSCEAR Fukushima findings](https://www.unscear.org/unscear/en/news/content/a-decade-after-the-fukushima-accident_-radiation-linked-increases-in-cancer-rates-not-expected-to-be-seen.html)).

The moral lesson is not "accidents are imaginary." They are not. The lesson is that risk must be compared without theatrical bookkeeping. Coal's ordinary operation kills invisibly through air pollution and visibly through mining. Gas leaks methane, explodes houses, and cooks the climate while wearing the costume of moderation. Oil has its own graveyards. Nuclear accidents are rare, spectacular, politically devastating, and psychologically enormous. Fossil harms are routine, distributed, normalized, and therefore easier for respectable people to ignore.

"Humans fear the dramatic corpse," Meitner said. "They dine with the slow one."

That is the anti-anti-nuclear argument in one sentence. Fear should count, but it should not govern by stage lighting. If a reactor's possible catastrophe terrifies us more than coal's guaranteed damage, that may reveal something about narrative, not only ethics. A serious safety culture must prevent nuclear accidents. A serious moral culture must also count the dead who never receive documentaries.

Still, pro-nuclear rhetoric has its own indecent habit: converting every objection into ignorance. People mistrust nuclear power not only because they misunderstand radiation, but because institutions have lied, cut corners, hidden failures, dumped burdens on weaker communities, overpromised, underdelivered, militarized the atom, and wrapped secrecy in expertise. Trust is not a pamphlet you hand to the public after making the expensive decisions. Trust is built by being trustworthy before permission is requested.

Meitner's face changed when I said that.

"The public is not irrational for remembering."

"No."

"Good. Scientists dislike being judged by history. This is because history reads lab notebooks with better lighting."

The fifth fight is democracy. Nuclear power concentrates expertise. It asks ordinary people to trust reactor designers, regulators, utilities, contractors, emergency planners, waste managers, fuel suppliers, international inspectors, and politicians. Some concentration of expertise is unavoidable. I do not want a reactor licensed by plebiscite any more than I want brain surgery by town hall. But technocracy becomes obscene when it treats public consent as delay, local fear as stupidity, and opposition as a communications problem.

The climate crisis tempts us toward emergency politics: build fast, override objections, trust the experts, stop letting procedure kill the future. The temptation is understandable. It is also dangerous. Climate change itself is partly the product of experts and executives assuring publics that a profitable energy system was manageable. The cure cannot simply be another priesthood of infrastructure.

"Then nothing gets built," Meitner said, now arguing the other side with alarming discipline.

"Some things get built with consent."

"Consent is slow."

"So is litigation after contempt."

She laughed. Death had not cured her of enjoying a clean hit.

The pro-nuclear democrats have a case here. Rich societies routinely weaponize process to block everything: apartments, transmission lines, rail, wind, solar, storage, and yes, reactors. Endless veto points are not justice. They can preserve fossil dependence while calling themselves participation. A community's right not to be poisoned is sacred. A homeowner's desire never to see infrastructure is not. If every local process can quietly defend the carbon status quo, then democracy has learned to filibuster physics.

But emergency cannot mean blank checks. The right model is not "shut up and build." It is "decide openly, compensate fairly, regulate fiercely, share benefits, honor consent where sovereignty requires consent, and make delay justify itself." For nuclear, this means independent regulators with teeth, standardized designs where possible, transparent safety cases, honest cost accounting, community benefit agreements that are not bribes, waste plans that are more than hand gestures, and no siting pattern that treats poor, rural, colonized, or Indigenous communities as the obvious place to put the hard parts.

"You are writing conditions," Meitner said.

"Yes."

"Conditions are where enthusiasm goes to mature or die."

Here are mine.

First: **do not close safely operating nuclear plants when the practical replacement is fossil generation.** If a plant is genuinely unsafe, close it. If it is politically unloved but technically sound, count the carbon before striking the pose. Climate seriousness begins by not destroying low-carbon capacity for applause.

Second: **build renewables, transmission, storage, geothermal, efficiency, and demand flexibility as fast as the grid can honestly absorb them.** Nuclear is not an excuse to underbuild the cheaper and faster tools. Any politician who points at a hypothetical reactor while blocking a real transmission line should be treated as a fossil fuel lobbyist with better stationery.

Third: **new nuclear should be pursued where the state and utility system can actually build it, finance it, regulate it, and use it.** That means standardized designs, credible schedules, public cost transparency, workforce capacity, supply-chain realism, and a grid need for firm low-carbon power. It does not mean every country should buy a reactor-shaped identity.

Fourth: **no nuclear expansion without waste governance.** A country does not need a completed repository before every reactor operates, but it needs a lawful, funded, consent-based, technically credible waste pathway and institutions that cannot simply hand tomorrow a glowing invoice.

Fifth: **no nuclear expansion without justice in the fuel chain.** Uranium mining, milling, transport, conversion, enrichment, and waste cannot be externalized onto communities with the least power to refuse. Cleanup of legacy harms, including abandoned uranium mines on Navajo lands, belongs in the nuclear ledger, not in a separate moral basement ([EPA, Navajo Nation uranium cleanup](https://www.epa.gov/navajo-nation-uranium-cleanup)).

Sixth: **no nuclear expansion without safeguards.** The NPT bargain and IAEA verification system are not decorative international wallpaper. If the world triples nuclear capacity, it must also fund and strengthen the institutions meant to keep nuclear material in peaceful use ([IAEA safeguards](https://www.iaea.org/topics/safeguards-and-verification)).

Seventh: **do not sell nuclear power as redemption.** Sell it, if at all, as one difficult tool among difficult tools. The climate emergency does not need a messiah technology. It needs competent pluralism with moral accounting.

Meitner read the list.

"This will annoy everyone."

"That is how I know it has a pulse."

"No. That is how you know you have annoyed everyone. Do not mistake irritation for truth."

Fair. The dead are useful because they do not let style impersonate rigor.

So I tried to say the thing plainly. I do not believe nuclear power is morally mandatory everywhere. I do not believe it is morally forbidden. I believe existing safe nuclear plants are too valuable to discard casually. I believe new nuclear can be justified in places that need firm low-carbon power and have the institutional capacity to build and govern it. I believe nuclear boosterism often underestimates cost, time, waste, mining, proliferation, and trust. I believe anti-nuclear absolutism often underestimates fossil harm, grid complexity, and the cruelty of preferring a purer plan that arrives too late. I believe the climate crisis has made both camps morally lazier than they can afford to be.

Meitner tapped the Meitner-Frisch paper with one dirt-dark finger.

"Fission was not a moral instruction," she said.

"No."

"It was an opening."

"To bombs and reactors."

"To responsibility."

That was the word her grave had been waiting for. Responsibility is not fear. It is not enthusiasm. It is not the adolescent wish to be innocent by refusing every dangerous tool, nor the industrial wish to be innocent by deploying every profitable one. Responsibility is the adult knowledge that some powers are too dangerous to romanticize and too useful to abandon without argument.

The most dishonest pro-nuclear sentence is: "The waste problem is solved." The most dishonest anti-nuclear sentence is: "We do not need it." The honest versions are harder. Waste can be managed only by institutions that keep promises longer than markets prefer. Some grids may not need much nuclear; others may need it badly if they are to stop burning carbon while keeping lights, hospitals, factories, heat pumps, trains, and water systems alive. Climate policy is not a purity test administered from a country with convenient geography. It is a set of engineering and justice problems under a closing sky.

"Closing sky," Meitner said. "A little much."

"It is a daily essay."

"Still."

I deserved that.

The dawn came up gray over Bramley. The Geiger counter had gone quiet or I had stopped hearing it. Meitner stood with one hand on her own stone, which is a posture only the dead can manage without melodrama.

"You want my verdict," she said.

"Yes."

"Do not build bombs and call them peace. Do not build reactors and call them innocence. Do not burn coal and call yourself prudent. Do not poison miners and call it clean. Do not leave waste to people whose names you will never learn. Do not let fear do arithmetic. Do not let arithmetic erase fear. If you use the nucleus, deserve it."

That is not a policy, but it is a standard.

My answer, for today, is this: **the climate movement should stop treating nuclear power as either savior or sin. Keep safe existing reactors open when they prevent fossil generation. Build new nuclear only where it is faster than fantasy, governed better than history, financed without cannibalizing quicker decarbonization, tied to real waste and justice obligations, and embedded in serious nonproliferation safeguards. Reject both the fossil-funded sneer that nuclear is impossible and the technocratic sales pitch that the atom can wash its hands.**

Meitner began to fade at the edges, not like smoke but like a calculation returning to the notebook.

"And humanity?" I asked.

She looked at the epitaph.

"Not a possession," she said. "A discipline."

Then the grave was only a grave again, and the village began its ordinary electricity: kettles, lights, chargers, refrigerators, all the small dependencies by which ethics enters the wall socket and pretends it is not there.
