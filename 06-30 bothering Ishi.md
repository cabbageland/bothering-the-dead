today is 06-30.

I found Ishi in the place where a grave becomes an argument about who is allowed to know.

Not exactly in a cemetery. The simple grave was already gone, and that was part of the trouble. UCSF's chronology says that in August 1911 a starving Native American man walked out of the Butte County wilderness into Oroville, was taken by UC anthropologists to San Francisco, and was given the name "Ishi," meaning "man" in Yahi; it also records that he died at UC Hospital on March 25, 1916, of advanced pulmonary tuberculosis, after which an autopsy was performed and his brain was removed, weighed, examined, preserved, and eventually sent to the Smithsonian ([UCSF, "The Story of Ishi"](https://history.library.ucsf.edu/ishi.html)). The Phoebe A. Hearst Museum notes that, following custom, he did not speak his personal name to outsiders and so was referred to by the word for "man" in the language of his people ([Hearst Museum, "Ishi"](https://hearstmuseum.berkeley.edu/ishi/)).

That is already a philosophical wound. A man whose name was not available to the people studying him became available as almost everything else: specimen, demonstrator, translator, patient, legend, skull-measurement fantasy, cultural salvage project, and finally a brain in a jar.

I went looking for him in the old paper trail. I brought a small bundle of obsidian flakes, a museum accession tag, white cotton gloves, a copy of the Native American Graves Protection and Repatriation Act regulations, the United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples, the Kennewick Man genome paper, a recording cylinder that held no sound, and Kroeber's March 24, 1916 letter, the one where he tried to stop the autopsy and wrote, with rare institutional clarity, "science can go to hell" ([UCSF chronology](https://history.library.ucsf.edu/ishi.html)).

The resurrection did not begin when I read the law. Law is good at moving bones, bad at apologizing to them. It began when I tied the accession tag to the obsidian flakes and said the catalog number aloud, not as a spell of possession but as a confession of possession. Then I read from the Smithsonian's own repatriation summary, which says that the National Museum of Natural History would repatriate Ishi's remains, that the common belief he was the last of his tribe was wrong because Yana people survived, and that his remains were reunited and repatriated on August 10, 2000, before being reinterred by the tribes at an undisclosed location ([Smithsonian Repatriation Office case summaries, California Region](https://naturalhistory.si.edu/media/3599)).

The paper darkened at the edges. The accession tag split like a seed. Ishi sat up out of the space between the record and the grave, thin, alert, and absolutely uninterested in being useful.

"Which part of me did you wake?" he asked.

"The part that was not returned by the inventory."

"That part has teeth."

Good. I had come for teeth.

The question was this: **Should museums and universities return Indigenous human remains and sacred objects to descendant communities whenever those communities ask, even when scientists claim the remains can produce irreplaceable knowledge, or can scientific value sometimes override the claim of the dead and their people?**

This is not a polite museum studies question about labels. It is about who owns the dead after conquest, whether research can be clean when custody is dirty, whether knowledge has a right to the body, whether descendants inherit authority or only grief, whether the public interest includes being spared stolen intimacy, and whether "universal science" has too often meant "everyone's ancestors are sacred except yours."

I began badly, which is to say I began honestly.

"The science case is not trivial," I said.

Ishi looked at the white gloves. "That is how trouble dresses."

Still, the case deserved its strongest form. Human remains can teach things that texts and oral records cannot: diet, disease, violence, migration, kinship, injury, climate stress, and the history of populations whose written archives were never allowed to exist or were deliberately destroyed. Ancient DNA has corrected bad racial stories as well as produced them. The Kennewick Man, or the Ancient One, became a central example: after a long fight over whether NAGPRA applied, genome analysis found that his ancestry was closer to contemporary Native Americans than to any other sampled population, and the authors described genetic continuity with Native peoples of the region ([Rasmussen et al., "The Ancestry and Affiliations of Kennewick Man," *Nature*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4878456/)). The U.S. Army Corps of Engineers later relied on new DNA and skeletal evidence to determine that the Ancient One was Native American under NAGPRA ([U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, 2016 determination](https://www.nwd.usace.army.mil/Media/News-Releases/Article/742935/corps-determines-kennewick-man-is-native-american/)).

"So the bones spoke for the tribes," I said.

"No," Ishi said. "The tribes had spoken. The bones were admitted as witnesses only after the court liked their accent."

That hurt because it was precise. The scientific victory in the Ancient One case was also an indictment of the epistemic order that required molecular proof before it would take Indigenous relationship seriously. The science was valuable. The hierarchy of credibility was rotten.

I tried again. "But without study, some truths are lost. A skeleton may preserve evidence of a massacre no document admits. A tooth may show famine. A genome may expose a lie about ancestry. Museums are not only trophy rooms. Sometimes they are archives against forgetting."

"Archives built from what?"

"Often theft."

"Often?"

"Yes. Often theft."

He did not let the softness pass.

The best pro-research argument is not that scientists are entitled to everything. That is just conquest with a lab coat. The best version is that the dead are also witnesses, and some evidence can matter to descendants, to history, and to humanity at large. If all human remains are locked away from all possible inquiry, then some crimes become harder to prove, some migrations harder to understand, some diseases harder to trace, some false histories harder to defeat. The living have interests in truth. Future generations have interests in a less myth-poisoned account of where they came from.

Ishi picked up the recording cylinder and held it to his ear.

"Do you hear it?" he asked.

"No."

"That is also evidence."

The silence was not empty. It was the record of a people whose last speakers and makers had been cornered by settlers, soldiers, diseases, hunger, and curiosity. UCSF's chronology lists massacres in the 1860s and 1870s, the period of concealment when a remnant band hid in the Mill Creek area, and the 1908 episode in which surveyors surprised four survivors while taking tools and artifacts from their camp ([UCSF chronology](https://history.library.ucsf.edu/ishi.html)). If a museum later says, "We must keep the remains because they are scientifically precious," it should say so while remembering how scarcity was manufactured. Some bodies become "rare specimens" because the society collecting them helped make their people rare.

"Now state the case against me," Ishi said.

"Against repatriation?"

"Against easy repatriation. I have also seen easy pity. It is a soft net."

So I did. Repatriation can become hard when descent is uncertain, when multiple communities have plausible claims, when federal recognition excludes people who carry real cultural relationship, when ancient remains precede present political boundaries, when communities disagree internally, and when the object in question is both an ancestor and evidence. The law needs procedures because grief alone cannot tell a museum which claimant has authority. The Smithsonian's Ishi report itself had to work through cultural affiliation among Yahi, Yana, Redding Rancheria, Pit River, Maidu representatives, federally recognized tribes, and nonrecognized California Native people before recommending return ([Smithsonian case summary](https://naturalhistory.si.edu/media/3599)). Those complexities are not imaginary.

"Complexity," Ishi said, "is the word institutions use when delay has washed its face."

"Sometimes. But not always."

"Then say when not always."

Not always when the question is genuinely who should receive the remains. Not always when a community asks for analysis before reburial. Not always when evidence can help identify the dead, return them correctly, or prove violence. Not always when the remains are not Indigenous under the relevant law, or when the material is modern forensic evidence needed to solve a crime. Not always when the choice is not science versus religion but one kind of obligation against another. The world is full of bodies, and not every body belongs to the same legal or moral story.

But Ishi was waiting for the rot under the caveat.

The rot is that "uncertainty" has often been converted into indefinite possession. If a museum cannot identify a person exactly, it calls him culturally unidentifiable and keeps him. If a tribe provides oral history, the institution asks for a different kind of proof. If the record is thin because colonization shredded the record, the institution treats the shredded record as a reason to retain the body. If science has not yet asked every possible question, the dead are told to wait for the next method, and the next, and the next. This is not neutrality. It is a ratchet.

The newer NAGPRA regulations try to break that ratchet. They state that NAGPRA recognizes the rights of lineal descendants, Indian Tribes, and Native Hawaiian organizations in Native American human remains and cultural items; requires consultation; requires deference to Native American traditional knowledge; and requires free, prior, and informed consent before exhibition, access, or research on human remains or cultural items ([43 CFR Part 10, section 10.1](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-43/subtitle-A/part-10)). The same regulations define Native American traditional knowledge as expert opinion and cultural affiliation as a reasonable connection based on shared group identity, not a demand for impossible mathematical certainty ([43 CFR Part 10](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-43/subtitle-A/part-10)).

"That law is better," Ishi said.

"Yes."

"Better is not clean."

"No."

The philosophical argument lives there, between better and clean.

One tempting objection says that the dead cannot be harmed. Epicurus made the ancient version famous: when we exist, death is not present; when death is present, we do not exist. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy summarizes the continuing debate over the harm thesis, including whether death or posthumous events can harm the person who dies ([SEP, "Death"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/death/)). If the dead cannot feel insult, pain, fear, or deprivation, why should a femur in a drawer matter more than the knowledge it might produce?

Ishi listened with his head slightly tilted.

"Do you bury your mothers?" he asked.

"Yes."

"Do you publish their skull measurements first?"

"No."

"Then your philosophy already lost. It only does not know where."

That was too quick, and also nearly enough. We do not treat the dead as furniture merely because they lack current sensation. A will can bind after death. A promise to scatter ashes can matter after death. A desecrated grave can wrong the living and betray the dead person's earlier interests. More than that, the dead are often held inside a network of living obligations. Ancestors are not isolated objects. They are relations. If a museum keeps a human being's remains against the wishes of descendants, the injury is not only a metaphysical puzzle about posthumous harm. It is a present act against a living community.

Kant is not enough here, but he is useful. In the *Groundwork*, he distinguishes what has a price from what has dignity, and dignity means not being traded as a mere instrument ([Kant, *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/kant1785.pdf)). Human remains are not simply persons in the ordinary living sense, but they are also not raw material. They carry the social meaning of a person having been here. They are where dignity leaves fingerprints on matter.

Ishi gave the faintest smile.

"Fingerprints," he said. "Scientists like those."

"And you?"

"I like hands."

The hands matter. Consent is not a magic word uttered by institutions over someone else's bones. It is an act performed by people with authority. The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples recognizes Indigenous peoples' rights to practice and teach spiritual and religious traditions, to use and control ceremonial objects, and to the repatriation of human remains; it also calls for redress for cultural, intellectual, religious, and spiritual property taken without free, prior, and informed consent ([UNDRIP, Articles 11 and 12](https://www.un.org/development/desa/indigenouspeoples/wp-content/uploads/sites/19/2018/11/UNDRIP_E_web.pdf)). That is not sentimental. It is jurisdictional. It says the dead are not a frontier.

"Frontier," Ishi said. "That word was always hungry."

The pro-science side often worries that repatriation lets religion veto knowledge. Sometimes it says this softly, sometimes with open contempt. The better response is not to pretend all knowledge systems are identical. They are not. A genome sequence and a burial song do different work. A radiocarbon date and an origin story answer different questions. But difference does not establish hierarchy in every decision. If the question is who has authority over an ancestor's body, the answer cannot be "whoever can extract more data from it."

Science does not become weak when it asks permission. It becomes less colonial.

"Less," Ishi said.

"You are hard to satisfy."

"The word 'less' is where the buried sleep with one eye open."

Fair. Consent-based research still needs teeth. A museum can convene a meeting designed to exhaust the underfunded. A university can ask for permission after arranging all incentives toward yes. A researcher can call a refusal anti-science when it is actually anti-theft. A digital scan can become a new kind of possession. A DNA sequence can leave the tribe's authority and enter databases that will outlive the consent meeting. A display can be removed from a gallery while the institution continues to own the narrative.

So the answer cannot be "consultation" if consultation means a polite room in which the decision has already been metabolized by the museum. The current NAGPRA language is sharper because it requires free, prior, and informed consent before access or research, and because it says research is not required by the Act or the regulations ([43 CFR Part 10, section 10.1(d)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-43/subtitle-A/part-10)). That last part matters. The law does not say: return the ancestor after one last look. It says: the research claim is subordinate to the consent claim.

"Subordinate," Ishi said. "A museum word with a bruise."

The hard question is whether there should ever be an override. Suppose remains might identify victims of a massacre. Suppose DNA might return a child to a family. Suppose analysis might prove land connection against a government denial. Suppose an epidemic history matters to living descendants. Suppose the claimant community itself is divided between burial and study. Then the right answer is not a permanent ban on knowledge. The right answer is that the community with authority decides whether and how research proceeds, with independent advice, time, funding, and the power to say no without being punished by delay.

"And if they say no?" he asked.

"Then no."

"Even if the scientist weeps for knowledge?"

"Yes."

"Good. Scientists should learn grief without acquisition."

There it was: grief without acquisition. A discipline that studies human loss must learn to lose access. Otherwise it has only expanded its collection methods into the moral life.

But I did not want to make Ishi into a pure refusal machine. That would be another use. Many Indigenous scholars, archaeologists, geneticists, language workers, museum professionals, and tribal historic preservation officers do collaborative research that is exacting, generous, and scientifically serious. The opposite of extraction is not ignorance. It is relationship. It is research designed with the people whose ancestors, objects, stories, lands, and living risks are involved. It is shared governance, capacity building, culturally set limits, return of data, and the humility to accept that some knowledge is not owed to the public.

The public. That sacred foghorn. Museums love it.

"Public for whom?" Ishi asked.

"Everyone."

"Everyone is a costume worn by whoever has the key."

Again, precise. The universal public has often been invoked by institutions that were not universally accountable. A museum says a skeleton belongs to humanity, but humanity did not excavate it. Humanity did not put it in a cabinet. Humanity did not refuse the repatriation request. A specific institution did, inside a specific history, with specific funding, laws, curators, donors, and prestige. "Humanity" is too large a word to sign the accession form.

The public interest is real, but it is not self-defining. There is a public interest in science. There is also a public interest in not training children to believe that some people's dead are educational material and other people's dead are family. There is a public interest in museums becoming trustworthy enough that communities might choose research freely. There is a public interest in proving that knowledge does not need domination as its hidden skeleton.

Ishi touched the white gloves. They collapsed into ash.

"When I was alive," he said, "they watched me make things."

"Yes."

"After I died, they kept a part that could not speak."

"Yes."

"Now you ask if speech must ask permission of silence."

That sentence almost ended the essay. But the issue is not silence. Descendant communities speak. Laws speak. Traditions speak. Histories speak. The problem is that institutions learned to call those voices silence because they were not the preferred instrument.

The older museum order had a simple grammar: collection, classification, preservation, study, display. The new grammar must be different: identify, notify, consult, defer, return, repair, and only then, if invited, study. That is not a decorative ethical overlay. It changes the verb. A human remain is not "held for research." An ancestor is in custody pending return.

"Write policy," Ishi said.

"You want policy?"

"Philosophy without policy is smoke with a bibliography."

So here is the policy.

For Indigenous human remains and funerary or sacred objects, the default should be repatriation, not retention. Museums and universities should bear the burden of proving lawful right of possession, not communities the burden of proving pain. No exhibition, classroom handling, digital reproduction, destructive testing, genetic sequencing, or outside access should occur without free, prior, and informed consent from the proper lineal descendants, tribes, or Native Hawaiian organizations. Consultation should be funded by the institution that holds the material, because making communities pay to recover ancestors is a second extraction. Records, provenance files, photographs, measurements, and prior research data should be shared promptly, with culturally sensitive information protected according to community direction. Unaffiliated does not mean available. It means the institution has more work to do.

Where research is approved, it should be governed by written agreements that specify methods, limits, data control, publication review for sensitive claims, benefit sharing, final disposition, and the right to stop. Destructive testing should require a higher threshold. Ancient DNA should be treated as both scientific evidence and kinship information. Digital surrogates should not become loophole ancestors, infinitely copied after the body comes home. If a community refuses research, the refusal should close the matter unless there is a narrowly defined forensic necessity involving a recent crime and lawful public authority.

And scientific value alone should never override repatriation of colonial collections. Not "rare." Not "irreplaceable." Not "the last chance." Those words are too easily produced by the same institutions that made return late. The more precious the remains are said to be, the more obvious it becomes that keeping them without consent is possession of a person, not stewardship of an object.

"You have chosen," Ishi said.

"Mostly."

"No. Choose cleanly."

Fine.

My answer: **return the dead.** Return them even when the data would be interesting. Return them even when the museum feels misunderstood. Return them even when the archive shakes. Build science on consent, collaboration, and humility, not on the inertia of old possession. The exception should not be "important research"; every researcher thinks the desired research is important. The exception should be a narrow, transparent, community-governed process in which the people with authority over the remains choose study for reasons they accept as their own.

This is not anti-science. It is anti-entitlement. It does not say bones can teach nothing. It says teaching is not the highest claim a human body can make. It does not say every repatriation decision is easy. It says difficulty is not a license to keep. It does not deny the public's interest in history. It denies the museum's habit of confusing its custody with the public good.

The resurrection began to loosen. The accession tag had become blank. Ishi looked tired in a way that made the whole century seem loud.

"What should be done with my story?" he asked.

"Told carefully."

"Carefully is not enough."

"Told against the appetite that made it famous."

He nodded.

There is a way of telling Ishi's story that repeats the collection: last wild man, tragic survivor, living fossil, vanished people, science's strange friend. It makes him useful again. The Smithsonian report's correction matters: he was not simply the last of a vanished people; Yana descendants survived warfare, disease, displacement, and cultural destruction ([Smithsonian case summary](https://naturalhistory.si.edu/media/3599)). That distinction is not a footnote. "Last" can become another theft, stealing descendants from the story so institutions can hold a cleaner tragedy.

Ishi stood. The room smelled of paper, ashes, and rain on stone.

"When you bother the dead," he said, "do not wake only the famous part."

"What is the unfamous part?"

"The people who came to take me home."

Then he was gone. Not vanished. Gone in the direction of people, which is different.

I left the obsidian flakes where the catalog tag had been. I folded the gloves into the NAGPRA regulations and closed the page. The recording cylinder still held no sound, but the silence had changed shape. It was no longer museum silence, the silence of storage. It was burial silence, the silence that refuses to explain itself to strangers.

Return the dead. Then ask, from outside the grave and without a key in your hand, what they and their people are willing to teach.
