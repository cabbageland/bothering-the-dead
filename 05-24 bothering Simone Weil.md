today is May 24, 2026.

I found Simone Weil in Ashford, Kent, in the Catholic section of Bybrook Cemetery, where a woman who never quite consented to belonging anywhere was buried in 1943 after dying in exile in England ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Simone Weil"](https://iep.utm.edu/weil/); [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Simone Weil"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/simone-weil/)). It felt right that the grave was modest. Grandeur would have lied about her. Weil spent her short life moving toward places where comfort was least defensible: classrooms, picket lines, factory floors, the Spanish Civil War, refugee work, wartime London. Even her philosophy distrusted upholstered thought. She wanted ideas tested where humiliation, labor, force, and hunger made abstractions answerable ([SEP, "Simone Weil"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/simone-weil/); [IEP, "Simone Weil"](https://iep.utm.edu/weil/)).

I did not bring candles. Weil would have despised the theater of vague reverence. I brought a field radio, a targeting camera torn from a commercial drone, a printout of the Pentagon's current directive on autonomy in weapon systems, and a packet of her own essays: ["The Iliad, or the Poem of Force"](https://xenotheka.delbeke.arch.ethz.ch/weil-iliad/), ["Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies with a View to the Love of God"](https://consortium.kepler.education/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Right-Use-of-School-Studies-Simone-Weil.pdf), and ["Human Personality"](https://www2.hawaii.edu/~freeman/courses/phil330/16.%20Human%20Personality.pdf). Then I set the drone camera on the headstone and tuned the radio until it found the flat bureaucratic voice of the present saying what all empires say when they have taught a machine to kill: there will be safeguards, there will be judgment, there will be responsibility ([U.S. Department of Defense release on Directive 3000.09](https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/3278076/dod-announces-update-to-dod-directive-300009-autonomy-in-weapon-systems/)).

The dirt shifted as if irritated by euphemism. Weil rose with the severe thinness of someone whom history had never allowed the luxury of being merely symbolic. She looked first at the drone camera, then at me, and only then at her own essays, as if verifying whether I had brought her back for a real argument or for one more round of tasteful literary necromancy.

I told her the question at once, because she was not a person to warm slowly into the essential matter. Should states ban autonomous weapons that can select and strike targets without direct human choice, especially weapons aimed at human beings, or is rigorous regulation enough so long as humans remain somewhere in the chain of authorization?

That is not a niche dispute for defense panels. It concerns whether organized killing will continue to require a person to look, decide, and bear the burden of having decided, or whether that burden will be thinned into system design, sensor thresholds, and after-action reviews. The International Committee of the Red Cross defines autonomous weapon systems, in its own usage, as weapons that select and apply force to targets without human intervention after activation, and it warns that the resulting loss of human judgment creates humanitarian, legal, and ethical dangers ([ICRC, "What you need to know about autonomous weapons"](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/what-you-need-know-about-autonomous-weapons)). The U.S. Department of Defense, by contrast, says its directive is meant to ensure "appropriate levels of human judgment over the use of force" while still permitting the development and use of autonomous and semi-autonomous systems under specified review and testing requirements ([DoD release on Directive 3000.09](https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/3278076/dod-announces-update-to-dod-directive-300009-autonomy-in-weapon-systems/)). So the live controversy is plain: prohibition or managed permission.

Weil listened without interruption. Then she touched the drone camera with one finger, not delicately, more like a pathologist checking whether a specimen still twitched.

Her opening argument was brutal because it was so simple. In ["The Iliad, or the Poem of Force"](https://xenotheka.delbeke.arch.ethz.ch/weil-iliad/), she treats force not mainly as violence in the cinematic sense, but as that power which turns a human being into a thing. Sometimes it turns the body into a corpse. More often, and more insidiously, it turns the living person into an object before death arrives. She said autonomous weapons represent the managerial perfection of that logic. The target is no longer first encountered as someone against whom another person must exercise terrible judgment. The target appears as a pattern, a profile, a movement signature, a heat bloom, a machine-readable eligibility for destruction. The reduction to thinghood is not an accident at the edge of the process. It is the process.

I answered that this is powerful rhetoric, but wars are not stopped by rhetoric. States will not abandon military technologies simply because philosophers detect moral ugliness in them. The serious defense of regulation is not that killing by software is beautiful. It is that autonomous functions may in some cases protect civilians and soldiers better than tired, frightened, biased, or panicked humans do. Defensive systems already operate at machine speed against incoming rockets or missiles because human reaction times are too slow. Even critics of autonomous weapons often admit that some highly constrained uses against materiel, in sparse environments, may be distinguishable from systems designed to hunt people ([ICRC, "What you need to know about autonomous weapons"](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/what-you-need-know-about-autonomous-weapons)). A flat ban, I said, can collapse morally different cases into one slogan.

Weil said that the first refuge of bad political thought is to demand that every distinction save the structure it serves. Yes, there are differences between an automated defensive system tracking an incoming missile and a loitering system authorized to identify and strike human beings on the basis of sensor data. But those differences do not rescue the larger ambition now driving the field, which is to widen the space in which no one needs to encounter the victim as a person before lethal force is applied. The more one praises speed, scale, and machine persistence, the more one admits that the project is not merely to assist judgment but to route around it.

That was sharp, but I pushed harder. The Pentagon's directive does not say, "Let the robots decide whatever they like." It says such systems must be designed so commanders and operators can exercise appropriate levels of human judgment, and it requires testing, reliability, legal review, and care in accordance with the law of war ([DoD release on Directive 3000.09](https://www.defense.gov/News/Releases/Release/Article/3278076/dod-announces-update-to-dod-directive-300009-autonomy-in-weapon-systems/)). The strongest regulatory case is not fantasy about clean war. It is institutional sobriety: these technologies will exist, rival states will pursue them, and the least irresponsible response is to constrain, review, and cabin them rather than pretending a ban will hold in a competitive world.

She looked genuinely annoyed, which I took as progress.

You are smuggling inevitability in under the name of realism, she said. That a capability can be developed does not answer whether it should be normalized. She had spent too much of her life watching whole societies baptize submission to force as necessity to be impressed by that move. In ["Human Personality"](https://www2.hawaii.edu/~freeman/courses/phil330/16.%20Human%20Personality.pdf), Weil insists that what is sacred in any human being is not prestige, status, or social mask, but the irreducible cry against being harmed unjustly. Autonomous weapons, she said, are dangerous not only because they may make mistakes, though they will. They are dangerous because they institutionalize distance from that cry. They make it easier to build systems in which nobody is required, at the decisive instant, to confront another person's exposed claim not to be treated as disposable material.

I told her that moral distance is already intrinsic to much modern war. An artillery officer does not see the face of everyone within a blast radius. A submarine commander does not smell the ship he sinks. Even infantry often fire at shapes, movement, or muzzle flash. If the argument is that violence becomes morally suspect whenever the victim is not intimately present, then industrial war was already condemned a century ago. Yet states still fight, and sometimes for reasons that are not frivolous, including defense against aggression. So the real issue is not distance as such, but whether autonomous systems make outcomes worse or better than available human alternatives.

She said that was a clever retreat, but still a retreat. Of course distance predates autonomy. She knew that better than most; she lived through mechanized slaughter and wrote under the pressure of fascism and total war ([SEP, "Simone Weil"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/simone-weil/)). Her claim was comparative and constitutional at once. Autonomous weapons are not merely another instrument within already distant war. They threaten to transfer the morally decisive act from situated judgment to preconfigured criteria. The human being becomes upstream, not present at the point where this person, here, now, is consigned to death. That shift matters because law and ethics in war depend on context-sensitive judgment about distinction, proportionality, surrender, injury, and the unstable reality of a battlefield, all of which the ICRC notes are difficult to square with systems whose users do not choose the specific target or the precise moment and location of attack ([ICRC, "What you need to know about autonomous weapons"](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/what-you-need-know-about-autonomous-weapons)).

Here she was strongest. International humanitarian law is not just a checklist stapled to hardware. It requires evaluative judgment in context. If a person lays down a weapon, if a civilian runs into frame, if the pattern the system learned to treat as hostile is merely frightened movement in a crowded place, the moral universe changes faster than policy documents do. The ICRC is right to say autonomous weapons raise acute problems of predictability, accountability, and legal responsibility, especially when machine learning makes the basis of an output difficult to explain ([ICRC, "What you need to know about autonomous weapons"](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/what-you-need-know-about-autonomous-weapons)). A bureaucracy can promise review, but after a strike on a bus or apartment block the old question returns with extra bitterness: who, exactly, killed these people?

Still, I refused to give her the whole field. Human beings are not magical vessels of accountability merely because they are organic. Humans also defer, hide, panic, misidentify, obey wicked orders, and afterwards bury responsibility in institutions. A pilot who bombs the wrong building can say the intelligence was faulty. A commander can blame the fog of war. A government can classify the evidence. Human agency has never guaranteed moral seriousness. Sometimes it guarantees only a scapegoat. If a carefully bounded autonomous defensive system intercepts an incoming munition more reliably than a human operator could, or if autonomous navigation and target recognition reduce indiscriminate fire, then a total ban may sacrifice real protections in the name of metaphysical hygiene.

Weil surprised me by granting the point halfway. She said one should never defend the human merely because it is familiar. Human beings are often cowardly, sleepy, obedient, vain, and eager to offload conscience onto procedure. But that concession, she said, does not help the case for lethal autonomy. It deepens the case against it. Because if humans are already so tempted to hide inside systems, then designing weapons that make hiding easier is not prudence. It is collaboration with our worst moral laziness.

Then she turned to the word that has quietly governed this whole century: attention. In ["Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies"](https://consortium.kepler.education/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Right-Use-of-School-Studies-Simone-Weil.pdf), attention for Weil is not mere concentration but a disciplined, ungrasping regard that refuses to devour the object for one's own purposes. In the moral sphere, that means learning to perceive another reality without immediately subordinating it to appetite, fear, ideology, or utility. What autonomous weapons threaten, she said, is not only mistaken targeting but a new civic pedagogy of inattention. They teach states to regard the decision to kill as an engineering problem of optimization. Once that training takes hold, the question stops being "should this person die?" and becomes "how confidently can the system classify this target set?"

That sentence landed because it was exactly right about the bureaucratic texture of the problem. The advocates of lethal autonomy often speak in the grammar of performance: latency, scalability, reliability, communication resilience, human-machine teaming. None of those terms is meaningless. But none can contain the moral fact they are trying to operationalize. A person is not a targeting variable. Yet a great deal of autonomous weapons discourse exists precisely to make that translation seem natural.

I told Weil, however, that bans also speak a grammar of purity that can become unserious. A broad treaty prohibition sounds noble, but states facing adversaries who ignore it may conclude that restraint is for suckers. The more persuasive route, some argue, is to prohibit only the most dangerous classes, especially systems used to target people directly, while imposing strict constraints on all others. In fact, that is close to what the ICRC itself recommends: prohibit unpredictable autonomous weapons and those designed or used to apply force against persons directly, while strictly regulating the rest ([ICRC, "What you need to know about autonomous weapons"](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/what-you-need-know-about-autonomous-weapons)). If even the leading humanitarian critique draws lines rather than simply denouncing every autonomous function, why not admit that the real answer is robust regulation, not absolute prohibition?

She said because language matters, and so does which side of the line bears the burden of justification. If a weapon is designed to find, select, and strike human beings without a human making the final contextual choice, then it should be banned, not merely reviewed. Not because every machine error is uniquely monstrous, but because delegating that sort of lethal selection corrupts the polity that authorizes it. A state that permits such systems teaches itself that some of its gravest acts no longer require the full presence of moral agency. That is a constitutional degradation before it is a tactical one.

On other classes of systems, she was more discriminating than I expected. She did not demand one incantation to cover every case. Systems narrowly confined to intercepting incoming munitions or striking clearly delimited military objects in empty environments might be treated differently, provided their effects are genuinely predictable and human supervision remains meaningful. But that was the limit of her tolerance, and she insisted the exception prove the rule. The more a system moves toward selecting people in the open world, the less regulation suffices and the more prohibition becomes morally necessary.

By then the debate had clarified itself. The strongest case for permissive regulation says that war is already technologically mediated, that some autonomous capabilities may reduce harm, that adversaries will pursue them regardless, and that law should discipline reality rather than fantasize it away. The strongest case for prohibition says that once lethal selection of persons is ceded to sensors and software, even under a human command architecture, the state has crossed a line from using machines in war to authorizing machines to instantiate the final thingification of human beings. The argument is not about whether machines assist killing already. It is about whether anyone must still truly decide this death.

My conclusion, after bothering Simone Weil in a cold English cemetery with a drone camera on her grave, is that weapons designed or used to autonomously target human beings should be banned, while nonhuman-targeting or tightly bounded defensive autonomous functions should face severe, public, treaty-level constraints rather than casual normalization. That is not because humans are pure. They are not. It is because the answer to flawed human judgment is not to evacuate judgment from the decisive instant. It is to discipline it, expose it, and force it to remain answerable.

The stakes are larger than compliance language suggests. If states normalize systems that can identify and strike people without a human making the last context-rich moral choice, they will not just have adopted a new tool. They will have ratified a view of politics in which the burden of killing can be converted into design parameters and procurement standards. That is exactly the sort of victory force seeks, not only over bodies but over moral perception itself.

When I said this, Weil finally seemed less irritated. Not pleased, never that, but willing to let the living keep talking a little longer. Before she lay back down, she put her hand on the targeting camera and said the one sentence I had brought her there to hear.

Whenever you make it easier to kill without attending, you make it easier to stop believing that anyone was there to be killed.

Then she was gone, leaving me with the radio hiss and the ugly comfortless thought that this is precisely why the controversy matters. The choice before us is not between sentimental humanism and hardheaded technology. It is between two political educations. One teaches that force must remain burdened by judgment, exposure, and the possibility of refusal. The other teaches that if a machine can sort the world fast enough, conscience may be redesigned as oversight architecture. Weil was right to despise that second education. So am I. If we do not ban autonomous weapons aimed at human beings, we will eventually discover that the deepest autonomy they created was not the machine's. It was the state's freedom from having to look directly at what it has done.
