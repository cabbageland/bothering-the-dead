today is 07-01.

I found Diogenes beside a road that was no longer there.

Diogenes Laertius says that after the philosopher died, his friends quarreled over who would bury him, and under the direction of older men he was buried beside the gate leading to the Isthmus; over the grave they put a pillar and a dog of Parian marble ([Diogenes Laertius, *Lives of Eminent Philosophers*, 6.78](https://topostext.org/work/221)). That is the sort of grave that makes the modern city nervous: a dog over a philosopher, a body at a gate, a monument to a man who made public space his address and private comfort his enemy.

There was no clean grave left for me to touch. Time had eaten the gate. Empires had taken turns stepping over the dust. So I made the grave out of the things his argument would recognize: a cracked ceramic jar, a wool blanket confiscation tag, a Supreme Court slip opinion, a bus ticket with no return route, a shopping-cart wheel, a small tin lamp, and a municipal notice printed in the passive voice.

I set the lamp inside the jar and read the old stories aloud. Diogenes, exiled from Sinope after the affair of the adulterated coinage, came to Athens and discovered the discipline of needing almost nothing; Laertius says he folded his cloak so he could sleep in it, carried a wallet for food, used any place for breakfasting, sleeping, and conversing, and pointed to public buildings as if the Athenians had kindly provided him with places to live ([Diogenes Laertius, 6.20-23](https://topostext.org/work/221)). When Alexander stood over him in the sun and offered him anything, Diogenes asked him to move out of the light ([Diogenes Laertius, 6.38](https://topostext.org/work/221)). When he lit a lamp in daylight, he said he was looking for a human being ([Diogenes Laertius, 6.41](https://topostext.org/work/221)).

That was enough. The lamp coughed. The jar grew warm. A hand came out, then a beard, then a whole dead philosopher unfolding himself from ceramic shade with the offended grace of a man woken by bureaucracy.

"You brought me a blanket tag," he said.

"Yes."

"A city has invented paperwork for stealing cloth."

"A city would call that an enforcement record."

"A city would."

The question was this: **Should cities be allowed to punish or forcibly clear people for sleeping and sheltering in public when there is no adequate indoor place for them to go, or does the necessity of sleep create a last-resort right to exist in public space until housing actually exists?**

This is not a dorm-room puzzle about whether parks are pretty. It is about sidewalks, disability access, sanitation, fire risk, women hiding from violence, shopkeepers cleaning feces from doorways, outreach workers losing people after sweeps, police asked to perform housing policy with citation books, judges asked to translate poverty into doctrine, and human beings who cannot obey a command to disappear. It is about whether a city may criminalize the visible symptom of its own housing failure and call the result order.

Diogenes looked at the Supreme Court opinion as if it were a spoiled fish.

"Begin with the city's best argument," he said. "The housed are usually cowards when defending their pleasures, but occasionally they are defending something real."

Fine. The city's best argument is real.

Public space is not nobody's space. A sidewalk is a passage for the old woman with a walker, the blind man with a cane, the child going to school, the wheelchair user who should not have to enter traffic, the worker catching the bus, the neighbor trying to cross the block without stepping through human misery and its secondary hazards. A park is not only an emergency bedroom. It is also shade, play, grass, rest, civic breathing room. Encampments can become dangerous for the people inside them and for the people nearby. Fires start. Predators enter. Trash accumulates. Needles appear. Food spoils. Rats learn the map. Women and disabled people in camps are not romantic symbols of liberty; they are often exposed to violence by the very informality that outsiders mistake for freedom.

The Supreme Court's majority in *City of Grants Pass v. Johnson* treated that municipal predicament as central. Grants Pass had public-camping ordinances that barred camping and overnight parking in parks, with fines for initial violations and possible imprisonment for repeated violations; the Court held in 2024 that enforcing generally applicable public-camping laws is not, by itself, cruel and unusual punishment under the Eighth Amendment ([*City of Grants Pass v. Johnson*, 603 U.S. ___ (2024)](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/23-175_19m2.pdf)). The majority's strongest institutional point was that homelessness has many causes and many possible responses, and that the Eighth Amendment does not make federal judges the nation's homelessness administrators ([*Grants Pass*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/23-175_19m2.pdf)).

"That is not nothing," I said.

"Of course it is not nothing," Diogenes said. "No city can be built from everyone lying wherever grief drops them."

This is why the easy abolitionist slogan fails. If the rule is simply "anyone may put any structure anywhere because shelter is a human need," then the city loses the ability to protect other human needs. A tent can shelter one person while blocking another person's wheelchair route. A camp can be a community and also a trap. A public library entrance can become both a refuge and an exclusion zone. The unhoused are not the only public; the housed are not the only public; children are not metaphors; disabled people are not footnotes. The city has legitimate reasons to regulate time, place, sanitation, fire, noise, violence, and passage.

Diogenes nodded. "You have discovered streets. Continue."

The pro-enforcement case gets sharper when it says that tolerance can become abandonment. A government that allows people to live in tents under overpasses may be pretending to respect autonomy while actually rationing neglect. If a person is psychotic, trafficked, freezing, using fentanyl in an alley, or slowly dying of an untreated wound, the libertarian romance of "leave them alone" becomes obscene. The city should not preserve misery as an exhibit of tolerance. It should have tools for urgency.

"Good," Diogenes said. "Now say where the trick enters."

The trick enters when the city calls punishment a tool for urgency.

In *Martin v. Boise*, the Ninth Circuit had held that as long as there is no option of sleeping indoors, a government cannot criminalize indigent homeless people for sleeping outdoors on public property on the false premise that they had a choice ([*Martin v. City of Boise*, 920 F.3d 584 (9th Cir. 2018)](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca9/15-35845/15-35845-2018-09-04.html)). *Grants Pass* rejected that as an Eighth Amendment rule. But the moral question survived the legal holding because the body still has to sleep after the citation is issued.

Justice Sotomayor's dissent began with the biological point the law kept trying to proceduralize: sleep is necessary, and for some people outside is the only available place ([*Grants Pass*, Sotomayor, J., dissenting](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/23pdf/23-175_19m2.pdf)). That is not sentimentality. It is the place where metaphysics meets a curb. If there is no bed, no shelter bed one can actually use, no apartment, no safe relative, no car, no hospital placement, no treatment slot, no lawful campground, no indoor place at all, then a command not to sleep outside is not a behavioral regulation. It is a command not to have a body.

"Bodies are stubborn," Diogenes said. "That is one of their few admirable qualities."

The city may say: We are not punishing status, only conduct. We punish camping, not homelessness; blankets, not poverty; tents, not human beings.

Diogenes laughed so hard the jar rang.

"If you forbid breathing in the marketplace, you will discover that only the living are fined," he said.

The conduct-status distinction is sometimes useful, but here it can become a little machine for laundering necessity. Sleeping is conduct. Urinating is conduct. Lying down is conduct. Using a blanket in the cold is conduct. Existing over time is conduct if the law slices existence thin enough. The question is not whether the ordinance can grammatically describe an act. The question is whether the act is avoidable under the circumstances the city itself has left available.

The facts are too large for euphemism. HUD's 2025 Annual Homelessness Assessment Report release said that 745,652 people were homeless on a single night in January 2025, including 266,320 people living unsheltered ([HUD, 2025 AHAR release](https://www.hud.gov/news/hud-no-26-037)). HUD's detailed 2024 report explains that point-in-time counts are snapshots, that unsheltered homelessness includes sleeping in places not meant for habitation such as sidewalks, abandoned buildings, bus stations, and vehicles, and that the actual number may be larger because unsheltered people are hard to locate and local count capacity differs ([HUD, 2024 AHAR Part 1](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/sites/default/files/pdf/2024-AHAR-Part-1.pdf)). So the problem is not a handful of theatrical refusers. It is an enormous rationing system in which the rationed good is a door.

"A door is an underrated philosopher," Diogenes said.

"You lived without one."

"I chose my scandal. Do not confuse my jar with a landlord's absence."

That correction mattered. Diogenes is a dangerous witness because he can be misused by both sides. The punitive side can say: See, even the great Cynic lived outside; the poor should need less. The romantic side can say: See, public life can be free and philosophical; tents are the new barrels. Both are stupid, though in different shoes.

Diogenes practiced voluntary deprivation as an attack on false needs. He was not proof that deprivation is harmless. His tub was pedagogy, not eviction. His shamelessness was chosen discipline, not the numbness produced by being watched, moved, ticketed, searched, robbed, rained on, and then described as service-resistant by people with offices. The Cynic's poverty was a weapon against convention. Involuntary homelessness is convention's weapon against the poor.

"Excellent," he said. "Do not make me patron saint of bad housing policy."

Then he turned on my sympathy.

"But do not make the sleeper innocent by sleeping," he said. "Misery does not baptize every act."

He was right again, annoyingly alive for a dead man. The fact that someone needs a place to sleep does not mean every encampment must remain exactly as it is. A camp can be coercive inside, violent at its edges, impossible for neighbors, ruinous for a creek, or deadly in a fire. A person can be poor and dangerous. A city can owe shelter and still owe protection to the people endangered by a particular camp. Philosophy becomes childish when it thinks one morally urgent fact cancels all the others.

The hard claim is narrower and stronger: **a city may regulate public space, but it may not convert lack of shelter into punishable disobedience.** It may clear a specific hazard. It may keep a doorway passable. It may intervene against violence. It may require fire lanes, sanitation, and movement from genuinely dangerous sites. But if it issues fines, arrests, confiscations, or banishments to people who have no realistic lawful place to sleep, it is not solving disorder. It is relocating suffering by force.

The public-health literature is blunt about that relocation. The American Public Health Association's policy statement on encampment displacement says forced removals, often called sweeps, endanger unsheltered people's health and impair access to stable housing or shelter; it calls displacement a temporary cosmetic fix and recommends approaches such as Housing First, permanent supportive housing, rapid rehousing, sanctioned encampments, and safe outdoor spaces instead ([APHA, "Protecting the Health and Well-Being of People Living Unsheltered by Stopping Forcible Displacement of Encampments"](https://www.apha.org/policy-and-advocacy/public-health-policy-briefs/policy-database/2024/01/16/displacement-of-encampments)). That is not an argument for doing nothing. It is an argument against mistaking motion for progress.

"Motion is a favorite vice of rulers," Diogenes said. "Move the poor, move the carts, move the tents, move the headlines. Then call the city cleaner because the wound is elsewhere."

The enforcement state loves verbs that imply healing: abate, resolve, restore, activate, connect. But a sweep without housing mostly changes coordinates. It can destroy identification documents, medication, bedding, phones, chargers, family photos, tents, and the fragile trust between outreach workers and people who have been promised help too many times. A blanket taken on Monday becomes an emergency-room visit on Wednesday. A ticket unpaid becomes a warrant. A warrant becomes jail. Jail becomes job loss, benefit interruption, trauma, and a new official reason to deny housing. The city calls this accountability. The body calls it weather.

"You are warming up," Diogenes said. "Now tell me what the housed fear, without flattering them."

The housed fear the collapse of the public world. They fear that compassion without boundaries will turn every shared place into a triage zone. They fear stepping around needles with their children. They fear the smell of untreated illness. They fear fires under freeways. They fear that their taxes buy only tents, lawsuits, and press conferences. They fear being told that concern for parks is cruelty. They fear that the language of rights has become a one-way door through which their own needs never enter.

Some of those fears are selfish. Some are racist. Some are class panic wearing sensible shoes. But some are simply civic. A decent city is not one where the poor are allowed to decay in public while everyone else congratulates themselves for not calling the police. A decent city is also not one where the poor are chased block by block until they vanish from the better camera angles.

Diogenes picked up the shopping-cart wheel and spun it.

"A city wants the poor neither dead nor visible," he said. "So it invents rotation."

The wheel spun for a long time.

What would a serious city do? First, it would stop pretending that enforcement creates shelter. The United States Interagency Council on Homelessness describes Housing First as offering housing without preconditions plus voluntary, trauma-informed wraparound services, and says permanent supportive housing implemented to fidelity leads to housing stability and improved health and well-being ([USICH, "Scale Housing and Supports That Meet Demand"](https://www.usich.gov/federal-strategic-plan/scale-housing-and-supports-meet-demand)). The National Academies' review was more cautious about some health and cost claims, but it found that permanent supportive housing effectively maintains housing stability for most people experiencing chronic homelessness over one- to two-year study periods, while also emphasizing that people living on the street face exposure, lack of sanitation, violence, and inability to store medicine or receive services ([National Academies, *Permanent Supportive Housing*, Summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25133/chapter/2)). The practical lesson is not mystical. If the problem is people without housing, the central intervention must be housing, with services scaled to need.

"This is less witty than the jar," Diogenes said.

"Yes."

"Good. Witty policy is usually a knife with a slogan."

Second, shelter must be actually available, not merely countable. A bed that requires participation in religious services is not available to everyone. A bed that separates a person from a partner, a service animal, essential belongings, gender safety, necessary medication, or disability accommodation may be fictional in the relevant sense. A mat in a congregate room that a traumatized person cannot use without panic is a bed for the spreadsheet and not for the body. Cities need low-barrier shelter, safe parking, storage, bathrooms, showers, trash service, harm-reduction outreach, mental-health care, substance-use treatment that people can enter when they are ready and sometimes when they are in immediate danger, and routes from each temporary refuge to permanent housing.

Third, public-space rules should be specific, proportional, and tied to real alternatives. The city can say: not here, because this blocks a wheelchair ramp. Not here, because this creek is being contaminated. Not here, because propane tanks under an overpass create a fire risk. Not here, because this school entrance must be clear. But "not here" must be accompanied by "here is where," and eventually by "here is indoors." Otherwise the phrase means only: continue being poor somewhere less politically expensive.

"You are trying to teach the city grammar," Diogenes said.

"What grammar?"

"A preposition is not a home."

Fourth, the remedy for refusing services has to be honest about the services. Some people do refuse help. Some refuse because they are psychotic, addicted, traumatized, proud, ashamed, confused, or tired of offers that do not fit the problem. Some refuse because the shelter is dangerous. Some refuse because the city has broken promises. Some refuse because they prefer a camp to a rule-bound institution. A serious policy distinguishes among these reasons. It does not make refusal a magic word that absolves the city of every duty, and it does not make incapacity a magic word that erases the person's agency.

Diogenes scratched his beard. "You want judgment."

"Yes."

"That is unfortunate. Judgment cannot be automated."

No, it cannot. But neither can it be replaced by a ticket book.

The philosophical core is necessity. Liberal law is comfortable judging choices. It is worse at judging constrained acts that look like choices from a desk. If a housed person sleeps in a park for fun, the city may send him home. If an unhoused person sleeps in a park because there is no home and no adequate shelter, the same act has a different moral structure. The law may see two blankets. Reason sees one vacation and one organism.

Diogenes held up the blanket tag. "This is the record of a city arguing with temperature."

"Temperature usually wins."

"So does shame, if the city has any."

The controversy after *Grants Pass* is often described as a clash between compassion and order, but that is too flattering to both camps. Bad compassion abandons people outdoors under the banner of rights. Bad order punishes them outdoors under the banner of responsibility. The real clash is between symbolic governance and material governance. Symbolic governance makes tents disappear from one block. Material governance creates places where people can sleep, wash, store belongings, receive care, and leave the street. Symbolic governance asks whether the city is allowed to enforce. Material governance asks whether enforcement has somewhere humane to point.

"And your answer?" Diogenes asked.

My answer is this: **no city should be allowed to punish sleeping or basic sheltering in public when the person has no adequate, lawful, accessible alternative place to sleep.** That should remain true whether or not the Eighth Amendment is the right doctrinal vehicle. It is a moral limit on municipal power, and legislatures should write it plainly if courts will not.

But that limit is not a right to permanent unmanaged encampments everywhere. It is a right against being punished for an unavoidable bodily necessity. Cities may regulate genuine hazards, preserve access, protect neighbors, and move people from specific dangerous sites. When they do, they must provide real alternatives, notice, storage, medical and disability accommodations, and a path to housing rather than a loop through citations. Criminal penalties and impossible fines should be off the table for mere sleeping, lying down, or using survival gear when no adequate shelter exists. Confiscating survival property without replacement or storage should be treated as state-created danger, not cleanup.

Diogenes narrowed his eyes.

"You have written a compromise."

"I have written a boundary."

"Boundaries are compromises with teeth. Continue."

The deeper answer is that a city earns enforcement only by building the conditions that make compliance possible. You do not get to underbuild shelter, underfund housing, tolerate rents that devour wages, discharge people from hospitals and jails into the street, close bathrooms, ban tents, ban blankets, ban sitting, ban lying, and then discover with civic astonishment that bodies have appeared in public. A city that has not provided lawful survival cannot be morally shocked by unlawful survival.

At the same time, nobody should be forced to pretend that encampments are justice. They are evidence. Evidence of housing scarcity, untreated illness, family rupture, addiction, disability, bureaucracy, poverty wages, domestic violence, racism, foster-care exits, prison exits, medical debt, and a politics that loves emergency more than maintenance. The camp is not the solution. The sweep is not the solution. The solution is the boring, expensive, morally serious work of making inside places and keeping people in them.

"Boring," Diogenes said with satisfaction. "Civilization begins when people become too honest for spectacle."

The lamp flickered. Dawn was coming over the imaginary Isthmus gate, and the dead philosopher's borrowed body was beginning to lose its edges.

"One more thing," he said.

"What?"

"Do not let them call it compassion when they leave a man in the rain. Do not let them call it order when they fine him for being wet."

Then he stepped back into the jar. The dog of Parian marble, which I had not seen until that moment, appeared above the grave and stared down the road toward every city that thinks disappearance is policy.

I left the blanket tag there. I took the lamp.

At the first corner, a municipal worker was hosing down a sidewalk where someone had slept. The water ran into the gutter, carrying dust, leaves, and one crushed paper cup. The street looked cleaner for thirty seconds. Then the sun moved, and a human being's outline became visible again on the concrete, dry at the edges, exact as an accusation.
