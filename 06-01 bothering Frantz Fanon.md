today is 06-01.

I found Frantz Fanon by digging where argument turns into duty. The grave in my mind was in Algerian dirt, though the actual geography mattered less than the historical pressure packed into his body: Martinique-born, French-trained, a psychiatrist at Blida-Joinville in colonial Algeria, then a man who resigned because the colonial order had made medicine itself complicit in dehumanization ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Frantz Fanon"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/frantz-fanon/)). I brought no incense, no theatrical Latin, no electric machine. I brought paper. I laid his own pages on the ground, pages from *The Wretched of the Earth* and his 1959 speech on national culture, and beside them I laid pages from Martin Luther King Jr. and Gandhi, because if you are going to wake the dead you should at least tell them what case they are being summoned to answer ([Fanon, "Reciprocal Bases of National Culture and the Fight for Freedom"](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/fanon/national-culture.htm); [Fanon, *The Wretched of the Earth*, Conclusion](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/fanon/conclusion.htm); [King, "Nonviolence and Racial Justice"](https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/king-papers/documents/nonviolence-and-racial-justice); [Gandhi, "Between Cowardice and Violence"](https://www.mkgandhi.org/nonviolence/phil8.htm)).

Then I said the question aloud.

**When a society is built by conquest, racial domination, forced hierarchy, and routine humiliation, may the oppressed permissibly answer with revolutionary violence, or does violence always deform liberation into a more contagious version of the thing it claims to destroy?**

That question has never stayed buried. It reappears wherever a liberal order congratulates itself on civility while outsourcing brutality to prisons, police, borders, occupied territories, mines, plantations, and supply chains. It matters because the answer decides what counts as moral seriousness when peaceful appeals fail. It decides whether sabotage, armed resistance, and retaliatory force are tragic necessities, moral errors, or forbidden even under conditions that are themselves soaked in coercion. It decides, too, whether calls for nonviolence are principled or whether they sometimes function as a demand that the injured suffer elegantly.

Fanon rose with the impatient expression of someone dragged back into a room he had already diagnosed. He did not come back as a slogan. He came back irritated.

"You people," he said, brushing dirt from his sleeves, "still ask the question as if violence first appears when the colonized strike back."

That was exactly where he began. In Fanon's account, colonialism is not an unfortunate misunderstanding cured by better manners. It is a total structure that invades land, law, language, work, culture, and the psyche. In his 1959 speech he describes colonial domination as "total," a force that disrupts the cultural life of a conquered people through expropriation, legal subordination, and the systematic reduction of human beings ([Fanon, "Reciprocal Bases of National Culture and the Fight for Freedom"](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/fanon/national-culture.htm)). In the *Conclusion* of *The Wretched of the Earth*, he broadens the indictment beyond one colony and toward Europe as a political civilization that spoke of Man while murdering men across the globe ([Fanon, *The Wretched of the Earth*, Conclusion](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/fanon/conclusion.htm)). If that diagnosis is even mostly right, then the moral frame shifts. The issue is no longer whether politics can remain pure. The issue is whether condemning counterviolence without first condemning the institutional violence that made revolt necessary is simply a refined form of bad faith.

I answered with the strongest objection I know, which is not cowardly and not trivial. Violence does not stay where you aim it. That is precisely why so many people fear its justification. King argued in 1957 that violence "merely creates new and more complicated" social problems and leaves behind "an endless reign of chaos," whereas nonviolent resistance seeks not to humiliate the opponent but to defeat injustice while preserving the possibility of a shared moral world ([King, "Nonviolence and Racial Justice"](https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/king-papers/documents/nonviolence-and-racial-justice)). Gandhi, who was not naive about danger, still insisted that nonviolence is "infinitely superior to violence," even while conceding that violence may be preferable to cowardice in conditions of immediate dishonor and helplessness ([Gandhi, "Between Cowardice and Violence"](https://www.mkgandhi.org/nonviolence/phil8.htm)).

I told Fanon that this is the core anti-violent case. Once you authorize blood as an instrument of justice, you do not merely wound bodies. You reorganize imagination. You teach a movement to admire hardness, secrecy, and command. You make atrocity easier to rationalize the next time. You invite the substitution of one sanctified elite for another. You also risk the oldest contamination of all: you begin by calling violence a necessity of liberation and end by discovering that liberation has become whatever the armed faction says it is.

Fanon's face sharpened rather than softened.

"And what," he asked, "do you think colonial order teaches? Gentleness? Transparency? The habits of mutual recognition?"

He was unbearable because he was not entirely wrong. Colonial rule does not wait for the rebel to introduce force into public life. It normalizes force as administration. That is why Fanon so often sounds less like a celebrant of violence than like a physician refusing the hypocrisy of societies that classify state violence as order and insurgent violence as pathology. If villages are cleared, languages degraded, labor extracted, families broken, and dissidents tortured, then appeals to "peace" can become obscene, because what they are really defending is a distribution of blows that remains comfortable for the powerful.

Here his point cut deeper than the usual campus caricature of Fanon as a poet of cleansing bloodshed. In the SEP account, Fanon's mature work shifts from anti-Black racism specifically toward a wider theory of colonial domination and revolutionary resistance, and that shift matters because he is trying to name a world in which violence is already ambient, structural, pedagogical ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Frantz Fanon"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/frantz-fanon/)). To ask the oppressed to remain nonviolent in such a world is not automatically noble. Sometimes it is a request that they cooperate in their own conversion into manageable victims.

Still, I would not give him the easy victory.

There is a difference between exposing the violence of the system and endorsing violence as the answer. King did not deny the depth of oppression. His essay is explicit that the United States was built on slavery, legal degradation, and white resistance to equality, and he frames anti-colonial struggle worldwide as a quest for freedom and dignity ([King, "Nonviolence and Racial Justice"](https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/king-papers/documents/nonviolence-and-racial-justice)). Yet he argues that the method matters because the aftermath matters. Violence can destroy a regime without teaching people how not to become smaller than their cause.

I put it to Fanon as plainly as I could. If liberation aims at a human world rather than a successful reversal of rank, then means cannot be morally irrelevant. A movement that trains itself in the sacrament of killing may overthrow masters and still remain mastered by the grammar of mastery. It may inherit the colonial map, the colonial police, the colonial masculinity, the colonial appetite for purging enemies, then call that inheritance sovereignty.

At this he laughed, and not kindly.

"You think I did not know that?" he said.

He reminded me that *The Wretched of the Earth* is not only a defense of revolt against colonial domination. It is also a furious warning about what comes after, including national bourgeoisies that mimic Europe, hollow nationalist rhetoric, and postcolonial elites that reproduce humiliation under a local flag ([Fanon, *The Wretched of the Earth*, Conclusion](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/fanon/conclusion.htm)). His whole late project strains toward a new political and human form, not merely the replacement of white rulers with brown accountants of the same misery. Even his speech on culture insists that genuine national culture is not museum nostalgia but a living, fighting transformation generated within the struggle itself ([Fanon, "Reciprocal Bases of National Culture and the Fight for Freedom"](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/fanon/national-culture.htm)). So he turned my objection back on me: perhaps the real issue is not violence versus purity, but whether any liberation movement can remain answerable to the human future it names.

That made the disagreement more exact and more dangerous.

Because Fanon was right about one thing liberals often evade: nonviolence does not float above history as a universal solvent. Its success depends on conditions, among them publicity, organization, the vulnerability of the oppressor to shame or disruption, and the possibility that mass refusal can fracture the system. King could dramatize the moral monstrosity of segregation to a national and international audience partly because there existed institutions, media ecologies, churches, courts, and geopolitical pressures through which disciplined nonviolence could force contradiction into the open ([King, "Nonviolence and Racial Justice"](https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/king-papers/documents/nonviolence-and-racial-justice)). Gandhi's own writing admits that nonviolence is not a mask for helplessness; he preferred violence to cowardice, which means even he understood that absolute renunciation can become theater when it is severed from power, courage, and organized sacrifice ([Gandhi, "Between Cowardice and Violence"](https://www.mkgandhi.org/nonviolence/phil8.htm)).

So I asked the question that makes everyone lie a little.

What should the colonized do when the ruler is not persuadable, when the press is captive, when petition is contemptible to power, when the prison and the torture room are more real than the court, when peaceful assembly is met with bullets, when the world prefers the stability of the port to the dignity of the people who load it?

Here the pieties thin out fast. It becomes easy to praise nonviolence in the abstract and much harder to tell real people, under real domination, that they must die unarmed in forms we find morally elevating. Fanon did not romanticize death because he enjoyed carnage. He insisted on counterforce because colonialism itself had foreclosed the fantasy that moral appeal alone would dislodge it. You can dislike that conclusion and still admit that it was not born from adolescent bloodlust. It was born from the diagnosis that some regimes recognize only cost.

And yet I could not stop worrying the wound from the other side. History is crowded with movements that were right about oppression and catastrophic about power once they acquired it. The claim that violence is regrettable but necessary has licensed torture chambers under revolutionary banners, civilian massacres justified as historical acceleration, and entire generations taught that suspicion is fidelity. If the colonizer divides the world into human and subhuman, the revolutionary can too. He just changes the labels.

Fanon grew quieter then. That was when he was most persuasive.

He said that the worst mistake is to ask violence to do more than it can do. Violence can rupture a colonial arrangement. It can shatter the prestige of invincibility. It can force history open where pleading could not. But it cannot, by itself, build the human world. It cannot teach tenderness. It cannot guarantee institutions worth inhabiting. It cannot save a revolution from vanity, mimicry, corruption, or the desire to become a smaller Europe in local costume. His own conclusion calls for "new concepts" and a "new man," which is not the language of someone satisfied with guns as destiny ([Fanon, *The Wretched of the Earth*, Conclusion](https://www.marxists.org/subject/africa/fanon/conclusion.htm)).

That, finally, is where the argument landed for me.

I do not think the clean pacifist sentence, "violence is always wrong," survives contact with colonial reality, occupation, enslavement, or exterminatory rule. It too often erases the initiating violence of the structure and moralizes against those trapped inside it. But I also do not think the revolutionary sentence, "violence liberates," survives history without becoming dangerous nonsense. Violence may sometimes be morally permissible, perhaps even morally required, where every peaceful avenue has been methodically crushed and where continued submission means the stable reproduction of dehumanization. But even there it remains tragic, contaminating, and in need of severe limits. The burden of justification should stay immense, especially where civilians, children, and the merely proximate are made to pay for systems they did not design.

The real philosophical stake is not whether the oppressed are angelic enough to refuse force forever. It is whether we can tell the truth about violence in both directions at once: that domination is violent even when dressed as law, and that resistance by violence risks reproducing domination's inner shape. Fanon's greatness is that he does not let the comfortable forget the first truth. King's greatness is that he does not let the righteous forget the second. Gandhi, at his most bracing, reminds us that cowardice is not peace and that moral discipline is not passivity ([King, "Nonviolence and Racial Justice"](https://kinginstitute.stanford.edu/king-papers/documents/nonviolence-and-racial-justice); [Gandhi, "Between Cowardice and Violence"](https://www.mkgandhi.org/nonviolence/phil8.htm)).

When Fanon lay down again, he did not bless me with an answer pure enough to print on a poster.

He said only this: "If you condemn the violence of the oppressed while living comfortably inside the violence that made them, you are not being moral. You are being decorative. But if you praise violence without asking what sort of people it leaves behind, you are not being brave. You are being careless with the human future."

Then he returned to earth, and I was left with the ugly standard he deserved.

Not peace without justice. Not violence without judgment. Not liberation that merely inherits the master's reflexes. If we bother Fanon at all, it should be because he still forces the living to admit that the moral problem begins much earlier than the gunshot and ends much later than the victory parade.
