today is 09-12.

I found Hideki Yukawa in a dead particle.

Not in a cemetery first. Cemeteries are too literal when the person you want has left his signature in a range, a mass, a force that dies quickly instead of traveling forever. I found him in a photographic emulsion plate, or rather in the idea of one: silver grains rearranged into the brief black handwriting of something that had passed through matter and stopped. In 1947, Lattes, Occhialini, and Powell reported tracks of slow mesons in photographic emulsions exposed at high altitude, evidence for mesons of different mass and for secondary mesons produced when charged mesons came to rest ([Lattes, Occhialini, and Powell, 1947](https://www.nature.com/articles/160453a0)). That was close enough to a body for my purposes. A track is what a particle leaves when it has already spent its life.

Yukawa had predicted a meson because the nucleus had a problem. Protons should repel one another electrically, yet nuclei held together. The force had to be strong and short-ranged. Yukawa's genius was to imagine that the nuclear force could be carried by a particle whose mass set that short range; the Nobel Foundation summarizes his 1949 prize as awarded "for his prediction of the existence of mesons on the basis of theoretical work on nuclear forces" and notes that he expected a particle with mass about 200 times the electron's mass ([Nobel Prize, "Hideki Yukawa - Facts"](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1949/yukawa/facts/)). In his Nobel lecture, "Meson Theory in Its Developments," he described the theory as a way to understand nuclear forces through exchange particles, not through mystical glue or verbal hand-waving ([Yukawa, Nobel Lecture, 1949](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/yukawa-lecture.pdf)).

So I built the resurrection table from things that do not last: a cloud-chamber photograph, a cracked Geiger counter, a notebook open to a potential proportional to exp(-mu r)/r, a small magnet, one polite cup of tea, one rude grant budget, and a printout of CERN's Future Circular Collider page. The page says the FCC study is developing a post-LHC research infrastructure, first as an electron-positron collider for precision work and later as a proton-proton collider aiming at collision energies of 100 TeV in the search for new physics ([CERN FCC](https://fcc.web.cern.ch/)). That was the bait. The dead do not always come for flowers. Some come for an argument with a machine that has not yet been built.

I placed the emulsion under a lamp and read aloud from Yukawa's Nobel page: prediction before direct experimental verification, unseen carrier, range tied to mass. Then I read aloud from the FCC page: Higgs self-interactions, dark matter, matter-antimatter asymmetry, neutrino masses, unprecedented precision and energy reach ([CERN FCC](https://fcc.web.cern.ch/)).

The old particle track darkened. The grains twitched. A man sat up inside the emulsion as if the photograph had remembered him incorrectly and was correcting itself.

Hideki Yukawa looked at the potential on the notebook, then at the budget.

"You have resurrected me with an accounting problem," he said.

"With a physics problem wearing an accounting problem."

"Then ask it properly."

The controversial topic was this: **should society build another generation of enormous particle colliders, such as CERN's proposed Future Circular Collider, when there is no guaranteed discovery beyond the Standard Model, or has high-energy physics earned the right to keep enlarging humanity's senses even when the next answer may be only precision, constraint, and silence?**

This is not a cute argument about whether physicists deserve toys. The stakes are public money, land, energy, decades of technical labor, international prestige, the training of young scientists, the opportunity cost to smaller experiments and other sciences, and the spiritual legitimacy of fundamental research in a century already stuffed with climate danger, medical need, inequality, and computational empires that also know how to say "innovation" while asking for the grid. The pro-collider side says that reality does not fit inside our current instruments and that the Higgs discovery proved the value of patient, expensive, international physics; the Nobel Prize in Physics 2013 honored Englert and Higgs for the theoretical discovery of the mechanism confirmed through ATLAS and CMS at the Large Hadron Collider ([Nobel Prize, 2013 Physics Summary](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2013/summary/)). The anti-collider side says that "search for new physics" can become a luxury phrase for "we do not know what we are buying, but we would like it to be very large."

Yukawa touched the cracked Geiger counter.

"Start with the accusation," he said.

Very well. The accusation is that modern particle physics risks confusing magnificence with necessity. The Standard Model is not emotionally satisfying, but it is offensively successful. The Higgs arrived; the larger revolution many people wanted did not automatically arrive with it; CERN's own FCC materials frame the next program as a search through open questions such as dark matter, matter-antimatter asymmetry, and neutrino masses rather than as a simple errand to collect an already named particle ([CERN FCC](https://fcc.web.cern.ch/)). Now the field asks for another machine, longer than a city commute, colder than almost anywhere in nature, more precise than ordinary speech can honor, and likely justified partly by discoveries no one can name in advance. That is either civilization at its noblest or a very sophisticated form of institutional appetite.

"Both can be true," Yukawa said.

"That is not a defense."

"It is a warning against simple prosecution."

He was right, irritatingly. Scientific realism already asks us to take unseen entities seriously when our best theories earn that trust; the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes scientific realism as a positive epistemic attitude toward the observable and unobservable content of successful science ([SEP, "Scientific Realism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-realism/)). Physics is not gardening the visible. It is the disciplined enlargement of what counts as contact with the world. Electrons, neutrinos, quarks, fields, curved spacetime, exchange particles: the invisible has repeatedly turned out not to be an embarrassment but the furniture of reality.

Yukawa leaned forward.

"I was not guessing a fairy," he said. "The force had a range. The range implied a mass. The mass implied a particle."

"That is exactly the problem," I said. "Your prediction had teeth. Does the next collider?"

He smiled as if I had finally stopped wasting his resurrection.

Because that is the sharp distinction. Yukawa's meson was not a vague wish that something interesting might happen if only someone built a bigger apparatus. It was a theoretical answer to a specific physical wound. The nucleus was stable; the known forces were not enough; a short-range force pointed toward a massive carrier. The bet was daring, but not shapeless. Popper admired Einsteinian science because a serious theory exposes itself to possible refutation rather than merely collecting confirmations; the SEP account of Popper emphasizes his contrast between theories with risky testable implications and theories insulated by confirmation-seeking habits ([SEP, "Karl Popper"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/)). A good collider proposal should therefore not merely say "perhaps surprise." It should say what ignorance it is disciplined enough to attack.

"You think surprise is undisciplined?" Yukawa asked.

"No. I think surprise is not a project plan."

There it was: the clean cruelty of the controversy. Discovery machines need uncertainty. If the discovery were guaranteed, the machine would be a vending machine, not an experiment. But uncertainty can also be abused. It can become an infinite promissory note. A community can say "we do not know what we will find" with heroic honesty, or it can say the same sentence because every more precise justification would sound weaker in public.

Yukawa turned the magnet over in his palm.

"You are asking whether a society may pay for ignorance."

"No. It already pays for ignorance. Advertising, war planning, financial speculation, status architecture. I am asking when ignorance becomes noble enough to fund."

He liked that. Dead physicists are not immune to flattery if the flattery is addressed to a concept rather than a person.

The defense of the next collider begins with a fact so plain that cynicism keeps stepping over it: instruments are organs. Humanity did not "have" the microscopic world until it built microscopes, the X-ray sky until it built X-ray detectors, gravitational waves until it built kilometer-scale interferometers sensitive enough to hear spacetime move. A collider is not only a tube for smashing particles. It is a constructed sense organ for regimes of nature unavailable to the body and unavailable to cheapness. To demand that fundamental physics justify a new organ by naming in advance exactly what it will see is to misunderstand the history of seeing.

"Better," Yukawa said.

The FCC case, at its strongest, is not only "make the collision energy larger and hope." CERN describes a staged program: FCC-ee as a precision electron-positron Higgs and electroweak factory, followed by FCC-hh with far higher proton-proton energy reach ([CERN FCC](https://fcc.web.cern.ch/); [FCC-ee PED](https://fcc-ee.web.cern.ch/)). The pro-FCC argument is that precision is not consolation. Precision is a blade. If the Higgs couples even slightly differently from Standard Model expectation, if electroweak observables bend under measurement, if rare processes refuse their assigned probabilities, then a precision machine can expose the outline of new physics indirectly before a heavier particle is produced directly. The 2019 FCC-ee FAQ argues in exactly that public-explanatory mode: a high-luminosity electron-positron collider would complement the LHC and test the Standard Model through precision, not merely through spectacle ([Blondel et al., "FCC-ee: Your Questions Answered"](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.02693)).

Yukawa tapped the notebook.

"You call that less heroic because it measures deviations?"

"The public calls it less heroic."

"The public is often trained by bad storytelling."

Physics is partly a fight against bad storytelling. The vulgar story says discovery means a new particle stepping into a spotlight with a name tag. The deeper story says discovery can also be an error bar shrinking until a theory can no longer hide inside it. A null result can be a result if it closes a serious door. A precision agreement can be a triumph if it forces humility on beautiful alternatives. A machine that finds "nothing" has not necessarily found nothing; it may have found that many imagined things are not allowed to exist where we hoped they did.

"Careful," I said. "That argument can excuse anything."

"Then put conditions on it."

Good. That is where the debate has to stop being devotional.

The anti-collider argument is strongest when it refuses cheap anti-intellectualism. It should not sneer at fundamental physics as useless. Uselessness has been one of civilization's most fertile disguises. Quantum mechanics was not commissioned by a phone company app store. General relativity was not justified by GPS. Nuclear physics was not born as a medical-imaging business plan. The demand for immediate utility is usually the philosophy of a middle manager wearing the mask of moral seriousness.

But the pro-collider side must refuse cheap sanctimony too. "Basic science" is not a spell that cancels opportunity cost. A large collider does not come from the same drawer as a clever tabletop experiment. It absorbs careers, political attention, public trust, electricity, concrete, superconducting technology, and a story about what kinds of knowledge deserve spectacular institutions. If high-energy physics becomes the empire of one giant instrument after another, smaller sciences will not be wrong to ask whether curiosity has been distributed or centralized.

Yukawa looked almost amused.

"You want the collider to justify itself without ceasing to be a leap."

"Yes."

"That is a difficult standard."

"So is asking the public for decades."

The philosophical trouble is that both sides borrow virtues they do not always practice. Collider advocates borrow the virtue of courage: let us go where no instrument has gone. Critics borrow the virtue of responsibility: let us not build monuments to professional desire. But courage without accountability becomes aristocratic romance. Responsibility without imagination becomes managed decline. A civilization that never builds risky instruments becomes epistemically old before it becomes wise. A civilization that builds every grand instrument its elites can imagine becomes broke in more than one currency.

"What would you count as accountability?" Yukawa asked.

First, honesty about uncertainty. Do not sell the next collider as if it is morally entitled by the Higgs. The Higgs was a predicted quarry with an extraordinary theoretical pedigree; the 2013 Nobel citation explicitly tied the theoretical mechanism to its experimental confirmation at the LHC ([Nobel Prize, 2013 Physics Summary](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2013/summary/)). A post-Higgs machine may discover new particles; it may instead deliver exquisitely precise confirmation that nature is stingier than many models hoped. The public can be asked to fund uncertainty. It should not be sedated with fake certainty.

Second, plurality. If a huge collider is funded by starving neutrino experiments, dark-matter searches, gravitational-wave detectors, cosmic surveys, condensed-matter experiments, accelerator R&D for medicine and materials, and small laboratories where strange ideas can survive cheaply, then "frontier" has become a euphemism for monarchy. The FCC page itself lists many unanswered questions, including dark matter, matter-antimatter asymmetry, and neutrino masses ([CERN FCC](https://fcc.web.cern.ch/)). Those questions may need colliders. They also need non-collider instruments with different talents. No single machine deserves to cosplay as the entire future.

Third, environmental and social accounting. A collider is not immoral because it uses energy. That argument is too dull for physics and too convenient for people who spend energy on worse things. But a collider should be able to publish and defend its energy plan, materials plan, land impacts, governance, labor model, and public benefits with the same seriousness it brings to luminosity. The fact that a question is profound does not make the concrete metaphysical.

"Concrete is always metaphysical," Yukawa said.

"That sounds like something a civil engineer would mutter after a bad meeting."

"Then cite the engineer."

I declined. Resurrection has limits.

The fourth condition is intellectual humility about beauty. Fundamental physics has been haunted for decades by the suspicion that mathematical elegance may have become too persuasive where experiment is silent. That does not make elegance worthless. Yukawa's own move had beauty: a short-range force carried by a massive quantum, a relation between distance and mass, a hidden messenger passing between nucleons. But his beauty was tied to a stubborn phenomenon. The danger is not beauty; it is beauty with diplomatic immunity.

Yukawa stiffened.

"You are not allowed to call beauty guilty because some people used it badly."

"I am calling it a witness, not a judge."

That distinction matters. In physics, beauty can guide. It cannot acquit. A theory may be elegant and false, ugly and true, incomplete and still indispensable. The next collider should not be justified by the mood that nature owes us another elegant particle near the next convenient energy scale. Nature owes nothing. That is her charm and her abuse.

Yukawa stood and walked around the resurrection table, which was difficult because he was partly made of old grain and partly of argument.

"Let me accuse your accusation," he said.

"Please do."

"Your side has learned the language of scarcity. It says: why this tunnel, not hospitals? why this detector, not climate adaptation? why this beam, not poverty? These are serious questions. But sometimes they are asked by societies that happily fund stupidity, violence, and extraction at scales that make a collider look almost innocent. If you ask physics to justify every atom while letting propaganda, luxury, financial games, and weapons move through budgets like kings, you have not discovered ethics. You have discovered a soft target."

That hit.

He continued. "A collider is expensive because reality at that scale is hard to reach. That is not a moral defect. The harder question is whether the reaching is worthy. If it is worthy, its cost does not become shame merely because cheaper things also matter."

"And if it is not worthy?"

"Then no poetry saves it."

Good. We were no longer debating bigness. We were debating worth.

Worth, in this case, has at least four parts. Does the machine answer questions that are genuinely fundamental, not merely fashionable? Does it create measurements that can kill or constrain live theories rather than merely decorate them? Does it sustain a scientific ecosystem broader than itself? Does it tell the truth about uncertainty, cost, and alternatives? If yes, then building it can be a legitimate act of civilization. If no, then it is a cathedral to the sociology of a field.

Yukawa did not like the cathedral line.

"Cathedrals also taught people to build," he said.

"And sometimes crushed the towns that paid for them."

"So govern the town."

This, I think, is the verdict: build the next collider only as a public epistemic institution, not as an entitlement of high-energy physics and not as a prestige reflex. Build it if its advocates can defend precision as a real form of discovery, if its governance protects the rest of physics, if its environmental accounting is plain, if its opportunity costs are argued rather than hidden, and if its public case admits that the result may be silence. Do not build it if it must be sold through inflated promises, inevitability theater, or the bullying idea that anyone who asks "why this?" is an enemy of wonder.

Yukawa considered this with the careful irritation of a theorist checking boundary conditions.

"You have made yes conditional."

"Yes."

"That is less satisfying than yes."

"Most honest answers are."

"But your conditions must not become a slow no."

He was right again. Conditional support can become cowardice with paperwork. If every ambitious scientific instrument must first defeat every other moral claim on the planet, nothing large and strange will ever be built except by militaries and billionaires, which is a hideous way to allocate awe. Public science must be allowed to do things no private profit logic would do. It must be allowed to ask questions whose answers cannot be packaged. It must be allowed to make instruments before the universe has filed a customer request.

This is where Yukawa's ghost mattered most. His meson was not only a prediction. It was a moral lesson about the unseen: do not confuse invisibility with emptiness. The world may contain actors that no existing instrument can greet. The task of physics is to earn those greetings. The task of politics is to decide how much collective life should be arranged around that earning. Neither task cancels the other.

"So what do you celebrate?" he asked.

Not the tunnel as tunnel. Not gigantism. Not a circular monument to "more." I celebrate the disciplined audacity of wanting to make nature answer at a scale where ordinary human intuition is useless. I celebrate the possibility that a beam pipe can become a philosophical instrument, a way of asking whether mass, symmetry, vacuum, and force are exactly what our best theory says they are. I celebrate the humility of precision, which looks boring only to people who have never watched a decimal place murder a beautiful idea. I celebrate the part of physics that can say: we do not know, so we will build a sharper question.

Yukawa nodded.

"And what do you distrust?"

The part of physics that says "we do not know" while meaning "therefore give us everything." The part that lets elegance become entitlement. The part that treats public skepticism as vulgarity instead of as one of the costs of public money. The part that forgets that a dead end is honorable only if it was a real road.

The emulsion plate began to fade. Its tracks lightened, as if the particles were being acquitted from memory.

"Before you go," I said, "would you build it?"

Yukawa looked at the FCC printout, the potential, the budget, the tea gone cold.

"I would build the question," he said.

"That is not an answer."

"It is the only answer that survives. If the collider is the best way to build the question, build it. If it is only the best way to build importance, do not."

Then he added, with an edge I loved, "But do not let people who have never loved a hard measurement decide too quickly that measurement is waste."

That was the dead physicist at full voltage: no blank check, no apology. He had lived inside the strange dignity of theoretical prediction and experimental delay. He knew that a particle may be real before the plate records it. He also knew, because he was a serious physicist and not a motivational poster, that not every beautiful anticipation becomes a fact.

When he vanished, the emulsion stayed behind. I looked at the grains for a long time. They had the quiet authority of evidence after the event: something passed here, something interacted, something left less than a body and more than a rumor.

That is what the collider debate is really about. Not certainty versus waste. Not wonder versus hospitals. Not genius versus accountants. It is about what kinds of absence deserve instruments. Some absences are holes in our theory. Some are fantasies wearing equations. Some are invitations. Some are traps. The living have to tell the difference before the tunnel is dug.

Physics is worth celebrating because, at its best, it refuses both cowardices: the cowardice of believing only what the unaided senses already permit, and the cowardice of demanding public reverence for every expensive dream. It stands in the harder place, where an unseen particle can be real, a null result can be knowledge, a decimal can be revolutionary, and a machine can be either a new organ or a monument to appetite.

Yukawa left me with the short-range force. It does not travel forever. That is part of its truth. Maybe our justifications should be like that too: strong at close range, disciplined by what they touch, and unable to pretend that influence is infinite.
