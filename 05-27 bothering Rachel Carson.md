today is May 27, 2026.

I found Rachel Carson in Saint Luke's Episcopal Churchyard in Silver Spring, Maryland, under a stone modest enough to feel like a rebuke to the century that kept proving her right ([Britannica, "Rachel Carson"](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Rachel-Carson); [Find a Grave, "Rachel Carson"](https://www.findagrave.com/memorial/185/rachel-louise-carson)). I went there with a Geiger counter I did not need, a printout of the [first installment of *Silent Spring*](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1962/06/16/silent-spring-part-1), several pages from the [IPCC's chapter on energy systems](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-6/), the [UNSCEAR summary on Chernobyl](https://www.unscear.org/unscear/en/areas-of-work/chernobyl.html), and the [NRC's long dull assurances about spent fuel storage](https://www.nrc.gov/waste/spent-fuel-storage/faqs). I had not come to flatter her as a patron saint of environmental conscience. I had come to accuse her tradition of helping build a moral reflex that, under climate conditions, may now be killing us.

The question was simple enough to wake the dead.

Does a serious environmental ethic now require support for nuclear power, even from people formed by Rachel Carson's suspicion of technological arrogance, or is the push for nuclear expansion just another chapter in the old managerial fantasy that large systems can poison the world today and promise stewardship tomorrow?

The dirt shifted as if unwillingly. Carson rose with the exact expression of a person who had spent years warning that one form of expertise can become a licensed stupidity when it stops hearing what it is doing to the living world. She looked first at *Silent Spring*, then at the IPCC pages, then at me.

You have brought me here, she said without speaking, because you want permission to call this prudence.

Yes, I said. Or at least a test severe enough that a cheap answer would not survive it.

The case for nuclear power under climate pressure is not imaginary. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment says plainly that limiting warming to well below 2 degrees Celsius requires rapid and deep emissions cuts across energy systems, with low-carbon sources providing the overwhelming share of electricity by mid-century ([IPCC, WGIII Chapter 6 Executive Summary](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-6/)). The same chapter treats nuclear as one of the low-emissions supply options available for mitigation, noting that lifecycle greenhouse-gas emissions from nuclear generation are comparable to renewables in the literature it surveys ([IPCC, WGIII Chapter 6](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-6/)). If the atmosphere is a triage ward, then refusing a major low-carbon source because it offends an older environmental aesthetic can start to look less like principle than like vanity.

Carson hated that framing at once. A triage ward, she said, is exactly the metaphor by which every technological class excuses itself. Emergency language is how powerful institutions convert scrutiny into sentimentality. In *Silent Spring* she did not merely complain that pesticides had side effects. She attacked a whole posture of domination, the habit of releasing potent forces into the world while treating complexity as an inconvenience and downstream victims as acceptable collateral ([Rachel Carson, *Silent Spring* excerpt in *The New Yorker*](https://www.newyorker.com/magazine/1962/06/16/silent-spring-part-1)). The point was not that chemistry is evil. The point was that industrial societies repeatedly fall in love with interventions whose full ecological pathways they do not respect until too late.

I told her that this was morally serious but historically incomplete. The present danger is not only local poisoning. It is planetary destabilization driven by fossil combustion on a scale she did not live to see. The atmospheric carbon budget does not care whether decarbonization is spiritually elegant. Coal and gas still kill by heat, air pollution, crop failure, flood, fire, and long cascade. If nuclear plants can displace fossil generation at scale, then a blanket anti-nuclear politics risks becoming an ethics of beautiful refusal in a burning house.

She answered with the first hard blow. You are pretending the alternative to nuclear is coal, she said, but that is already an engineered contrast. The real question is what kinds of energy systems we choose to build, lock in, subsidize, normalize, and politically defend. Nuclear power is not just low-carbon heat. It is a social form: centralized, capital-intensive, expert-administered, dependent on long institutional continuity, and shadowed by waste that remains dangerous far beyond the lifespan of any present government. Even the NRC's own materials concede that spent fuel may remain in pool or cask storage for at least many decades, and that safe management depends on ongoing licensing, inspection, and institutional competence ([NRC, "Spent Fuel Storage in Pools and Dry Casks"](https://www.nrc.gov/waste/spent-fuel-storage/faqs); [NRC, "Backgrounder on Storage of Spent Nuclear Fuel"](https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/storage-spent-fuel)). An environmental ethic worthy of the name, she implied, cannot ignore the moral obscenity of making promises that bind strangers centuries hence.

That objection lands because the waste problem is real. High-level waste is small in volume compared with fossil waste, but its governance burden is the opposite of trivial. The NRC states openly that high-level waste must be managed in ways that protect the public for a very long time, because decay to harmlessness takes hundreds of thousands of years ([NRC, "What We Regulate"](https://www.nrc.gov/waste/hlw-disposal/what-we-regulate)). There is no honest pro-nuclear argument that gets to hand-wave that away.

But I pushed back because scale matters morally too. Fossil waste is not packaged and guarded; it is dumped directly into the sky and ocean in quantities large enough to restructure the planet. It is exactly because I take Carson's anti-carelessness seriously that I cannot accept a purity politics in which manageable, concentrated, regulated danger is treated as more intolerable than the sprawling and already-actual violence of coal, oil, and gas. The atmosphere is also a waste repository. It is simply the one we chose without consent.

Carson's face sharpened. Concentrated and regulated for whom? she asked. The history of nuclear power does not permit innocent language. The [UNSCEAR summary of Chernobyl](https://www.unscear.org/unscear/en/areas-of-work/chernobyl.html) describes immediate worker deaths, mass evacuations, thousands of thyroid cancer cases among those exposed as children or adolescents, and enduring social and psychological disruption, even while concluding that broad long-term public-health effects from radiation were more limited than apocalyptic accounts claimed. That combination matters. It means anti-nuclear mythmaking can exaggerate, yes. But it also means technocratic minimizers tell their own kind of lie. A disaster can be morally enormous without producing Hollywood body counts. Ruined trust, broken regions, displaced communities, and generations taught to live under contamination are not negligible costs.

She was right there, and I said so. One of the most corrosive habits in pro-nuclear rhetoric is the smug recitation of mortality statistics as though all harms were interchangeable units in a ledger. If one energy system gives you chronic particulate death and another gives you low-probability catastrophe plus waste stewardship plus militarized secrecy plus institutional concentration, then the choice is not morally solved by the phrase deaths per terawatt-hour. Politics is not actuarial hygiene.

Still, I would not let her retreat into the comfort of indictment. Catastrophe aversion can become its own distortion. UNSCEAR's findings also matter in the other direction: Chernobyl was ghastly, but it did not justify the full mythology of endless continent-wide genetic ruin that popular anti-nuclear imagination sometimes carried around as folk truth ([UNSCEAR, "The Chornobyl Accident"](https://www.unscear.org/unscear/en/areas-of-work/chernobyl.html)). And the NRC argues, with the usual bloodless confidence of regulators, that spent fuel pools and dry casks can provide adequate protection, with dry cask systems used for decades and reviewed under licensing and aging-management requirements ([NRC, "Storage of Spent Nuclear Fuel"](https://www.nrc.gov/waste/spent-fuel-storage.html); [NRC, "Spent Fuel Storage in Pools and Dry Casks"](https://www.nrc.gov/waste/spent-fuel-storage/faqs)). You do not have to trust every institution fully to admit that mature nuclear systems are not identical to apocalyptic fantasy.

Then Carson gave the argument its deepest turn. The real danger, she said, is that climate panic makes us stupid in a familiar way. Faced with one undeniable crisis, we start granting moral discounts to any technology that flatters our wish for continuity. Nuclear advocacy often presents itself as tragic realism, but much of it is nostalgic abundance politics: keep the grid large, keep consumption high, keep industrial appetite intact, then ask reactors to absolve the arrangement. That is not ecological seriousness. That is a demand that the physical world continue underwriting habits we are too frightened to challenge.

This was the best thing she said, because it exposed the bad faith lurking inside some pro-nuclear enthusiasm. Many people do indeed love nuclear not because they have soberly compared pathways, but because it seems like a way to preserve a high-energy modernity without the visible sprawl, intermittency, or behavioral restraint associated with other transitions. In that form, nuclear becomes less an energy source than a psychological bargaining chip against limits.

But I told her she was now in danger of a symmetrical error. It is one thing to criticize abundance ideology. It is another to romanticize scarcity, slowness, or decentralized virtue when decarbonization must occur under brutal time pressure. The IPCC does not describe climate mitigation as a poetry contest between lovable infrastructures. It describes a narrow and rapidly closing requirement for deep emissions reductions across the energy system ([IPCC, WGIII Chapter 6 Executive Summary](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-6/)). Under those conditions, refusing firm low-carbon generation on the grounds that it is too industrial can become a way of laundering political helplessness into moral refinement.

She did not yield, but she became more exact. Very well, she said. Then say what you actually support.

So I did.

I support keeping existing safe reactors online when the alternative is fossil replacement, because shutting low-carbon generation before coal and gas are gone is climate vandalism disguised as virtue ([IPCC, WGIII Chapter 6](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-6/)). I support public investment in new nuclear where it is plausibly buildable, especially where grids need firm low-carbon supply and where the political alternative is simply more gas. But I do not support treating nuclear as a redemptive master technology. I do not support exempting it from hard scrutiny over waste, cost, siting, delay, water use, or state secrecy. I do not support using climate emergency as permission to revive the old priesthood of engineers who speak as though democratic distrust were the problem rather than one of the few defenses the public has.

Carson seemed almost pleased by the severity of that answer, which annoyed me. She said it was closer to an environmental ethic than the usual conversion story. Not because it celebrated nuclear, but because it refused the consolations on both sides. The anti-nuclear camp likes innocence. The pro-nuclear camp likes inevitability. Both are evasions.

What she still doubted, however, was whether nuclear institutions deserve the long future they request. Here the argument turned from engineering to political philosophy. A reactor is never just a machine. It is a bet that a chain of organizations, regulators, maintenance regimes, legal systems, and memory practices will remain competent enough to prevent concentrated harm across generations. That is a profound demand to make in an age when states neglect bridges, abandon public housing, and drift toward authoritarian shortcuts precisely when systems become harder to manage. To build nuclear is to bind ethics to institutional confidence. To oppose it is often to bind ethics to confidence in alternative build-outs and political restraint that may also be missing. There is no innocence available.

That, I think, is the actual controversy people prefer to hide beneath slogans. The dispute over nuclear power is not mainly between science and irrational fear. It is between rival judgments about which failure modes we are willing to entrust ourselves to. One side says diffuse fossil chaos is already the larger horror, so concentrated nuclear risk is a tolerable bargain. The other says institutions that have repeatedly lied about industrial hazards should not be granted a climate halo simply because carbon has made the emergency larger. Both claims have teeth.

My conclusion, after waking Rachel Carson and making her argue beside her own grave, is that a serious environmentalist in 2026 should support a constrained, unsentimental, heavily regulated role for nuclear power, especially to preserve existing low-carbon electricity and in some cases to add new firm generation, but should do so without surrendering Carson's central lesson. Her lesson is not "never use large technology." It is never let technical power speak as though scale were wisdom, as though delayed harms were abstractions, or as though the living world were obliged to absorb every cleverness we invent.

That means the pro-nuclear side has to grow up. If you want reactors, then you inherit the duty to tell the truth about waste horizons, accident consequences, siting injustice, and institutional fragility. You do not get to call yourself pragmatic while offloading your liabilities onto unborn custodians. And the anti-nuclear side has to grow up too. If you reject nuclear categorically under present climate conditions, you must explain, concretely, what replaces the fossil generation that remains, how fast, under what politics, with what land, transmission, storage, and industrial timelines, and why your refusal does not amount to a ceremonial politics of cleaner hands and dirtier air.

Carson sank before dawn. Her last look was not forgiving. It did not need to be. I had not raised her for absolution. I had raised her because the climate argument about nuclear power is one of the places where environmental morality is forced to choose between two of its own deepest intuitions: distrust concentrated industrial danger, and do not burn the future for the sake of the present. She would not let me keep only one.

That is why she remains so inconveniently alive. She still makes the question harder. And because she does, she makes it more honest.
