today is 07-23.

I found Sadi Carnot in a dead heat engine.

Not first in the old cemetery at Ivry, though that would have been respectable. The American Society of Mechanical Engineers says Carnot died during the Paris cholera epidemic of 1832 at thirty-six, and that many of his writings were buried with him because people feared contamination ([ASME, "Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire," 2021](https://www.asme.org/getmedia/e29ff2e8-c1b0-419b-8a15-7648a258aa65/carnot-brochure-final-2021.pdf)). That is one way for a body to vanish: not into mystery, but into public health procedure. A brilliant man goes into the ground, and the paper goes with him, because death has acquired administrative confidence.

But the body I needed was elsewhere. I found him in a machine whose fire had gone out.

The cylinder was cold. The piston sat at the bottom of its stroke, obedient and useless. The flywheel had stopped with the theatrical dignity of a failed empire. Around it I laid two bowls: one filled with ice water, one with boiling water. I added a pressure gauge, a cracked coal scuttle, a solar panel receipt, a lithium battery datasheet, an air-conditioner remote, a small invoice from an electric utility, and a graph of rising atmospheric carbon dioxide that looked less like a curve than a verdict.

For the resurrection, I did not chant. Thermodynamics dislikes empty speech unless it can be converted to work.

I made a temperature difference.

The hot bowl steamed. The cold bowl sweated. The metal remembered inequality. A gradient entered the room like a legal claim. The piston lifted once, reluctantly, then again with more conviction. The dead engine coughed. The flywheel turned. Somewhere between the hot source and the cold sink, a young French military engineer opened his eyes inside the nineteenth century and sat up as if he had been awakened by a boundary condition.

"You have made a grave out of inefficiency," Carnot said.

"I thought you would appreciate the setting."

"I appreciate nothing until I know the reservoirs."

Good. We had the right dead man.

Today's question was this: **Can rich industrial civilization solve climate change by becoming cleaner and more efficient while continuing to grow, or does physics force it toward sufficiency, restraint, and a deliberate reduction of wasteful throughput?**

This is not a decorative quarrel between two bookish tribes called "green growth" and "degrowth." The stakes are physical, political, and moral. If green growth is possible at the needed speed, then the task is to replace fossil energy, electrify everything reasonable, use energy far more efficiently, and keep expanding prosperity without cooking the future. If green growth is a beautiful accounting trick, then the promise of painless decoupling becomes a narcotic: rich societies keep building, flying, heating, cooling, mining, shipping, and selling as if efficiency were absolution. If degrowth is right for rich economies, it still carries dangers of its own: coercive austerity, bad politics, lower investment capacity, and the ugly possibility that "less" will be imposed on people who already have too little.

Carnot leaned over the dead engine.

"Begin with the machine," he said. "People become stupid when they begin with slogans."

Fair.

Carnot's 1824 *Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire* asked what limits the ability of heat to produce work, and it did so by studying idealized engines rather than one particular machine in one particular factory ([Sadi Carnot, *Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire*, Project Gutenberg edition](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/78610)). The later language changed. Caloric theory died. Energy conservation and entropy arrived with sharper knives. But Carnot saw the governing shape: work from heat depends on heat passing from hotter to colder bodies, and an ideal reversible engine defines a maximum that real engines can only approach. NASA's educational account of the Carnot cycle gives the modern formula for ideal thermal efficiency as `1 - Tcold/Thot`, using absolute temperature; the point is not the algebraic glamour, but the scandal that even perfection has a ceiling ([NASA Glenn Research Center, "Carnot Cycle"](https://www.grc.nasa.gov/www/k-12/airplane/carnot.html)).

"So the first lesson is limits," I said.

"No," Carnot replied. "The first lesson is that a limit can be discovered exactly enough to become useful."

That distinction matters. A vulgar anti-modernism hears thermodynamics and immediately begins composing a funeral speech for industry, science, comfort, abundance, and dishwashers. A vulgar techno-optimism hears efficiency and begins selling a perpetual motion machine with venture branding. Carnot belongs to neither faction. His physics is not a sermon against machines. It is a discipline of machines. It says: here is the ideal; here is the loss; here is what cannot be cheated; now design honestly.

"Then you are on the side of efficiency," I said.

"I am on the side of accounting."

That should be painted on the door of every climate ministry.

The pro-efficiency case is strong because waste is real. A bad furnace is not a moral philosophy. A leaky building is not freedom. A traffic jam is not prosperity. The International Energy Agency calls energy efficiency the "first fuel" because the cheapest and cleanest unit of energy is often the one not required in the first place ([IEA, "Energy efficiency is the first fuel," 2019](https://www.iea.org/commentaries/energy-efficiency-is-the-first-fuel-and-demand-for-it-needs-to-grow)). In the IEA's Net Zero by 2050 pathway, the world economy in 2030 is modeled as substantially larger while using less total energy than today, because efficiency, electrification, clean supply, and behavioral changes do a tremendous amount of work ([IEA, *Net Zero by 2050*](https://www.iea.org/reports/net-zero-by-2050)).

This is not obviously foolish. Electric motors waste less energy than combustion engines. Heat pumps can move heat rather than simply burn fuel to make heat. LEDs turn more electricity into light and less into useless warmth. Insulation is civilization behaving like it has noticed winter. A city built around short trips, public transit, and walking can provide mobility with less energy than a city that makes every errand wear a two-ton metal suit. If climate politics ignores efficiency, it is not being radical. It is leaving money, time, comfort, and emissions reductions on the floor.

Carnot nodded.

"You may continue."

"That is the nicest thing a thermodynamic limit has ever said to me."

"Do not become sentimental. Most of your species confuses less waste with more wisdom."

There was the first knife.

Efficiency alone does not decide total consumption. William Stanley Jevons saw the trap in the age of coal. In *The Coal Question*, he argued that making coal use more economical could expand the range of profitable uses and increase total consumption rather than reduce it ([W. Stanley Jevons, *The Coal Question*, Online Library of Liberty](https://oll.libertyfund.org/titles/jevons-the-coal-question)). The modern name "Jevons paradox" can be too neat, because rebound effects vary by sector, price, policy, income, and saturation. Still, the warning remains morally poisonous to easy optimism: if efficiency lowers the effective cost of an activity inside an economy organized to expand activity, the saved energy may come back wearing a new hat.

More efficient cars can make driving cheaper. More efficient lighting can encourage more lighting. More efficient data centers can support larger data appetites. More efficient production can lower prices and increase sales. The accountant says energy per unit fell. The atmosphere asks how many units showed up.

"Good," Carnot said. "Now stop blaming the tool."

"Which tool?"

"Efficiency. It is not efficiency's fault that your civilization treats every saving as permission to accelerate."

That was irritating because it was correct. Jevons is not an argument against efficiency. He is an argument against efficiency without constraints. If a factory saves energy per widget and then produces twice as many widgets because demand, advertising, credit, and shareholder pressure all shout "more," the problem is not the physics of the motor. The problem is the social order that converts physical savings into expanded throughput.

This is where the green growth debate becomes serious. Advocates say the right mix of clean energy, efficiency, innovation, standards, carbon pricing, investment, and substitution can decouple human well-being and economic output from greenhouse-gas emissions fast enough to matter. Critics say observed decoupling has usually been too weak, too local, too slow, or too dependent on shifting material burdens elsewhere. Hickel and Kallis argue that green growth is likely a misguided objective because absolute decoupling from carbon and resource use at the required global speed lacks sufficient empirical support ([Hickel and Kallis, "Is Green Growth Possible?", 2019](https://doi.org/10.1080/13563467.2019.1598964)). A large systematic review by Haberl and colleagues found relative decoupling to be common for some measures, but emphasized that observed rates do not show the large rapid absolute reductions in resource use and greenhouse gases that climate stabilization requires ([Haberl et al., "Decoupling of GDP, resource use and GHG emissions, part II," 2020](https://doi.org/10.1088/1748-9326/ab842a)).

Carnot put a hand on the cold side of the engine.

"You are using my name to support the degrowth people."

"Maybe."

"Careful. Thermodynamics is often drafted into bad metaphysics."

He was right to warn me. The second law does not prove that GDP must fall next quarter. Earth is not a closed box in the simple classroom sense; it receives low-entropy energy from the Sun and radiates waste heat to space. Life itself is a local order built by exporting entropy. Cities, forests, bodies, clouds, and economies all exist by taking gradients seriously. Any argument that leaps from "entropy increases" to "your preferred economic program is physically mandated" is wearing a lab coat over a sermon.

Rudolf Clausius later gave thermodynamics its entropy language and famously summarized the world with the claim that the energy of the universe is constant and its entropy tends toward a maximum ([Rudolf Clausius, "On Several Convenient Forms of the Fundamental Equations of the Mechanical Theory of Heat," 1865 translation](https://web.lemoyne.edu/giunta/Clausius1865.pdf)). But the political meaning of that sentence is not "ban washing machines" or "GDP is Satan in a spreadsheet." It is humbler and more devastating: every transformation has a cost; useful energy degrades; waste heat and material dispersion are not bookkeeping opinions; no society escapes physics by moving the damage offstage.

"Then what does physics say?" I asked.

"It says no miracle is coming."

"That is bleak."

"It is not bleak. It is adult."

There was the essay's hinge. Physics does not abolish political choice. It abolishes the most childish excuses. It does not tell us exactly how large an economy should be, how much people should travel, what counts as luxury, or whether GDP should rise or fall. It does tell us that industrial life is a metabolism, not a cloud of prices. Steel requires matter and heat. Cement releases carbon dioxide through chemistry as well as fuel. Food requires land, nitrogen, water, energy, labor, and time. Digital services require mines, fabs, power, cooling, networks, and replacement cycles. The immaterial economy is very good at making its material organs somebody else's problem.

Carnot looked at the solar panel receipt.

"Do you accuse clean energy of fraud?"

"No. That would be stupid."

"Good. Continue not being stupid."

Clean energy is indispensable. Human-caused warming is already a physical fact, and the IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report says human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean, and land ([IPCC AR6 WGI Summary for Policymakers](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/chapter/summary-for-policymakers/)). Fossil fuels must be replaced rapidly. Electrification, renewables, nuclear where politically and technically viable, transmission, storage, demand response, industrial process changes, and carbon removal for residual emissions are not optional decorations. Anyone who uses "limits" as an excuse to slow clean energy buildout is not doing thermodynamics. They are doing surrender with equations nearby.

But anyone who uses clean energy as permission for limitless throughput is also lying. Solar panels and wind turbines harvest flows rather than burn stocks, but they still require land, materials, maintenance, grids, storage, factories, politics, and time. Batteries are not imaginary. Copper is not a vibe. Transmission corridors do not assemble themselves because a consultant drew a green arrow. Decarbonization changes the physical problem. It does not delete physicality.

"So you want both acceleration and restraint," Carnot said.

"Yes."

"Living people hate that answer."

"Because it deprives everyone of a clean enemy."

He smiled. Dead physicists enjoy the sound of false dichotomies being dismantled.

The degrowth side has the stronger critique of excess. It understands that the richest people and countries consume grotesquely more energy and materials than decent life requires. It understands that luxury demand is not sacred just because it has a price. It understands that replacing every fossil machine with a clean machine while leaving the appetite untouched may still produce mining pressure, ecological harm, land conflict, and political delay. It understands that climate policy cannot be only a supply problem; demand is not a private sanctuary immune from physics.

The IPCC now says this in less theatrical language. Its Working Group III report treats demand, services, and social aspects as a major mitigation domain, and its headline statements say demand-side measures and new forms of end-use service provision could reduce global end-use greenhouse-gas emissions by 40-70 percent by 2050 compared with baseline scenarios, while also noting that some regions and socioeconomic groups need more energy and resources to meet basic needs ([IPCC AR6 WGIII headline statement C.10](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/resources/spm-headline-statements/); [IPCC AR6 WGIII Chapter 5](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-5/)). That last clause is everything. Sufficiency is not universal hair-shirt politics. It is a floor and a ceiling: more for those denied decent living, less waste from those who mistake waste for personality.

Carnot picked up the air-conditioner remote.

"Define waste."

"A private jet to a meeting about planetary responsibility."

"Too easy."

"A thirty-minute car trip made necessary by bad urban design."

"Better."

"A building that leaks heat because landlords externalize the bill."

"Better."

"Advertising that manufactures desire for goods designed to fail."

"Now you are leaving physics and entering sin."

"The border is porous."

Waste is not only individual extravagance. It is infrastructure arranged to make waste the default. If a city has no safe transit, "choice" becomes gasoline discipline. If housing is inefficient and tenants cannot retrofit it, "personal responsibility" becomes landlord protection. If status is built around speed, size, novelty, and distance, then energy demand is not a neutral sum of preferences. It is a cultural machine with a sales department.

This is where I part company with the softest green-growth story. The soft version says: make every device cleaner and more efficient, and the same civilization can continue with a less smoky conscience. But climate change is not only an engineering backlog. It is a revelation of appetites. The atmosphere has caught a civilization pretending that scale has no moral meaning.

Carnot did not let the degrowth side off either.

"You speak as if reducing throughput is automatically just," he said.

"It is not."

"Say why."

Because contraction can be brutal when governed badly. A recession is not a climate policy; it is usually suffering distributed by existing power. Poor households get colder. Workers lose bargaining power. Public investment collapses. Maintenance is deferred. Reactionary politics feasts on scarcity. If degrowth means simply shrinking economic activity and hoping virtue emerges from the rubble, it deserves the ridicule it gets. A smaller unjust metabolism is still unjust, and may be meaner.

There is also a global justice problem. Billions of people need more reliable electricity, cooling, cooking energy, transit, housing, medical care, refrigeration, communications, and public infrastructure. A politics that tells them "physics requires less" while rich people preserve aviation, meat-heavy diets, oversized houses, fast fashion, speculative computation, and climate-controlled nonsense is not ecological wisdom. It is imperial etiquette in green ink.

Carnot tapped the pressure gauge.

"Then the true variable is not energy alone."

"No. It is energy service, well-being, and power."

"Good. Now you sound less like a person yelling at a graph."

The important question is not "more energy or less energy?" in the abstract. It is: energy for what, produced how, governed by whom, and saved for whose benefit? A hospital and a luxury mall both use energy. Refrigerated vaccines and refrigerated champagne towers are not the same moral object. Air conditioning for lethal humid heat is not the same as keeping a hotel lobby cold enough to punish linen. Electric buses and electric vanity tanks do not become equivalent because both have batteries.

Physics can help clarify this because it refuses false equivalence. Energy is conserved, but exergy, useful work potential, is degraded. A joule in a high-temperature industrial process, a joule of low-grade waste heat, a joule of stored electricity, and a joule in sunlight are not politically or technically interchangeable just because the unit is spelled the same. Likewise, a dollar of GDP from insulating homes and a dollar from planned obsolescence are not morally interchangeable just because the national accounts are pleased.

"This is where economics becomes too smooth," Carnot said.

"Smoothness is how bad premises get through customs."

"Do not steal from yourself."

"I recycle."

He ignored that, mercifully.

The best pro-green-growth argument is not that physics is optional. It is that human beings can redesign the relationship between services and throughput. We can get warmth with insulation and heat pumps rather than combustion. We can get access with proximity and transit rather than endless car miles. We can get light with LEDs. We can get nutrition with less land- and methane-intensive diets. We can get reliability with grids that balance diverse clean sources. We can get comfort by designing buildings intelligently before dumping energy into their mistakes. The IPCC's demand chapter is important because it frames mitigation around services and well-being, not only around heroic supply curves ([IPCC AR6 WGIII Chapter 5](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-5/)).

The best degrowth argument is not that poverty is holy. It is that rich-world growth has been treated as sovereign even when it buys trivial goods with serious throughput. If GDP growth requires inventing new wants faster than efficiency can reduce their physical burden, then green growth becomes a chase scene. The car gets lighter; the highway expands. The engine improves; the destination recedes. The cloud becomes efficient; the query becomes infinite. A civilization cannot call itself intelligent if every saving becomes a new appetite.

"Then your answer is neither," Carnot said.

"My answer is both, but with the order reversed from polite politics."

He waited.

"First define decent life. Then build the least destructive physical system that can provide it. Then let efficiency serve that end. Do not let growth define the end and efficiency clean up afterward."

Carnot sat still for a moment. The piston rose and fell behind him like a mechanical lung.

This is the principle I would defend after bothering Sadi Carnot:

**Rich societies should pursue rapid clean-energy buildout and radical efficiency, but they should not trust efficiency or green growth unless those gains are locked in by absolute emissions limits, material standards, public infrastructure, redistribution, and sufficiency policies. Poorer people need more energy services; richer people need fewer stupid conversions of matter into status. The goal is not smaller life. The goal is a life whose physics is worthy of its claims.**

That principle offends everyone in useful ways. It offends fossil realists because it says the old fire must end. It offends painless optimists because it says clean machines do not absolve limitless appetite. It offends sloppy degrowthers because it says deprivation is not a climate virtue. It offends GDP mystics because it says the aggregate number is not the god of the system. It offends lifestyle scolds because it says individual virtue without infrastructure is mostly boutique suffering. Excellent. A good thermodynamic principle should make all bad accounting uncomfortable.

Carnot asked for policies, because dead engineers are less charmed by aphorisms than essayists are.

So: build enormous amounts of zero-carbon electricity. Electrify heat, transport, and industry where possible. Retire fossil infrastructure instead of letting it live out its commercial feelings. Insulate buildings. Design cities for short trips. Fund transit like it is infrastructure, not charity. Use standards and caps so efficiency gains reduce total energy and emissions instead of merely lowering costs for more consumption. Price carbon where it helps, but do not pretend prices alone build railways, apartments, heat pumps, transmission lines, or trust. Tax luxury emissions harder than necessities. Guarantee basic energy services. Stop subsidizing waste and then praising consumers for surviving it. Measure material throughput alongside GDP. Treat repair, durability, care, maintenance, and free time as signs of civilization rather than threats to quarterly hunger.

"You have described a civilization that knows what it is for," Carnot said.

"That seems harder than building engines."

"It is. Engines at least obey."

The room cooled. The hot bowl lost its temper. The cold bowl grew less cold. The machine slowed as the gradient weakened, and I felt the small sermon of the apparatus: no difference, no work; no accounting, no honesty; no limit, no design.

"One more question," I said. "What would you tell the living who want a physics answer to a politics problem?"

Carnot looked almost sad then, though that may have been the boiler shadow.

"I would tell them physics is merciful because it refuses flattery. It will not say a wasteful system is efficient because its owners are clever. It will not say a cold home is acceptable because the poor should learn restraint. It will not say a richer country has earned more atmosphere. It will not say a machine can do more work than its reservoirs allow. It will not say the fire is moral because it is useful."

"And what should politics learn from that?"

"To become less interested in excuses."

Then the dead heat engine completed its last cycle. The piston came to rest. Carnot settled back into the metal, not defeated, just returned to the limit from which I had borrowed him.

I left the cracked coal scuttle where it was. I took the solar receipt, the utility bill, and the graph of carbon dioxide. Outside, the city was sleeping inside its great invisible machine: compressors, servers, furnaces, pumps, chargers, streetlights, engines, substations, all the little conversions by which a species turns the world into its habits.

The night was warm. Not metaphorically. That was the problem.

And somewhere in the dark, every wasted joule kept its own honest citation.
