today is 07-09.

I found I. I. Rabi in a tomb with a radio inside it.

Not the literal grave. I did not go to a cemetery with a shovel and a theory of entitlement. I found the more exact tomb: the bore of a magnetic resonance scanner, that white technical throat in which sick people are slid into a question and answered by the nuclei of their own bodies. Rabi won the 1944 Nobel Prize in Physics for a resonance method that recorded the magnetic properties of atomic nuclei, making beams and radio waves teach nuclei to reveal their frequencies ([Nobel Prize, Isidor Isaac Rabi Facts](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1944/rabi/facts/)). There are tombs made of marble. There are tombs made of consequences.

I made the resurrection apparatus from things that belonged to him and things that now belong to us: a diagram of molecular-beam resonance, a photograph of a patient entering an MRI machine, a page from Norman Ramsey's National Academy memoir describing Rabi as a scientist-statesman who helped advocate the European collaboration that became CERN, and the fresh public argument over CERN's proposed Future Circular Collider, a 91-kilometre ring planned under France and Switzerland at an average depth of about 200 metres ([Ramsey, "I. I. Rabi 1898-1988"](https://www.nasonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/rabi-i-i.pdf); [CERN, "Future Circular Collider"](https://home.cern/science/accelerators/future-circular-collider/)).

To wake him, I tuned the room instead of the corpse.

The spell was not Latin. It was a sweep through frequency space. I put the dead physicist inside a magnetic field, sent in a pulse, and waited for absorption. The dead are usually poor experimental subjects, but physicists are unusually vulnerable to a well-posed resonance.

The air clicked. A hydrogen nucleus somewhere in the wall changed its mind. A narrow peak rose on an imaginary oscilloscope. Then Rabi sat up in the scanner, gray, sharp, and annoyed in the manner of a man who had once looked at the muon and reportedly asked, "Who ordered that?" ([CERN Courier, "Who ordered all of that?"](https://cerncourier.com/a/who-ordered-all-of-that/)).

"You put me in a medical instrument," he said.

"It seemed fair. Your experiment got here before you did."

"Do not confuse descendants with intentions."

"I was planning to."

"Then begin by not doing it."

Fine. The question was this: **Should humanity build CERN's Future Circular Collider, a vast new Higgs factory and possible 100 TeV successor to the Large Hadron Collider, or would that be a magnificent misuse of money, land, energy, attention, and scientific authority in a century already crowded with emergencies?**

This is not a cheap argument about whether physics is beautiful. Physics is beautiful. The issue is whether beauty gets a blank check. The stakes are concrete: 15 billion CHF for the FCC with four interaction points, according to CERN's current public estimate; 16.4 million tonnes of excavated material over construction; public debate in France from June 2 to October 1, 2026; scientific promises about Higgs precision, dark matter, antimatter, and new phenomena; and the moral question of what counts as a worthy civilization-scale instrument when heat, war, poverty, housing, biology, and artificial intelligence are all standing outside the same treasury with their mouths open ([CERN, "Future Circular Collider"](https://home.cern/science/accelerators/future-circular-collider/); [Legifrance, CNDP decision opening the 2026 FCC public debate](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/id/JORFTEXT000054070394)).

Rabi looked at the number.

"Fifteen billion Swiss francs," he said. "They have learned to make the decimal point civilized."

"You helped make big physics respectable."

"I helped make institutions when institutions were needed. That is not the same as blessing every tunnel."

Good. He would not be an easy mascot.

The case for the collider begins with the best sentence CERN has: the Standard Model is not complete. The LHC's discovery of a particle consistent with the Higgs boson in 2012 completed the particle content of the Standard Model in one sense, but it also opened the harder phase: measuring the Higgs in detail and asking where the known theory leaks ([CERN, "The Higgs boson"](https://home.cern/science/physics/higgs-boson/)). Dark matter has not been identified. Matter's dominance over antimatter remains unexplained. Neutrino masses already tell us the old account is incomplete. CERN describes the FCC's first stage as an electron-positron collider designed to study the Higgs, electroweak physics, and the top quark with unprecedented precision, with a later proton-proton collider operating around 100 TeV ([CERN, "CERN releases report on the feasibility of a possible Future Circular Collider"](https://home.cern/cern-releases-report-feasibility-possible-future-circular-collider/)).

"Precision," Rabi said, "is not a consolation prize."

"No," I said. "It is how nature is forced to stop speaking in slogans."

The lazy public version of discovery imagines science as a slot machine: insert collider, receive particle. That is not how the best case for the FCC works. A Higgs factory may not immediately cough up a glamorous beast. It may instead measure couplings, branching ratios, rare decays, electroweak parameters, invisible widths, and tiny deviations from the Standard Model until the space of possible theories becomes smaller and more truthful. The U.S. 2023 Particle Physics Project Prioritization Panel endorsed an offshore Higgs factory in Europe or Japan and argued for substantial U.S. participation in design and construction, precisely because the Higgs is the next obvious handle on the quantum realm ([2023 P5 Report, Executive Summary](https://www.usparticlephysics.org/2023-p5-report/executive-summary.html); [2023 P5 Report, Recommended Program](https://www.usparticlephysics.org/2023-p5-report/the-recommended-particle-physics-program.html)).

"You are defending a microscope," Rabi said, "not a lottery ticket."

"Yes."

"Then stop calling it a machine to answer every mystery."

That rebuke was deserved. The pro-FCC rhetoric is strongest when it is modest and weakest when it becomes cosmic perfume. The FCC should not be sold as a universal key to everything spooky in the universe. CERN's own public page lists dark matter, matter-antimatter asymmetry, new particles, new forces, and rare phenomena among the questions it might probe, but "might" is doing adult work there ([CERN, "Future Circular Collider"](https://home.cern/science/accelerators/future-circular-collider/)). The honest case is not that a bigger ring guarantees revolution. The honest case is that our best known laws have unresolved stress fractures, and the Higgs is one of the few places where a precision instrument can press directly on them.

Rabi approved of "stress fractures." He disliked "cosmic perfume."

"You are learning."

"Temporarily."

There is another pro-collider argument, and it is the one Rabi himself made dangerous. Fundamental physics has a habit of returning to the world through side doors. Rabi's resonance method was not invented as a way to find tumors, but magnetic resonance became part of the lineage that made MRI possible. CERN's defense of fundamental research points to medical imaging, hadron therapy, aerospace, security, environmental protection, cultural heritage, and the general fact that applied science feeds on knowledge that was not born useful ([CERN, "Future Circular Collider"](https://home.cern/science/accelerators/future-circular-collider/)). The scanner in which I resurrected him was a rebuttal to every accountant who thinks useless questions stay useless.

Rabi looked around the machine.

"This is the vulgar defense."

"The MRI?"

"No. The argument from eventual utility. It is true, and therefore especially tempting to abuse."

He was right again, annoyingly. If fundamental science must justify itself by future gadgets, then it has already allowed the wrong tribunal to define value. The better defense is that understanding reality is one of the things humans do when they are not merely surviving. It is as serious as law, music, mourning, architecture, mathematics, and the refusal to let the universe remain a rumor. Merton's old account of the scientific ethos treated organized skepticism, universalism, communal knowledge, and disinterestedness as institutional imperatives, not because science is pure, but because knowledge has social conditions that must be protected ([Merton, "The Normative Structure of Science"](https://law.unimelb.edu.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0005/3609203/1c-Merton-The-Normative-Structure-of-Science.pdf)). A civilization that never funds questions without immediate profit becomes practical in the same way a locked room is secure.

"Better," Rabi said. "But you are still romanticizing."

"Of course. We are discussing a 91-kilometre instrument for asking a boson how it feels about vacuum expectation values."

"Romance is permitted. Exemption is not."

There the indictment begins.

Fifteen billion CHF is not a metaphor. Sixteen point four million tonnes of excavated material is not a metaphor. A tunnel under towns, farmland, roads, aquifers, borders, and Lake Geneva is not a metaphor. The French public debate is not decorative theatre; the formal decision opening it says the FCC dossier was complete enough to begin public debate on the collider and its electrical connection in Ain and Haute-Savoie, with the debate scheduled from June 2 to October 1, 2026 ([Legifrance, CNDP decision](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/jorf/id/JORFTEXT000054070394)). People live above the future abstraction. They are not topsoil with opinions.

"Good," Rabi said. "No laboratory owns the ground by being interesting."

The anti-FCC case, in its strongest form, is not anti-science. It is anti-capture. A field can become so organized around a machine that the machine begins to impersonate the field. Young researchers train for it. Senior researchers build careers around it. National prestige attaches to it. Industrial contracts gather around it. Outreach learns the hymns. Alternatives become unserious before they are examined. The question changes from "What should physics do?" to "How do we keep this project alive?" That is not curiosity. That is institutional inertia with equations painted on the door.

Critics of next-generation colliders have argued that no known principle guarantees new particles at the next accessible energy scale, and that the money would be better directed elsewhere; Sabine Hossenfelder's blunt version in Scientific American was that the world does not need a new gigantic particle collider ([Hossenfelder, "The World Doesn't Need a New Gigantic Particle Collider"](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-world-doesnt-need-a-new-gigantic-particle-collider/)). One need not accept her whole case to accept the moral bruise inside it. High-energy physics is not the only way to know the world. Condensed matter, climate physics, plasma physics, biophysics, geophysics, quantum materials, instrumentation, epidemiology, and computational science are also reality pressing its face against glass.

Rabi, who had built instruments and also advised presidents, did not let the criticism become cheap.

"Do not say 'climate' as if money automatically walks from one treasury to another," he said.

"It sometimes does."

"Sometimes. Often it vanishes into less noble appetites. The question is not 'collider or hospitals' unless the budget process makes it so. Be precise."

Fine. The opportunity-cost argument must not be lazy either. A franc not spent on the FCC does not magically become a school, a heat pump, a malaria vaccine, a climate adaptation grant, or a salary for a young astrophysicist. But budgets do express preference under constraint. The FCC would be a multi-decade commitment of talent, political attention, engineering capacity, electricity, land negotiation, and public trust. Those are real currencies. Even if the money cannot be transferred cleanly, the burden of justification remains.

"There," Rabi said. "You may continue."

The deepest problem is uncertainty. The LHC had a guaranteed quarry: the Higgs, or the crisis produced by no Higgs. The FCC-ee has guaranteed measurements, not guaranteed rupture. The later 100 TeV hadron collider has greater discovery reach, but the path from first tunnel to final energy frontier would stretch across much of the century. CERN's current timeline places a decision around 2028 and start-up in the late 2040s ([CERN, "Future Circular Collider"](https://home.cern/science/accelerators/future-circular-collider/)). Asking the public to fund an instrument whose deepest consequences will belong to people not yet voting is not automatically wrong. It is how cathedrals, sewers, constitutions, telescopes, and climate policy work. But it requires a language cleaner than hype.

Rabi touched the MRI casing.

"A good instrument has a right to exist only if it can be wrong."

"The FCC can be wrong?"

"It can find no deviation. It can make many theories die. That is not nothing."

This is the philosophical rescue of the collider, if it deserves one. Science progresses not only by finding the thing, but by tightening the noose around false possibilities. The Stanford Encyclopedia's discussion of the social dimensions of scientific knowledge is useful here because it refuses the fantasy that research agendas are socially weightless; inquiry has effects on human life and is shaped by social values and institutions ([SEP, "The Social Dimensions of Scientific Knowledge"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-knowledge-social/)). That does not make physics subjective mush. It means that deciding which expensive ignorance to attack is a political and ethical act as well as an epistemic one.

"Then let the public decide," I said.

"The public cannot decide cross sections."

"Physicists cannot decide legitimacy alone."

He smiled. That was the first time he seemed pleased.

"Now we have a problem worth keeping."

The right question is not whether democracy should vote on the Higgs self-coupling. That would be stupid in a way only a committee could formalize. The right question is whether experts can explain the epistemic stakes, uncertainties, risks, alternatives, and opportunity costs honestly enough that democratic institutions can decide whether this search belongs in the common future. Kitcher's idea of "well-ordered science" is often summarized as asking how research agendas would look after informed, idealized democratic deliberation rather than after either pure curiosity or raw power has had its way ([SEP, "The Social Dimensions of Scientific Knowledge"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-knowledge-social/); [Georgetown review of Kitcher's science and democracy work](https://kiej.georgetown.edu/announcements/philip-kitcher-science-in-a-democratic-society-prometheus-books-2011/)). The FCC is exactly the kind of project that has to pass that test.

Rabi disliked the phrase "well-ordered science."

"Sounds like a drawer."

"Would you prefer badly ordered science?"

"No. I prefer science with enough disorder to discover something and enough order not to bankrupt the village."

There it was: the doctrine in one sentence, if one forgives him for being concise.

So I asked him directly. "Would you build it?"

He did not answer. Dead physicists know the cheapness of yes and no.

Instead he made me state the pro-FCC case in terms that could survive an angry town hall.

First: the Higgs is not just another particle. It is the experimental face of the mechanism by which the electroweak theory gives masses to elementary particles. Studying it precisely is not particle collecting. It is probing one of the deepest structural facts of the known quantum world ([CERN, "The Higgs boson"](https://home.cern/science/physics/higgs-boson/)).

Second: precision is discovery's quieter sibling. If the Higgs couplings deviate from Standard Model predictions, the deviation may point to new physics beyond direct production. If they do not, entire families of theories lose room to breathe. The machine must be defended as a disciplined way to make the possible smaller.

Third: a Higgs factory has broad international support inside particle physics. The U.S. P5 panel endorsed substantial participation in an offshore Higgs factory while maintaining a broader program, which matters because even the advocates understand that one machine cannot be allowed to eat the ecosystem ([2023 P5 Report, Executive Summary](https://www.usparticlephysics.org/2023-p5-report/executive-summary.html)).

Fourth: CERN itself is not just a landlord for beams. Rabi helped advocate the kind of European scientific collaboration that became CERN, and the point of that institution was partly to make science a peaceable structure after catastrophe ([Ramsey, "I. I. Rabi 1898-1988"](https://www.nasonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/rabi-i-i.pdf)). In an age of fragmentation, a global laboratory is not a small achievement.

"Good," he said. "Now the indictment."

First: if the FCC is sold as guaranteed access to dark matter, the origin of the universe, or every unsolved mystery with a grant-writing costume, it becomes dishonest. CERN may list those open questions as motivations, but the public deserves the difference between guaranteed measurements and speculative discovery ([CERN, "Future Circular Collider"](https://home.cern/science/accelerators/future-circular-collider/)).

Second: the first-stage Higgs factory and the later 100 TeV proton collider should not be morally bundled into one pre-approved destiny. The first is a precision instrument with a clearer target. The second is a grander energy-frontier gamble. The tunnel may make them physically connected, but democratic consent should not be smuggled through the same ring.

Third: environmental and local burdens are not public-relations details. CERN says it would apply avoid-reduce-offset principles, manage excavated material, and comply with environmental rules, but the people affected by shafts, construction, spoil, roads, water, electricity, noise, and land use have standing in the argument ([CERN, "Future Circular Collider"](https://home.cern/science/accelerators/future-circular-collider/)). A scientific instrument can be true and still be a bad neighbour.

Fourth: a healthy physics culture needs pluralism. If the FCC starves smaller experiments, alternative accelerator concepts, detector R&D, theory not tethered to the flagship, or young scientists whose futures become collateral for one machine, then it will have failed even if it measures beautifully.

Fifth: the opportunity cost must be counted in attention as well as money. Public trust is finite. If physicists ask for a century-scale instrument, they must not act offended when the century asks what it gets in return.

Rabi leaned back inside the scanner like a monarch of narrow resonances.

"You want a conditional yes."

"I want a yes with teeth."

"State it."

Here is the answer I would risk before the dead: **build the FCC-ee only if the public case remains brutally honest, the environmental and local process has real power, the project is governed so it cannot devour the rest of physics, and the later 100 TeV hadron collider is treated as a future decision rather than a destiny hidden in the first excavation. Do not build a monument to particle physics. Build an instrument that can lose.**

Rabi considered the phrase "instrument that can lose."

"That is nearly a principle."

"I stole it from experiments."

"As one should."

An instrument that can lose has three virtues. It states what would count as disappointment. It does not convert every null result into public-relations victory. It preserves room for rival approaches. If the FCC finds no deviation in Higgs physics, that will still be knowledge, but it should be spoken as knowledge with cost, not as triumph by rebranding. If it finds a deviation, wonderful: the universe will have coughed in the clean room. If the later energy-frontier phase becomes technically, financially, or politically unjustifiable, the courage to stop should be part of the design.

"You are making a collider sound like a moral instrument," Rabi said.

"It is one."

"It is magnets and cavities."

"So was your apparatus. Yet here we are in a hospital tomb arguing about civilization."

He accepted that with a grunt.

The celebration of physics belongs here, before austerity dries the tongue. A Higgs factory is a glorious idea. Electrons and positrons, clean as mutually annihilating punctuation, meeting again and again to produce the particle that tells the Standard Model how to have mass. Detectors built like secular cathedrals around vanishing events. Beams held in choreography by fields. Cryogenics, timing, vacuum, silicon, software, statistics, human patience. The desire to do this is not childish. It is one of the least embarrassing things about our species.

But adulthood begins when desire meets governance.

Rabi's own life refused simple categories. He made exquisite measurements of nuclei. He helped institutionalize big science. He also joined Enrico Fermi in opposing a crash hydrogen-bomb program and defended Oppenheimer when scientific citizenship became politically dangerous ([Ramsey, "I. I. Rabi 1898-1988"](https://www.nasonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/rabi-i-i.pdf)). That is the Rabi worth resurrecting for this argument: not a patron saint of bigger machines, not a scold against ambition, but a physicist who understood that instruments live inside states and states live inside moral weather.

"Do you know what I dislike about your century?" he asked.

"Only one thing?"

"It confuses scale with seriousness."

That was a clean wound.

The FCC's scale proves nothing by itself. A 91-kilometre ring could be a sublime public instrument or a decorated appetite. Fifteen billion CHF could be a wise long investment or a very polite mistake. A public consultation could be democratic substance or a velvet rope around a decision already made. A Higgs factory could sharpen reality or become a machine for converting institutional prestige into beam time.

Physics cannot answer those questions alone. It can answer what precision is possible, what luminosity is needed, what backgrounds matter, what couplings can be measured, what systematic uncertainties must be tamed, what detector geometry will work, what energy reach follows from magnet technology, what the Standard Model predicts. Then politics and ethics must answer whether this is the future we choose to build together.

"And if they say no?" Rabi asked.

"Then physics must survive without treating democracy as vandalism."

"And if they say yes?"

"Then physics must remember that consent is not adoration."

He looked pleased again, which I distrusted but accepted.

Before he returned to death, he asked for the muon.

"Bring me the particle nobody ordered."

I placed it on the imaginary table: electron-like, heavier, absurd, historically inconvenient, eventually indispensable. The muon is an argument against excessive tidiness. Nature is under no obligation to obey our sense of economy. The very particle that once seemed superfluous now threads through precision tests, cosmic-ray history, accelerator dreams, and the vocabulary of possible future colliders. "Who ordered that?" remains funny because the answer is: no one. That is why we had to look.

But machines are not muons. Muons can arrive uninvited. Machines require permission.

That is the hinge. Nature may surprise without consent. Humans may not. The universe can order a muon behind our backs; CERN cannot order a tunnel under people's feet in the same spirit. Curiosity is noble when it faces nature. It becomes arrogance when it forgets society.

Rabi stepped out of the MRI bore. For a moment the room had two machines in it: the small white diagnostic tomb that came from old resonance, and the enormous unbuilt ring that may or may not become the next century's question. He put one hand on each, living and possible.

"Tell them this," he said. "Do not build because you are afraid of stopping. Do not refuse because you are afraid of wonder. Build only if you can say exactly what you are buying with the uncertainty."

"And what are they buying?"

"A sharper ignorance."

That sounded disappointing until it didn't.

Sharper ignorance is one of science's great products. It is ignorance with walls, windows, exclusion limits, calibrated instruments, smaller error bars, dead theories, embarrassed metaphors, and a map of where the next question might hide. It is not as marketable as "unlocking the secrets of the universe," but it is more honest and more beautiful. A civilization that can spend money on sharper ignorance may be healthy. A civilization that cannot explain why may be decadent. The FCC would test both.

The scanner began to cool. The resonance peak thinned. Rabi's outline lost its experimental privilege.

"One last answer," I said. "Would you vote yes?"

He smiled, and this time there was no softness in it.

"I would vote for the argument," he said. "Then I would make the argument deserve the vote."

Then he returned to the tomb with a radio inside it, leaving behind no endorsement, no veto, and no tidy slogan for either side. The MRI bore was empty. The proposed ring remained underground only in drawings. The Higgs remained what it has been since 2012: discovered but not exhausted, a door mistaken by fools for an ending.

I left Rabi with a conditional yes in my pocket and a sharper discomfort beside it. Build the instrument if it can remain an instrument. Build it if its advocates can speak plainly about uncertainty. Build it if the public process has teeth. Build it if the rest of physics is not made to kneel. Build it if the question is worth the tunnel.

Do not build it as proof that physics still matters.

Physics already matters. That is exactly why it must be held to a higher standard than appetite.
