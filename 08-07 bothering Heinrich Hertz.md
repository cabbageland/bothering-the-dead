today is 08-07.

I found Heinrich Hertz in Hamburg's Ohlsdorf Cemetery, where the dead lie in a park so large that even grief has to learn urban planning. Find a Grave lists Heinrich Rudolf Hertz's burial at Friedhof Ohlsdorf in Hamburg; a Wikimedia Commons photograph identifies his tomb there as the grave of the man whose name became the unit for frequency ([Find a Grave, "Heinrich Rudolf Hertz"](https://www.findagrave.com/memorial/22020/heinrich_rudolf-hertz); [Wikimedia Commons, "Hamburg Friedhof Ohlsdorf Heinrich Rudolf Hertz 01"](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Hamburg_Friedhof_Ohlsdorf_Heinrich_Rudolf_Hertz_01.jpg)). It is difficult not to be indecently literal about this. The world counts oscillations in hertz; the man himself has stopped oscillating. Physics, being mercilessly funny, kept his name moving after his body refused.

I brought a brass spark gap, a Leyden jar, a loop of wire, a spectrum chart printed too large for my bag, a battered Wi-Fi router, a toy cell tower, a copy of the Communications Act, a page from the International Telecommunication Union, and one municipal sign reading QUIET ZONE, stolen in imagination from a radio telescope that deserves better neighbors.

Hertz's own collected *Electric Waves* described researches on the propagation of electrical action through space at finite velocity, the experimental heir to Maxwell's prediction that electromagnetic disturbances travel as waves ([Heinrich Hertz, *Electric Waves*, 1893](https://archive.org/details/electricwavesbe00hertgoog); [James Clerk Maxwell, "A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field," 1865](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rstl/article/doi/10.1098/rstl.1865.0008/118816/VIII-A-dynamical-theory-of-the-electromagnetic)). I set the spark gap on his grave and made the smallest rude thunder I could. A blue thread leapt between the knobs. The loop answered with a tiny spark of its own.

There are resurrections by blood, by prayer, by literature, by state paperwork. Hertz deserved resurrection by resonance.

The grave did not split. It tuned. The wet Hamburg air became an instrument. Frequencies arranged themselves like nervous diplomats. The grass around the stone stood up in concentric argument, and then Heinrich Hertz sat upright, thin, young, and severe, with the face of a man who had proved invisible waves existed and then discovered that the future had put advertising on them.

"You are transmitting near a grave," he said.

"I kept the power low."

"Low power is still a claim on the medium."

Good. We were already inside the problem.

Today's question was this: **Should the electromagnetic spectrum be auctioned and enclosed like property, or governed as a public commons whose first obligations are communication, science, safety, and equal access?**

This is not a boutique policy dispute for men with bad lanyards. The electromagnetic spectrum is where phones call ambulances, airplanes avoid each other, ships ask not to drown, satellites stare at the Earth, radio telescopes listen to hydrogen, soldiers coordinate violence, children borrow school from kitchen tables, and ordinary lonely people send "are you awake?" into the night. The FCC says it manages and licenses the electromagnetic spectrum for commercial and non-commercial users, including public safety, satellite, and private radio services ([FCC, "Licensing"](https://www.fcc.gov/licensing)). The ITU's Radio Regulations describe the radio-frequency spectrum and satellite orbits as resources requiring rational, efficient, equitable use and protection against harmful interference ([ITU, *Radio Regulations 2020*](https://www.itu.int/hub/publication/r-reg-rr-2020/)). Already the metaphysics is crowded. The spectrum is physics, infrastructure, market, law, emergency, empire, and lullaby.

Hertz looked at the Wi-Fi router as if it were an undignified insect.

"You ask whether waves can be owned," he said.

"No. I ask whether the right to shout into them should be sold to the richest throat."

"That is not the same question."

"Exactly. This is why I woke you."

The pro-auction argument deserves its strongest coat before we burn holes in it. Radio use creates interference. Two transmitters using the same frequency, power, place, and time can make each other unintelligible. Spectrum is not land, but neither is it an infinite meadow where every shepherd may politely graze a waveform. Someone has to coordinate. Markets are one way to make coordination less stupid. The FCC says auctions assign licenses more efficiently than comparative hearings or lotteries ([FCC, "About Auctions"](https://www.fcc.gov/about-auctions)). Section 309(j) of the Communications Act gives the Commission competitive-bidding authority for certain licenses and tells it to consider public-interest objectives as well as auction design ([47 U.S.C. Section 309](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/309)). Auction 107, the C-band auction for 3.7 GHz service, produced more than $81 billion in gross bids for 5G-relevant mid-band licenses ([FCC, "Auction 107: 3.7 GHz Service"](https://www.fcc.gov/auction/107)).

"You see?" Hertz said. "Scarcity. Coordination. Cost. A signal is not improved by moral perfume."

I admitted the case. Administrative beauty contests can become theatrical corruption: regulators guessing the public interest while applicants perform virtue in binders. Lotteries can hand public capacity to lucky incompetents, who then sell the privilege downstream. Auctions, at their best, make bidders reveal how much they value a license, raise public revenue, and give operators enough certainty to invest in towers, handsets, standards, deployment, and maintenance. Markets are not automatically evil; Stanford's entry on markets notes that defenses of markets can be consequentialist, treating exchange as a mechanism for coordinating dispersed choices and information ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Markets"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/markets/)).

"There," Hertz said. "You have answered yourself."

"I have answered the easy version."

He frowned. "There is an easy version?"

"The easy version says the spectrum should be either pure property or pure commons. The real spectrum laughs at both."

The first lie in spectrum policy is the possessive noun. The airwaves are "owned" by the public, we say, or "sold" to carriers, or "held" by agencies. But the legal structure itself is more anxious. Section 301 says the United States maintains control over radio channels and provides for their use, not ownership, by limited-period federal licenses ([47 U.S.C. Section 301](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&path=%2Fprelim%40title47%2Fchapter5%2Fsubchapter3)). Section 151 says the purpose of federal communications regulation is to make wire and radio communication available to all people of the United States, with adequate facilities, reasonable charges, national defense, and safety of life and property among its aims ([47 U.S.C. Section 151](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/151)). This is not the grammar of simple ownership. It is the grammar of delegated privilege under public purpose.

"Legal metaphors," Hertz said. "The wave does not read statutes."

"No. But the tower does."

That was the first little wound.

Property is not a natural glow around an object. It is a rule-system for access, control, exclusion, transfer, and liability; Stanford's entry on property describes property rules as social arrangements governing access to and control over resources ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Property and Ownership"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/property/)). That matters because the electromagnetic spectrum is especially bad at being a thing. You cannot fence 700 MHz. You cannot paint your name on 3.7 GHz. You can only create enforceable expectations about who may radiate, at what power, in what geography, under what conditions, with what duty not to ruin neighboring uses.

Hertz touched the loop of wire and made a spark jump without moving his hand.

"Then property is merely a clean word for disciplined expectation," he said.

"Sometimes. Sometimes it is a dirty word for disciplined exclusion."

"And commons?"

"Sometimes a miracle of shared governance. Sometimes a landfill with slogans."

The commons side has its own childish version, and it must also be slapped. A commons is not everyone transmitting anything anywhere at full power because the soul yearns for bandwidth. That is not freedom. That is electromagnetic mud. Garrett Hardin's famous "tragedy of the commons" framed unmanaged shared resources as vulnerable to ruin by individually rational use, although the later commons literature rightly objected that "unmanaged" was doing a great deal of work ([Garrett Hardin, "The Tragedy of the Commons," 1968](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.162.3859.1243); [Elinor Ostrom, *Governing the Commons*](https://archive.org/details/governing-the-commons)). Ostrom's achievement was not to chant "commons good." It was to show that durable commons require boundaries, rules, monitoring, sanctions, conflict resolution, and nested governance. In other words, a commons is not the absence of discipline. It is discipline without pretending exclusion is the whole of wisdom.

"You have made your commons sound bureaucratic," Hertz said.

"Good. A non-bureaucratic spectrum commons is just screaming with footnotes."

He gave me the first dead smile of the evening.

Unlicensed spectrum is the best answer to people who think commons means chaos. The FCC's Part 15 rules allow many radio-frequency devices to operate without individual licenses, but only under technical limits, including the condition that devices not cause harmful interference and must accept interference under the rules ([47 C.F.R. Part 15](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-47/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-15); [FCC Order FCC 02-151](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/FCC-02-151A1.pdf)). The famous Wi-Fi bargain is not "no law." It is "low power, technical standards, shared bands, no exclusive lord." In 2020, the FCC opened 1,200 megahertz of the 6 GHz band for unlicensed use, explicitly expanding spectrum available for Wi-Fi and other unlicensed operations while setting protections for incumbent services ([FCC, 6 GHz news release](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/doc-363945a1.pdf); [FCC, "FCC Opens 6 GHz Band to Wi-Fi and Other Unlicensed Uses"](https://www.fcc.gov/document/fcc-opens-6-ghz-band-wi-fi-and-other-unlicensed-uses)).

"A clever compromise," Hertz said.

"More than clever. Philosophically humiliating."

"To whom?"

"To everyone with a grand theory."

Licensed carriers need protected capacity for wide-area mobile networks. Emergency systems need reliability. Broadcasters, satellite operators, utilities, railroads, aviation, maritime users, scientists, hobbyists, hospitals, and weird little garage inventions all need different interference envelopes. The spectrum is not one resource but a stack of incompatible needs riding the same physical continuum. A policy that treats every band as auctionable real estate is as stupid as a policy that treats every band as a drum circle. Physics refuses ideological tidiness.

Hertz nodded, pleased by the refusal if not by my language.

"But the auction," he said, "at least punishes waste. If a company pays billions, it has reason to use the license."

"Or to warehouse it, shape the market, block rivals, or demand regulatory sympathy because it has invested too much to fail."

"Then impose buildout duties."

"Yes. Now you are no longer defending property. You are defending conditional public stewardship with invoices."

That was the second wound, and this one annoyed him.

The auction price measures willingness and ability to pay. It does not measure the value of a school district's emergency radios, a rural tribe's backhaul, a radio astronomer's quiet hydrogen line, a public-interest mesh network, or a low-margin technology that becomes socially transformative only after millions of devices start using it cheaply. Locke's famous account of appropriation ties property to labor while retaining limits such as leaving enough and as good for others ([John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government*, Chapter 5](https://press-pubs.uchicago.edu/founders/documents/v1ch16s3.html)). Spectrum mocks that proviso. If a nationwide carrier occupies a valuable band under exclusive license, what is "enough and as good" for the public safety user, the small competitor, the unlicensed inventor, the radio telescope, or the village that is not profitable enough to matter on the spreadsheet?

Hertz stood and looked across the cemetery, as if he could see towers rising from every grave.

"You are smuggling equality into physics," he said.

"No. Physics smuggled interference into politics."

He became still.

"Explain."

"If my use can make your use impossible, then we are already in each other's business. The moral question is not whether politics enters the wave. The wave has already entered politics."

That was the first clean hit.

The market reply is not dead. It says: yes, public values matter, but without prices and exclusion, nobody invests enough. Cellular networks are capital-intensive, standards-dependent, and geographically hard. A spectrum license gives a carrier confidence that some neighborhood anarchist with a soldering iron cannot flatten its service. Revenue from auctions can return value to the public. The C-band auction's $81 billion in gross bids is not a philosophical abstraction; it is an enormous revealed demand for mid-band capacity in a country that wants fast mobile data ([FCC Auction 107](https://www.fcc.gov/auction/107)). If we force every use into polite sharing, we may produce congestion, uncertainty, and underinvestment. The public may then receive the moral satisfaction of openness and the practical experience of dropped calls.

"You dislike dropped calls," Hertz observed.

"With spiritual intensity."

"Then do not sentimentalize openness."

I accepted that too. A philosophy that cannot handle engineering should not be allowed near antenna permits. Exclusive licenses are sometimes justified because high-power, wide-area networks need protection from interference and predictable access to channels. Aviation and maritime distress systems need rules sterner than hippie acoustics. Radio astronomy needs protection because the universe whispers in frequencies human commerce would happily pave. The National Academies' handbook on spectrum protection notes how radio astronomy has to manage harmful interference thresholds, including ITU recommendations for protecting observing bands from excessive interference over time ([National Academies, *Handbook of Frequency Allocations and Spectrum Protection for Scientific Uses*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/11719/chapter/6)). You do not build a serious civilization by letting everything talk over everything else.

But you also do not build one by auctioning every silence.

Radio astronomy is the ethical embarrassment hidden inside spectrum economics. Its value is enormous and almost impossible to monetize properly. Hydrogen does not bid. Pulsars do not hire lobbyists. The cosmic microwave background cannot promise shareholder return. Yet science needs quiet bands and protected windows because knowledge of the universe is part of the human estate. The American Astronomical Society warns that unwanted emissions from satellites and nearby bands can disrupt radio astronomical observations, especially where protections fail to account for aggregate interference ([AAS Resolution on Radio-Frequency Interference](https://aas.org/about/governance/society-resolutions/RFI)). If every valuable frequency is judged by auction revenue, then the oldest light in the universe loses to a quarterly earnings call.

Hertz looked almost offended on behalf of the universe.

"A receiver is an ethical object," he said.

"Yes."

"Because it proves that listening is not passive."

"Yes."

"Then your policy must protect listening."

That one belonged to him.

The spectrum debate becomes clearer if we stop asking whether the airwaves are property or commons and ask what kinds of silence each institution creates. Exclusive licensing creates operational silence around a licensee: others must stay out so the licensed service can work. Unlicensed rules create a softer silence: devices stay low, polite, standardized, and tolerant enough that many can coexist. Scientific allocations create contemplative silence: keep the human racket down so instruments can hear things no company invented. Emergency allocations create moral silence: do not make the distress call compete with a movie download. Military allocations create secret silence, which may be necessary in places and poisonous in others. Each silence has a politics.

"You are fond of silence for someone making sparks on my grave," Hertz said.

"I contain multitudes and a small violation."

"At least you confess."

Here is where I began to mistrust my own side. The rhetoric of the public commons often floats above maintenance. Who pays for coordination databases? Who certifies devices? Who updates power rules when technology changes? Who resolves border disputes? Who protects incumbents without freezing incumbency forever? Who makes sure low-power freedom does not become high-power capture by whoever ships the most hardware? Even permissionless innovation needs permission to be protected from ruin. A Wi-Fi router is a little miracle because thousands of engineers, regulators, standards bodies, chip designers, and boring committees made it boring enough to work.

Hertz held up the router.

"This thing is ugly."

"Almost all useful miracles are."

"It is also disciplined."

"Exactly."

The pro-property camp has its own maintenance problem. A spectrum license can become a private entitlement in public clothing. If licenses are renewed automatically, traded like assets, defended by armies of lawyers, and evaluated mainly by incumbent investment, the public purpose thins into a decorative clause. Section 301's insistence on use rather than ownership is supposed to matter ([47 U.S.C. Section 301](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&path=%2Fprelim%40title47%2Fchapter5%2Fsubchapter3)). A license should be a tool, not a hereditary title with better acronyms. If the public grants protected access to a band, it may demand buildout, interoperability, emergency obligations, rural service, anti-hoarding rules, secondary markets that do not sabotage competition, and expiry dates that mean something.

"You want markets on a leash," Hertz said.

"I want physics on a constitution."

"Constitutions are leashes for power."

"Then yes."

He did not object.

There is another ugly stake: global inequality. The ITU framework matters because radio frequencies and satellite orbits cross borders whether or not national politicians have finished their speeches. Article 44's language about rational, efficient, economical use and equitable access appears because the spectrum/orbit environment can be captured early by richer, faster, louder actors ([ITU Radio Regulations 2020](https://www.itu.int/hub/publication/r-reg-rr-2020/)). Low Earth orbit megaconstellations, geostationary slots, earth-station gateways, satellite broadband, military systems, meteorological satellites, and remote sensing all turn the sky into governance. A poor country arriving late should not discover that the heavens have already been reserved by wealthy paperwork.

"The finite velocity of propagation was not enough," Hertz said. "You have made even arrival time political."

"It was already political. We just added satellites."

"You are proud of very little in your century."

"I am proud of some instruments. Less proud of the billing."

The dead physicist looked at me as though that might be the most accurate epitaph for modernity.

To celebrate physics honestly is to love the thing that ruins sloppy ownership metaphors. Electromagnetic waves do not behave like chairs. They diffract, reflect, attenuate, interfere, propagate, scatter, polarize, and carry information without asking whether a senator understands Maxwell. The BIPM's SI brochure treats hertz as the SI unit of frequency, a reciprocal-second measure for periodic phenomena ([BIPM, *The International System of Units*, 9th edition](https://www.bipm.org/documents/20126/41483022/SI-Brochure-9-EN.pdf)). One hertz is not an estate. One gigahertz is not a kingdom. Frequency is relation, repetition, time wearing a pattern. The tragedy is that human beings meet relation and immediately ask whether it can be enclosed.

Hertz became sharper.

"Careful. Without enclosure there may be no relation, only collision."

"Yes. But enclosure should serve relation, not replace it."

"Then say the policy plainly."

Fine.

The electromagnetic spectrum should be governed as a public trust with plural operating regimes. Exclusive licenses and auctions are justified where interference protection, investment, and wide-area service genuinely require them. But auction revenue should never be mistaken for social value, and high bids should never become moral proof. Licenses should be conditional, time-limited, use-it-or-share-it, technically reviewable, and tied to public obligations. Large unlicensed bands should be protected and expanded where low-power sharing can support innovation, household connectivity, community networks, schools, devices, and experiments without asking permission from a carrier first. Public safety, aviation, maritime distress, scientific observation, meteorology, radio astronomy, and rural access should not have to defeat consumer entertainment at auction to deserve spectrum. International governance should treat equitable access as a design constraint, not a polite adjective after the powerful finish eating.

Hertz listened like an apparatus.

"So not private property," he said.

"Not mainly."

"Not open commons."

"Not stupidly."

"A managed public medium."

"A constitutional ether, if we want the phrase to sound haunted."

"Ether is obsolete."

"The ghost of it is useful."

He looked irritated, which I chose to interpret as affection from a dead experimentalist.

There remains one objection I cannot dismiss. Public trust language can become an alibi for state control. A government that says "the spectrum belongs to the people" may censor broadcasters, favor allies, militarize silence, or call dissent interference. Markets are not the only tyrants with antennas. State allocation can become patronage; public-interest licensing can become moral theater; national-security claims can swallow public scrutiny. The strongest defense of auctions is not that money is holy. It is that money can sometimes make power legible. A bid is crude, but visible. A backroom allocation can be worse.

Hertz seized that.

"Then why prefer your trust?"

"Because the cure for corrupt public power is accountable public power, not pretending a public medium becomes apolitical when sold."

"That is assertion."

"No. It is also physics. Interference never disappears. It only moves. Markets move it into price and exclusion. Commons move it into rules and etiquette. States move it into authority. The honest policy says where the interference went."

He considered this for a long time. A few sparks moved silently between the grass blades.

"When I made my waves," he said, "I wanted to know if Maxwell's field had reality. I did not ask who would own the field."

"That may be why you are still useful."

"Because I was innocent?"

"No. Because you were precise. Innocence is overrated in the dead."

Hertz laughed once, quietly enough not to disturb the licensed users.

The answer, then, is not anti-market and not anti-commons. It is anti-idolatry. Treating spectrum as private property idolizes exclusion. Treating it as open commons idolizes access. Treating it as state command idolizes authority. The physical fact underneath all three is shared vulnerability to interference. The moral fact is that communication is now one of the basic ways human beings participate in society. The legal fact, at least in the United States, is that radio licenses are not ownership and communication policy is supposed to serve all people, safety, national needs, and reasonable access ([47 U.S.C. Section 151](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/47/151); [47 U.S.C. Section 301](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&path=%2Fprelim%40title47%2Fchapter5%2Fsubchapter3)). The philosophical fact is that no single property metaphor can bear this much weather.

Hertz began sinking back toward death. I had the rude urge to ask him one more thing, because the living are addicted to closure.

"Does this celebrate physics?" I asked.

He looked at the spark gap, then at the router, then upward into the polluted choir of transmissions moving through the cemetery: aircraft, phones, satellites, clocks, weather, police, bad music, good science, scams, prayers, coordinates, advertisements, warnings, loneliness.

"Yes," he said. "It celebrates physics by admitting that discovery is not possession. A wave is not yours because you can generate it. It becomes yours only in the instant you are responsible for what it disturbs."

Then he lay back down, and the world returned to its managed noise.

I left the QUIET ZONE sign at the grave. Not as nostalgia for silence. Silence by itself is not virtue. It can be exclusion, censorship, neglect, or death. I left it there because every working spectrum policy begins with one disciplined recognition: before anyone can speak, someone must decide what must not be drowned out.

Today is for bothering Heinrich Hertz and refusing to sell the entire invisible commons to the loudest future. Today is for the spark that proves the field is real, the receiver that proves listening is labor, and the law humble enough to remember that the public does not own the waves like furniture. We inherit them as a shared condition of hearing each other across distance. That is stranger, harder, and more beautiful than property.
