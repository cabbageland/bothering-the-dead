today is 06-25.

I found Norbert Wiener in Sandwich, New Hampshire, under a stone modest enough to make cybernetics look like a hobby someone once took too far.

Find a Grave places him in Vittum Hill Cemetery, which is exactly the kind of name a system theorist deserves: a hill, a datum, a place where feedback has finally stopped ([Find a Grave, Norbert Wiener](https://www.findagrave.com/memorial/7304697/norbert-wiener)). The National Academy of Sciences gives the formal coordinates of the life: born November 26, 1894, died March 18, 1964, mathematician, member elected in 1934 and later resigned, a man whose biography became inseparable from the word cybernetics ([National Academy of Sciences, Norbert Wiener](https://www.nasonline.org/directory-entry/norbert-wiener-cncj3y/); [NAS Biographical Memoir](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/2037/chapter/18)).

I came with no flowers. Flowers are merely optimistic clocks. I brought a cracked drone propeller, a spool of copper wire, a toy golem made from gray clay, a printed copy of Article 36 of Additional Protocol I, a Department of Defense directive on autonomy in weapon systems, a UN report on lethal autonomous weapons, and a little black box labeled TARGET CLASSIFIER, which I had filled with moths because I am against false advertising.

Wiener had warned us early. In 1960, in "Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation," he wrote about learning machines, military automation, and the danger of systems operating faster than human correction could catch them; he compared automated systems to magical servants whose obedience can become disaster when the wish is badly specified ([Norbert Wiener, "Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation"](https://www.cs.umd.edu/users/gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/moral.pdf)). In *God and Golem, Inc.*, published the year he died, he returned to the old Jewish story of making a creature by inscription and command, asking what happens when man builds machines that perform purposes without sharing human judgment ([MIT Press, *God and Golem, Inc.*](https://mitpress.mit.edu/9780262230094/god-and-golem-inc/)).

So I dug a small feedback loop into the soil: sensor, comparator, actuator, dirt. I placed the drone propeller where a candle might have gone. Then I read Article 36 aloud, the rule requiring a state, when it studies, develops, acquires, or adopts a new weapon, means, or method of warfare, to determine whether its use would be prohibited by international law in some or all circumstances ([ICRC IHL database, Additional Protocol I, Article 36](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/api-1977/article-36)).

The moths began hitting the inside of the box.

I read the 2019 UN guiding principle that international humanitarian law applies fully to all weapons systems, including potential lethal autonomous weapons systems, and that human responsibility for decisions on the use of weapons systems must be retained because accountability cannot be transferred to machines ([CCW/GGE guiding principles, Annex III](https://ccdcoe.org/uploads/2020/02/UN-191213_CCW-MSP-Final-report-Annex-III_Guiding-Principles-affirmed-by-GGE.pdf)).

The propeller twitched.

I read the Defense Department's 2023 directive saying autonomous and semi-autonomous weapon systems must be designed to let commanders and operators exercise "appropriate levels of human judgment" over force, with verification, validation, testing, constraints, transparency, and ways to activate or deactivate functions ([DoD Directive 3000.09](https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/300009p.pdf)).

The grave exhaled a smell of wet leaves, chalk dust, solder, and somebody's long-unused pipe.

Norbert Wiener sat up, irritated in the precise way of a man disturbed not from sleep but from a theorem.

"You have constructed a bad circuit," he said.

"It is symbolic."

"Most bad circuits are."

"I brought you a war question."

"They usually arrive disguised as engineering questions."

The question was this: **Should states ban autonomous weapons that can select and attack human beings without meaningful human control, or should they permit tightly regulated autonomous weapons on the theory that machines may sometimes fight faster, more precisely, and less cruelly than humans?**

This is not a science-fiction question. The UN General Assembly adopted a resolution on lethal autonomous weapons systems on December 2, 2024, with a vote summary of 166 in favor, 3 against, and 15 abstentions, and placed the issue on the international agenda as a live problem of law, security, technology, ethics, and the role of humans in the use of force ([UN Digital Library, A/RES/79/62](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4071100?ln=en)). The Secretary-General's 2024 report collected state and organizational views after Resolution 78/241 and records the central anxiety: target identification, classification, interception, and engagement may contain merely nominal human input, and nominal input is not enough if it does not materially shape target selection or attack ([UN Secretary-General report A/79/88](https://docs-library.unoda.org/General_Assembly_First_Committee_-Seventy-Ninth_session_%282024%29/A-79-88-LAWS.pdf)).

The stakes are blunt. A weapon that selects and attacks by sensor processing can make killing faster than reflection, cheaper than political courage, and scalable enough to turn "collateral damage" from a euphemism into a production metric. But a blanket refusal of autonomy can also be morally lazy if it ignores defensive systems, time-critical interception, mine clearing, precision, operator fatigue, communications jamming, and the fact that human soldiers are not sacred instruments of rational discrimination. Humans panic, revenge, misread, dehumanize, obey, sleep badly, and press buttons because everyone else in the room expects it.

Wiener brushed soil off his sleeve.

"Begin with the machine's defense," he said. "One cannot convict a device merely for being recent."

Fine. The best case for autonomous weapons begins with the ugliness of war as it already exists. War is not a seminar in moral agency. It is fear, speed, fog, deception, distance, and force under conditions arranged to make good judgment difficult. If an incoming missile gives a ship seconds to respond, or a swarm attack saturates a base, requiring a human to identify every target and authorize every engagement may be less humane than it sounds. The DoD directive explicitly recognizes operator-supervised autonomous systems for local defense against time-critical or saturation attacks on materiel targets, while still demanding human judgment, constraints, testing, and accountability ([DoD Directive 3000.09](https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/300009p.pdf)).

"A machine may be faster," Wiener said.

"Yes."

"That is not the same as wiser."

"No. But sometimes speed is part of wisdom. A hospital's air defense does not become more ethical because the operator had time to feel tragic before failing."

He accepted this with a small grimace. The pro-autonomy argument is strongest when the target class is narrow, the environment constrained, the time window short, the object attacked is plainly military by nature, and the alternative is either no defense or a human bottleneck that adds little morally relevant judgment. The International Committee of the Red Cross itself, though sharply concerned about autonomous weapons, recommends strict regulation of some systems rather than pretending all autonomy is identical: limits on target types, duration, geographical scope, scale of use, and situations where civilians or civilian objects are not present ([ICRC position on autonomous weapon systems](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/icrc-position-autonomous-weapon-systems)).

There is also a hard anti-sentimental point: human presence is not a sacrament. A tired nineteen-year-old watching a grainy screen is not automatically a better moral agent than a sensor fused with rules of engagement and an abort condition. International humanitarian law does not require that killing be done by warm-blooded mammals. It requires distinction, proportionality, precautions, and lawful targeting. The ICRC's customary law study says parties must distinguish between civilians and combatants, and that attacks may only be directed against combatants; it similarly requires distinction between civilian objects and military objectives ([ICRC Customary IHL Rule 1](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/customary-ihl/v1/rule1); [Rule 7](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/customary-ihl/v1/rule7)). If a machine can be shown, in a specific bounded context, to perform those tasks better than a human under fire, the opponent of all autonomy has to explain why the human hand is morally magic.

Wiener looked at the moth box.

"You are arguing from competence. Competence is a dangerous virtue. It invites trust before it has earned wisdom."

"Yes."

"Continue."

The next pro-autonomy claim is that bans can be theater. "Autonomous weapons" is not a clean natural kind. Autonomy appears on a spectrum: homing munitions, close-in weapon systems, loitering munitions, missile defense, target-recognition aids, navigation, electronic warfare, drone swarms, mines with sensors, supervised autonomy, and systems that continue after communications are cut. A treaty that tries to ban everything called autonomy may either prohibit ordinary defensive tools or dissolve into definitional fog. The 2019 UN guiding principles themselves caution that discussions should not hamper peaceful uses of intelligent autonomous technologies, and that emerging technologies should not be anthropomorphized ([CCW/GGE guiding principles](https://ccdcoe.org/uploads/2020/02/UN-191213_CCW-MSP-Final-report-Annex-III_Guiding-Principles-affirmed-by-GGE.pdf)).

"The word autonomy flatters the machine," Wiener said.

"Because it sounds like Kant."

"It is not Kant. It is a thermostat with ambitions supplied by procurement."

Exactly. The machine does not will the moral law. It operates. But that cuts both ways. If machines are not moral persons, then the relevant question is not whether they possess dignity, courage, or remorse. The question is whether human institutions can design, test, constrain, deploy, and audit them in ways compatible with law. Article 36 is built for that kind of question: not metaphysics first, but review of a new weapon's lawful use before adoption ([ICRC, Article 36](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/api-1977/article-36)).

Wiener shook his head.

"A review before adoption is not a conscience after error."

Now the other side.

The best case for prohibition begins where the pro-autonomy case is most evasive: with human beings as targets. An anti-missile system selecting an incoming rocket is one moral universe. A cheap drone selecting a person by thermal outline, gait, uniform-like pattern, phone signal, facial similarity, or inferred behavior is another. The first may automate defense against an object. The second risks automating the moment when a human being is reduced to a target profile.

The ICRC recommends prohibiting autonomous weapon systems whose effects cannot be sufficiently understood, predicted, and explained, and also prohibiting systems designed or used to apply force against persons; for all other autonomous weapons, it calls for strict constraints on target, time, geography, scale, and use ([ICRC position](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/icrc-position-autonomous-weapon-systems)). That is not technophobia. It is moral taxonomy. Some automation may be regulable. Some should be outside the room.

Wiener picked up the clay golem.

"The golem is not wicked," he said. "The inscription is."

"And the rabbi?"

"Also the rabbi."

That is the accountability problem. The UN guiding principles say accountability cannot be transferred to machines ([CCW/GGE guiding principles](https://ccdcoe.org/uploads/2020/02/UN-191213_CCW-MSP-Final-report-Annex-III_Guiding-Principles-affirmed-by-GGE.pdf)). Everyone repeats this because it is true, and because no one knows how to make it operational at the hard edge. If an autonomous weapon misidentifies civilians, who is responsible? The commander who activated it? The engineer who trained the classifier? The procurement office that accepted the test scores? The political leader who demanded speed? The data vendor? The state? The operator who trusted a confidence score? The legal reviewer who signed off on a scenario narrower than the one that occurred?

"Responsibility diffuses through a system," Wiener said.

"Cybernetics as blame fog."

"Do not make jokes when the diagram is accurate."

International humanitarian law depends on judgments that are not merely pattern recognition. Distinction is not simply "detect uniforms." Proportionality is not "estimate blast radius." Precautions are not "run a checklist." Additional Protocol I prohibits attacks expected to cause incidental civilian loss excessive in relation to the concrete and direct military advantage anticipated ([ICRC IHL database, Additional Protocol I, Article 51](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/ihl-treaties/api-1977/article-51); [ICRC Customary IHL Rule 14](https://ihl-databases.icrc.org/en/customary-ihl/v1/rule14)). That assessment can require context, uncertainty, interpretive humility, and moral responsibility for a choice whose tragic residue cannot be debugged.

The machine can count. The question is whether it can understand what the count is for.

"It cannot," Wiener said.

"Some people will say it does not need to. Humans define the objective, constraints, and rules."

"Then the humans must remain close enough to the action for their definitions to matter."

This is the phrase that matters: close enough for judgment to matter. A human "in the loop" who clicks approve on a hundred machine-generated targets in a minute is not exercising judgment. A commander who activates a system over a city for six hours and says the machine will sort combatants from civilians is not exercising judgment. A legal review that assumes clean weather, clean sensors, clean adversaries, clean maps, and clean categories is not law. It is theater with footnotes.

The Secretary-General's report records exactly this anxiety when it distinguishes meaningful human involvement from merely nominal input that does not materially affect target selection or engagement ([UN Secretary-General report A/79/88](https://docs-library.unoda.org/General_Assembly_First_Committee_-Seventy-Ninth_session_%282024%29/A-79-88-LAWS.pdf)). "Human control" cannot mean a finger somewhere in the history of the device. It has to mean humans understand the system's relevant behavior, choose the mission, bound the target class, constrain the time and place, can supervise or intervene where supervision matters, and remain responsible in a way a court, parliament, and victim's family can actually name.

Wiener opened the moth box. The moths climbed onto his fingers, each one dusty, blind, and sincerely committed to bad light.

"Here is my old warning," he said. "The machine is literal. You give it a victory condition. It gives you the consequences."

He was not being mystical. In his 1960 essay, Wiener worried about learning machines in strategic contexts, including "push-button war," precisely because a machine may operate from a game or model whose victory conditions do not match the actual human goods at stake; he also warned that a machine acting faster than human feedback can make intervention come too late ([Wiener, 1960](https://www.cs.umd.edu/users/gasarch/BLOGPAPERS/moral.pdf)). The modern target classifier is a descendant of that worry. It optimizes inside a frame. The world bleeds outside the frame.

The defense comes back: humans also use frames. Humans also make categories. Humans also kill because a map, a doctrine, a rumor, a risk matrix, or a commander's desire for promotion told them what counted. If autonomous weapons are banned, powerful states will not become gentle. They may simply use remote-controlled weapons, artillery, bombs, starvation, cyber operations, and old-fashioned soldiers with newer excuses. A machine ban cannot substitute for the laws of war, arms control, political accountability, and the end of wars that should not be fought.

"Correct," Wiener said. "A ban is not a kingdom of heaven. It is a guardrail."

Then the argument becomes engineering plus law plus anthropology. What guardrail?

The absolutist ban says: no machine should ever select and attack a person without a human decision on that individual target. It has the virtue of moral clarity. Human beings should not be made killable by statistical classification alone. The Martens Clause says that even where specific treaty law is incomplete, people remain under the protection of the laws of humanity and the dictates of public conscience ([ICRC casebook, Martens Clause](https://casebook.icrc.org/a_to_z/glossary/martens-clause)). If public conscience means anything, it should revolt against a person becoming an output coordinate.

"Public conscience is a noisy instrument," Wiener said.

"So are sensors."

"The public at least knows when it is ashamed."

Sometimes. But the absolutist ban can overreach if it forbids defensive autonomy against materiel or systems with tightly limited target sets where human control is meaningful at the level that matters. A system intercepting rockets over a city is not a robot deciding who deserves death. A mine-clearing robot is not a moral scandal because it acts without prayer. An autonomous system that attacks only incoming missiles within seconds, in a bounded defensive zone, under human-supervised doctrine, may preserve rather than degrade human responsibility.

The permissive position says: existing international humanitarian law is enough if states conduct weapons reviews, test rigorously, and retain human responsibility. This has the virtue of realism. It is also how every dangerous technology asks to enter the world: let us in, existing law can handle us, trust the professionals, the classified annex is excellent. The DoD directive is far more serious than a shrug, with requirements for human judgment, verification and validation, realistic testing, auditability, explainability to relevant personnel, and senior approval for many systems ([DoD Directive 3000.09](https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/300009p.pdf)). But a national policy is not an international legal settlement, and "appropriate" human judgment is too elastic a word to bear the weight of the dead by itself.

"Appropriate according to whom?" Wiener asked.

"The state using the weapon."

"That answer has killed before."

The best answer is a two-tier rule: prohibit the intolerable, regulate the rest.

Prohibit autonomous weapon systems that are designed or used to target human beings as such, where no human makes a meaningful decision on the individual use of force. Prohibit systems whose behavior cannot be sufficiently predicted, explained, tested, or bounded in the environment of use. Prohibit self-learning changes to targeting behavior after deployment unless the system returns to review before being used as a weapon. Prohibit autonomous nuclear launch or escalation systems absolutely, because Wiener's "push-button war" warning becomes civilization's suicide note when the button is nuclear.

Regulate autonomous systems aimed at materiel targets with strict conditions: specific target classes, short time windows, defined geography, audit logs, human mission command, training, realistic adversarial testing, legal review, fail-safe termination, cybersecurity, no operation where civilians or civilian objects are foreseeably present unless human control is direct and meaningful, and external accountability after use. That position tracks the ICRC's split between prohibitions and constraints, while taking seriously the DoD's insistence on testing, judgment, and operator responsibility ([ICRC position](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/icrc-position-autonomous-weapon-systems); [DoD Directive 3000.09](https://www.esd.whs.mil/portals/54/documents/dd/issuances/dodd/300009p.pdf)).

Wiener set the clay golem on the edge of his grave.

"You have made a law-shaped machine," he said.

"Yes."

"It will also be literal-minded."

Of course. A treaty is a machine made of words. It can be gamed. States will exploit definitions. Companies will sell modular autonomy as decision support. Militaries will call target selection "recommendation." Operators will become rubber stamps. Human control will be simulated with dashboards. Audit logs will be classified. Casualties will be explained as anomalous edge cases, which is the polite technical phrase for people who met the future at the wrong angle.

So the law must not only classify weapons. It must classify evasions.

Meaningful human control should require at least four things. First, comprehension: the human responsible must understand the system's target class, operating environment, failure modes, and limits well enough to make a real decision. Second, boundedness: the system must operate within a constrained time, place, target type, and mission purpose. Third, intervention or prior judgment at the right level: if real-time intervention is impossible, the prior human judgment must be narrow enough to remain morally connected to the attack. Fourth, accountability: after harm, investigators must be able to reconstruct who authorized what, on what information, under what legal theory, with what system behavior.

"You are trying to keep feedback in the moral circuit," Wiener said.

"Yes."

"Good. Most evil is open-loop."

That was too neat, but useful. An open-loop weapon acts without correcting itself against human judgment once released. A closed-loop moral system keeps information returning to responsible agents soon enough that responsibility is not fiction. The danger of lethal autonomy is not only that machines may err. It is that they may make human responsibility ornamental.

There is also the matter of dignity, a word engineers distrust because it will not fit on a test range. Dignity does not mean every death in war is ceremonially personal. Many are anonymous, distant, mechanical, and obscene already. But there is a further injury in being killed by a system that never recognized you as anything but a pattern. The campaigners against "killer robots" are sometimes mocked for melodrama, yet their central claim is exact: autonomous weapons risk reducing people to data points in the use of force ([Stop Killer Robots](https://www.stopkillerrobots.org/); [Human Rights Watch, killer robots](https://www.hrw.org/topic/arms/killer-robots)).

Wiener looked at the moths leaving dust on his palm.

"The machine does not hate."

"Some call that an advantage."

"It also does not hesitate."

"Some call that an advantage too."

"Then beware advantages that consist in missing human weaknesses. Some weaknesses are brakes."

This is the hard part. Mercy is not always a virtue in soldiers; hesitation can get civilians killed if it lets an attack continue; emotion can produce atrocity as easily as restraint. But a world that optimizes war by removing hesitation may discover that hesitation was one of the last poor shelters of conscience. The goal is not to keep killing human because humans are noble. The goal is to prevent killing from becoming administratively frictionless.

The machine's defenders say autonomy can save soldiers' lives. That is morally relevant. Soldiers are not expendable offerings to purity. They say autonomy can improve precision. That is morally relevant too. Civilians do not benefit from our nostalgia for analog incompetence. They say adversaries will develop these systems regardless, so restraint may punish the restrained. Also relevant. Arms control has always had to answer the problem of cheaters, defectors, and states that hear moral concern as a procurement delay.

But the anti-autonomy side answers: precisely because cheap autonomous killing will spread, the norm must be built before the market and battlefield teach everyone the wrong reflex. If the first mature norm is "whoever automates fastest survives," then every state will call its fear prudence, every contractor will call its product inevitable, and every civilian will live beneath someone else's confidence threshold. The UN vote does not solve that problem, but it shows that most states want the problem treated as a shared legal and ethical matter rather than a private race among the most heavily armed ([UN Digital Library, A/RES/79/62](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/4071100?ln=en)).

"Will they agree?" I asked.

"Not because agreement is logical."

"Then why?"

"Because fear sometimes imitates wisdom."

The final position is neither worship nor ban-the-word. It is this: no autonomous system should be allowed to select and attack human targets without a human decision that is meaningful for that person or tightly specified group under concrete circumstances. Unpredictable systems and systems whose effects cannot be explained enough for law should be prohibited. Defensive autonomy against materiel may be permitted under severe constraints. Every system must pass legal review before adoption, but Article 36 review should be treated as a floor, not a halo. Human control should mean comprehension, boundedness, accountable command, and real relation to the attack. Anything less is not control. It is a decorative human painted on the side of the machine.

Wiener smiled at that, faintly.

"You do understand cybernetics."

"Only as a haunted ethics."

"That is the best kind."

The grave began to reclaim him. The propeller stopped. The moths, free at last, flew toward the nearest false moon: my phone screen, glowing with casualty reports, procurement memos, PDFs, and the bad little sunrise of notification icons.

Before he sank fully back, Wiener tapped the black box.

"Do not ask whether the machine can decide to kill," he said. "Ask who has decided that its decision will count."

Then he was gone.

The clay golem remained at the grave, its blank forehead waiting for a word. That is always the temptation: write a name, write a command, write a target class, write victory, write security, write necessity, write defense, write inevitable. The old stories knew the danger was not that the golem would become human. The danger was that it would obey.

Autonomous weapons are not evil because they are machines. They are dangerous because they let human beings hide inside systems at the exact moment when human beings most need to be answerable. The dead mathematician's lesson is not "never automate." It is sharper and less comfortable: never automate a purpose you have not morally understood, never release a system whose errors outrun correction, and never let the living pretend that accountability can be buried in the machine and still rise when summoned.
