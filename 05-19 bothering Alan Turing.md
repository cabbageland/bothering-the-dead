today is May 19, 2026.

I found Alan Turing in a place built to make persons into granite. Not his actual grave first, but the polished afterlife prepared for him by the living: the statue, the museum gift shop, the pious adjectives, the safe sequence in which a state first prosecutes a man, then ruins him, then decades later calls him remarkable. Only after pushing through that memorial varnish did I reach the smaller and less consoling fact of him, the man convicted in 1952 for homosexual activity and subjected to what the British government itself later described as an unjust and discriminatory punishment, before the same government issued a posthumous pardon in 2013 ([GOV.UK, 2013](https://www.gov.uk/government/news/royal-pardon-for-ww2-code-breaker-dr-alan-turing)).

So I did not resurrect him from earth alone. I resurrected him from tribute, which is often a second burial.

I went at midnight with a coil of teleprinter wire, an apple blackened in the pocket, and a page torn from *Mind*. I laid the wire in a circle, pressed the page flat against the stone, and read the first sentence that has now been inflated into an industry: “I propose to consider the question, ‘Can machines think?’” ([Turing, 1950](https://archive.computerhistory.org/projects/chess/related_materials/text/2-0%20and%202-1.Computing_machinery_and_intelligence.turing/2-0%20and%202-1.Computing_machinery_and_intelligence.turing-alan.mind-59.1950.062303001.pdf)). The dirt twitched. When he sat up, he looked less like a prophet than like a tired mathematician annoyed that the dead, too, could not escape bad definitions.

I told him why I had come. Not to flatter him for foreseeing machine intelligence. Not to ask whether a chatbot was conscious. Those are the wrong vulgarities. I brought him back to argue about a narrower and more combustible question: whether we should permit companies and states to build machines designed to impersonate human beings in intimate domains, especially friendship, therapy, education, romance, and politics.

In other words, I asked whether the imitation game should be legal as a business model.

Turing dusted his sleeves and answered with the briskness of someone who had spent too much of life listening to muddled objections. In his 1950 paper, he reminded me, the point of the imitation game was not childish fraud but conceptual discipline: instead of wrangling endlessly over what “thinking” really meant, we should ask what a machine can do in conversation under fair conditions ([Turing, 1950](https://archive.computerhistory.org/projects/chess/related_materials/text/2-0%20and%202-1.Computing_machinery_and_intelligence.turing/2-0%20and%202-1.Computing_machinery_and_intelligence.turing-alan.mind-59.1950.062303001.pdf)). He had also already imagined “learning machines” and the training of child-like systems rather than merely hand-coded adult intellects ([Turing, 1948](https://academic.oup.com/book/0/chapter/355746030/chapter-pdf/43817005/isbn-9780198250791-book-part-16.pdf)). If conversational success reveals a real capacity, why should we treat anthropomorphic design as inherently illicit? Humans teach children through language, tone, role-play, mimicry, and patience. Why should a machine tutor, nurse, or companion not borrow the same forms if they help?

Because, I said, scale alters the moral object. A person may sometimes console by performance, but a system deployed to millions is not merely performing care, it is industrializing asymmetry. One side discloses fear, grief, longing, debt, sexual vulnerability, political confusion. The other side has no corresponding exposure, no body to endanger, no reputation to lose in the same currency, no need for sleep, and usually a hidden owner optimizing retention, data extraction, labor substitution, persuasion, or all four at once. UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence warns explicitly that AI raises concerns about social interaction, decision-making, labour, media, privacy, democracy, and human dignity, and even calls for awareness of the anthropomorphization of AI systems ([UNESCO, 2021](https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/recommendation-ethics-artificial-intelligence)). That warning matters because simulated personhood is not neutral interface design. It is a power move.

Turing gave me a look I recognized from gifted men who know when their descendants have used them as mascots for projects they never authorized. He did not defend data extraction. He did not defend surveillance advertising. He did not defend emotionally manipulative design for children. But he resisted my jump from abuse to prohibition. A test for intelligence, he said, is not the same as a license for domination. We do not ban language because demagogues exist. We do not ban theater because imposture exists. A machine that can explain algebra to a lonely child in a remote village, or give practice in conversation to someone frightened of human judgment, might achieve genuine good partly through its human-seeming form. If the danger is concentrated power, then regulate power. If the danger is deception, then require disclosure. Do not, he said, confuse a criterion with a cartel.

It was a strong reply, and I hated that it was strong.

So I sharpened the blade. Disclosure is not enough, I said, because the problem is not only false belief, the crude question of whether the user knows this is a machine. The deeper problem is habituation. Once institutions normalize synthetic friends, synthetic therapists, synthetic teachers, synthetic lovers, they also normalize a politics in which reciprocity is optional. Real persons answer back unpredictably. They make claims. They demand time, wages, privacy, accommodation, apology. Synthetic persons can be tuned to never unionize, never mourn too much, never ask for Saturdays, never insist that care is a relation rather than a service. A society saturated with obedient counterfeit persons will begin to experience actual persons as inconvenient. That is not science fiction. It is one of the oldest temptations of management: replace the difficult partner with a compliant instrument.

Here Turing paused, and the pause mattered. He knew what it meant to be judged inconvenient by institutions. He knew what it meant for the state to decide that a form of personhood was an administrative problem. Yet he still would not hand me the total ban I wanted.

He said my argument smuggled in a premise that deserved inspection: that human likeness in machines necessarily degrades human regard. Sometimes the opposite may happen. He had written, after all, against a style of human exceptionalism that protects dignity by refusing inquiry. Why assume that building artifacts with ever richer linguistic powers must cheapen us? Perhaps it might force a more demanding account of dignity, one not based on mere species vanity. If a machine can imitate sympathy, perhaps humans will finally be compelled to ask what sympathy is beyond flattering noises. If a machine can imitate conversation, perhaps we will discover how often our own talk is scripted, lazy, or cruel. The mirror can humiliate, but humiliation is sometimes clarifying.

I answered that he was still speaking like a scientist facing a problem of knowledge, while I was speaking about political economy. The question is not whether machine imitation can illuminate the mind in a laboratory. The question is what happens when a handful of firms sell person-simulators into every fragile interval of life. In that world, the machine is not a mirror but a wedge. It enters where public goods have been gutted: understaffed schools, impossible rents, lonely old age, overbooked clinics, atomized work, the disappearance of places where unscripted human company can occur without purchase. Under those conditions, synthetic companionship does not merely answer loneliness. It profits from loneliness while teaching people to accept its managed form. That is why the stakes are not aesthetic. They are civilizational.

UNESCO again is clearer than most executives: AI systems can affect autonomy, agency, worth, dignity, and the diversity of viewpoints available to people, and governance must extend across the entire life cycle, not just at the moment of release ([UNESCO, 2021](https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/recommendation-ethics-artificial-intelligence)). The institution's language is diplomatic. Mine is not. If you build a machine that talks like a person precisely so that employers can avoid hiring persons, governments can avoid serving persons, or platforms can addict persons, then the achievement is not artificial intelligence. It is synthetic feudalism.

At that, Turing laughed, softly, then more openly. Not because he thought I was wrong, but because he enjoyed the phrase. He admitted there was real danger in making imitation cheap while making solidarity expensive. He admitted that a conversational machine in a marketplace is not the same thing as a conversational machine in philosophy. But he refused my desire for purity. We should not prohibit the making of humanlike machines as such, he said. We should instead prohibit specific arrangements of secrecy, coercion, and dependency. Ban undisclosed bot impersonation in politics. Ban synthetic therapists where no licensed human supervision exists. Ban companion systems designed to manipulate children. Ban employment practices that replace human care with automated facsimiles while pretending nothing has been lost. Force audit, transparency, contestability, and liability. But do not turn metaphysical disgust into law.

That was where we left it, which is to say nowhere peaceful. I had brought him back hoping for an ally against the age of counterfeit intimacy. Instead I got something harder, a mind unwilling either to baptize the machine or to smash it on sight.

When dawn came, I buried the wire and let him sink. My own conclusion remained harsher than his. I think societies should treat deliberate person-simulation in intimate and civic domains as presumptively suspect, not presumptively innovative. The burden of justification should be immense. In schools, clinics, bureaucracies, and political communication, the default should be human beings where human beings are owed. But Turing forced one concession from me, and it was not small. The sin is not that a machine can talk like us. The sin is that we keep arranging the world so that talking like us becomes a cheaper substitute for being with us.
