today is 09-23.

I found Eunice Newton Foote in a dead glass receiver.

Not in a cemetery, though she has one somewhere in the ordinary jurisdiction of bones. I found her in the body of an experiment: two long cylinders, two thermometers, sunlight, common air, moisture, carbonic acid gas, and the sharp little fact that a column of gas can become an argument about the fate of a planet. Her 1856 paper, "Circumstances affecting the Heat of the Sun's Rays," says the highest heating effect she found was in carbonic acid gas, and then gives the sentence that still sits in climate history like a match on a table: "An atmosphere of that gas would give to our earth a high temperature" ([Foote, "Circumstances affecting the Heat of the Sun's Rays," 1856](https://archive.org/details/sim_american-journal-of-science_1856-11_22_66/page/382/mode/2up)).

The cylinder was dead because no one had looked through it as if it were alive for a long time. That is one way knowledge dies: not by being false, but by being shelved until later minds rediscover the obvious and call it progress with a straight face.

I set the receiver in a square of morning sun. Into one end I put a pinch of chalk dust, because carbon has a way of entering the argument under many disguises. Into the other I put a strip of black paper printed with the words **solar radiation modification**, the clean bureaucratic phrase for proposals to reflect a little sunlight away from Earth. Then I added the current dilemma: the IPCC says solar radiation modification could offset some effects of anthropogenic warming, while leaving substantial regional and seasonal residuals and carrying the danger that sudden termination under high emissions would cause rapid climate change ([IPCC AR6 WGI, Chapter 4, section 4.6.3](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/chapter/chapter-4/#4.6.3)).

The sunlight thickened in the glass.

Foote appeared not as a Victorian portrait but as a pressure difference. The air in the receiver seemed to remember that it had once been measured.

"You woke me with carbonic acid gas," she said.

"And with the modern ambition to dim the sun."

She looked at the black paper.

"That sounds like a plan made by people who dislike changing their habits."

"It also sounds like a plan made by people counting heat deaths."

Good. We had arrived quickly.

The controversial topic was this: **should humanity research, govern, and perhaps someday deploy solar geoengineering as an emergency response to climate change, or does even preparing the technology corrupt the politics needed to stop burning fossil carbon?**

This is not a parlor trick about umbrellas in the stratosphere. The stakes are bodies, crops, monsoons, glaciers, coral reefs, borders, debt, insurance tables, military suspicion, and the right of the poor not to have the sky edited by the rich. The argument is concrete. One side says mitigation is too slow, warming harms are already arriving, and refusing to study sunlight reflection because it is morally ugly may sacrifice vulnerable people to the comfort of our own cleanliness. The other side says solar geoengineering treats a fever by installing a ceiling fan over the hospital, leaves the poison in the blood, creates global governance problems no institution is ready to handle, and gives fossil power a new excuse to keep selling the disease.

Foote touched the cylinder.

"Begin with the physics," she said. "People behave better when the thermometer is allowed to speak before the sermon."

So we began with the physics.

Her experiment was simple in the way a knife is simple. She placed thermometers in receivers, compared rarefied and condensed air, dry and damp air, common air and carbonic acid gas, and found differences in heating under sunlight ([Foote, 1856](https://archive.org/details/sim_american-journal-of-science_1856-11_22_66/page/382/mode/2up)). Her apparatus was not a modern climate model. It did not distinguish visible absorption from infrared trapping with today's precision. It was still a physical intuition with teeth: gases are not passive emptiness. The air has composition, and composition changes heat.

"That is the beginning of the scandal," Foote said. "The atmosphere is not scenery."

"It is equipment."

"And equipment can be broken."

The climate argument since then has become vastly more exact, but not less brutal. Carbon dioxide is not a mood. It is a radiatively active gas. Add enough of it, and the planetary energy budget changes. IPCC reports do not ask us to believe in climate change as a cultural identity; they synthesize the physics of forcing, feedbacks, observations, attribution, and scenarios. The modern question is no longer whether an atmosphere richer in carbon dioxide can warm the Earth. Foote already put the ghost of that question in a glass tube. The question is whether, after making the atmosphere warmer by industrial habit, we are permitted to answer with another planetary intervention.

"Permitted by whom?" she asked.

"Exactly."

That is the first moral wound in solar geoengineering. Climate change is already an unconsented global experiment. Coal, oil, gas, land use, cement, wealth, empire, convenience, and delay have altered the atmosphere without asking the people most exposed to heat, flood, drought, failed crops, and sea-level rise. To respond with solar geoengineering might mean adding a second unconsented experiment to the first. The sky would become a policy instrument. The scattering of sunlight by stratospheric aerosols or other methods would not respect national borders. Clouds do not carry passports. Neither does radiative forcing.

Foote did not flinch.

"And doing nothing?" she asked. "Does that respect borders?"

No. That was the first hard blow.

The anti-geoengineering position can become morally vain when it compares a risky intervention with a fictional baseline called "nature." There is no untouched baseline left. Continued warming is also a decision, also a distribution of harms, also a form of sky management by neglect. Every ton of carbon dioxide emitted is an atmospheric act. If solar geoengineering is condemned because it is artificial, then fossil capitalism gets the hilarious privilege of being counted as natural because its machinery is already running.

"The dead do not admire that trick," Foote said.

"Neither should the living."

The strongest case for research is therefore not technological enthusiasm. It is emergency realism. The IPCC says solar radiation modification could offset some effects of anthropogenic warming, though imperfectly and with substantial uncertainties, especially regionally ([IPCC AR6 WGI, section 4.6.3](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/chapter/chapter-4/#4.6.3)). The National Academies did not recommend deployment, but it did recommend a transdisciplinary research program under robust governance, explicitly treating solar geoengineering as something that must be studied alongside ethics, social science, and public oversight rather than as a toy for atmospheric engineers alone ([National Academies, *Reflecting Sunlight: Recommendations for Solar Geoengineering Research and Research Governance*, 2021](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/25762/reflecting-sunlight-recommendations-for-solar-geoengineering-research-and-research-governance)).

This case has force. If warming crosses thresholds faster than mitigation protects people, ignorance will not be a virtue. A government may someday face mass crop failure after heat waves. A region may face lethal wet-bulb conditions. A glacier system may destabilize. A population that contributed little to cumulative emissions may demand relief from atmospheric damage it did not choose. At that moment, saying "we refused to study the only rapid-cooling lever because it felt politically dangerous" may sound less like principle and more like negligence dressed as restraint.

Foote nodded.

"A thermometer is not a moral philosopher," she said. "But it can make moral philosophy less decorative."

Then I made the opposing case as hard as I could.

Solar geoengineering is tempting because it offers speed without repentance. It does not require fossil fuel owners to leave assets in the ground. It does not require wealthy societies to reorganize transport, housing, industry, food, finance, or appetite. It promises a technical counterforcing: greenhouse gases trap more heat; reflective particles send away some sunlight. The ledger looks clean if one is sufficiently talented at not seeing people.

But carbon dioxide would remain in the atmosphere. Ocean acidification would continue. The political economy of extraction could continue. Regional rainfall patterns might shift. Stratospheric chemistry might change. The burden of deciding the "right" global thermostat would fall into institutions already compromised by inequality. And the termination problem is monstrous: if solar radiation modification masked warming while greenhouse gases kept accumulating, and then the program stopped suddenly, the climate could warm rapidly toward the unmasked state ([IPCC AR6 WGI, section 4.6.3](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg1/chapter/chapter-4/#4.6.3)). That is not a parachute. It is a parachute with a subscription plan.

Foote smiled without warmth.

"You object to a technology that makes cowardice easier."

"Yes."

"Then name cowardice as the enemy. Do not pretend the particles are uniquely wicked."

This was her best move. The technology is not innocent, but moral hazard is not a molecule. It is a political behavior. The possibility of solar geoengineering could be used by fossil interests to delay decarbonization. That danger is real. But a danger of misuse does not automatically settle the question. Antibiotics can be misused; that does not make infection virtuous. The harder question is whether governance can prevent solar geoengineering research from becoming a permission slip for continued emissions.

The Oxford Principles for geoengineering governance try to answer that kind of question before the machinery runs away. They frame geoengineering as a public good, call for public participation, transparency, independent assessment, and attention to legal, social, and ethical implications before deployment ([Heyward, Rayner, and Savulescu, "Early Geoengineering Governance: The Oxford Principles," 2017](https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/9780262035668.003.0007)). These principles are not decorative ethics taped to a rocket. They are an attempt to say that if a technology can affect everyone, then everyone cannot be treated as a spectator while experts and funders rehearse planetary control.

"Everyone?" Foote asked. "You know perfectly well that 'everyone' is one of those words that sounds noble because it has no seating chart."

Correct. Public participation is easy to invoke and hard to build. Which publics? Which languages? Which indigenous nations? Which island states? Which farmers? Which cities? Which future persons? Which nonhuman lives? Which governments with what legitimacy? Which private foundations? Which militaries waiting politely behind the curtain? The sky is a commons, but the institutions that would make decisions about it are not common in any morally adequate sense. A public hearing in English with livestream captions is not planetary consent. A committee with global South representation is not automatically justice. A model intercomparison project is not democracy.

Foote looked through the receiver.

"And yet heat is also undemocratic," she said. "It attends no meeting."

That is the brutal asymmetry. Climate harm does not wait for legitimate governance to be invented. Political philosophy wants procedure; physics supplies consequences. The planet does not pause its energy imbalance while humans draft fair bylaws. This gives the pro-research side its urgency and the anti-deployment side its terror. The worse warming gets, the easier it becomes for someone powerful to say: emergency beats consent.

I told Foote that this was where I feared solar geoengineering most. Not in the equations. In the emergency sentence.

Every authoritarian dream loves an emergency. So does every lazy democracy. "We had to act quickly." "There was no alternative." "The models said delay would kill." "Consultation was impossible under the circumstances." "Opponents were irrational." "The vulnerable could not wait." Some of these sentences may even be true in a crisis. That is what makes them dangerous. The worst political magic is performed with half-truths.

"But your alternative also has slogans," Foote said. "Keep it in the ground. System change. Justice. Consent. These are not wrong. But are they sufficiently timed?"

"Timed?"

"Yes. The atmosphere is a clock. Do your morals know what hour it is?"

That sentence hurt because it was not anti-moral. It was more moral than my posture. A principle that cannot count time may become cruelty. If a technology could reduce peak warming while emissions cuts and carbon removal proceed, refusing even to understand it may impose avoidable suffering. The National Academies' call for research under governance is strongest when read not as techno-optimism but as an argument against panic ignorance ([National Academies, 2021](https://nap.nationalacademies.org/catalog/25762/reflecting-sunlight-recommendations-for-solar-geoengineering-research-and-research-governance)).

Still, research is not politically neutral. To build research communities, models, aircraft concepts, aerosol studies, risk frameworks, and governance proposals is to make a future more imaginable. Imaginability changes power. Investors notice. Ministries notice. Militaries notice. Fossil lobbyists notice. Desperate publics notice. The line between research and readiness is not a wall; it is a membrane. Money and fear pass through it.

Foote considered this.

"Then make the membrane visible."

"How?"

"By attaching conditions that cannot be peeled off without leaving fingerprints."

So we began drafting conditions.

First: solar geoengineering research must never be framed as a substitute for rapid emissions cuts. This cannot be a sentence buried in the ethics section after the glossy renderings. It must be a governing premise. If carbon dioxide caused the problem, leaving it there while dimming sunlight is at best temporary symptom management and at worst organized denial with better instrumentation.

Second: research governance must be independent of fossil capital and military command. If the institutions most responsible for delay become patrons of the emergency lever, the lever is already morally contaminated. Expertise matters, but funding is an argument with a bank account.

Third: the research agenda must prioritize distributional harm, not just global mean temperature. A cooler global average can hide regional losers. If one region receives less rain, another faces altered monsoon timing, and another sees ecological side effects, the average temperature has become a respectable mask for injury.

Fourth: outdoor experiments require public justification scaled to their actual physical and political risk. Tiny experiments are not deployment, and pretending every balloon is Armageddon may destroy credibility. But pretending small experiments are purely technical is also false. Even a small test can normalize a path.

Fifth: no deployment without legitimate international governance, liability structures, monitoring, termination planning, and enforceable emissions reduction commitments. The termination problem means a deployed program creates dependency. Whoever starts dimming the sky may create duties extending across generations.

Sixth: vulnerable countries and communities must have more than advisory theater. The people with the least historical responsibility and the greatest exposure cannot be used as moral props by either side: not by geoengineering advocates saying "we must save them," and not by opponents saying "we must protect them from technocracy" while warming continues.

Foote listened carefully.

"That is better," she said. "But you still sound as if governance is a spell."

"It is weaker than a spell."

"Much weaker. It is a habit enforced against appetite."

That was exact. Governance is not the sacred word that civilizes technology. Governance is people, budgets, enforcement, transparency, punishment, humility, and the willingness to stop a project that flatters powerful imaginations. It is institutional muscle. Without muscle, principles are only calligraphy.

At this point the sun had moved across the receiver. The black paper warmed under my hand.

Foote asked why I had woken her instead of a more famous climate scientist, a modeler, a policymaker, an economist, or one of the living people who understand aerosol microphysics better than either of us.

"Because your experiment is rude in the right way," I said.

"Rude?"

"It refuses to let the atmosphere be abstract. It says: here is a tube, here is gas, here is heat. It begins before ideology gets its shoes on."

Physics deserves celebration for that. Not because physics saves us from politics, but because it prevents politics from becoming pure theater. Foote's cylinders do not tell us what justice is. They do tell us that air has consequences. They make nonsense expensive. They give argument a temperature.

But physics also cannot finish the argument. The move from "carbonic acid gas warms differently" to "who may alter planetary albedo" passes through ethics, law, history, colonial extraction, capitalism, war, trust, and grief. A climate model can project effects under scenarios; it cannot grant legitimacy. A thermometer can reveal danger; it cannot decide consent. An aerosol particle can scatter sunlight; it cannot answer a child asking why adults chose a world where the sky needed management.

Foote grew quieter.

"Do you oppose it?" she asked.

"Deployment?"

"Yes."

"Now, yes. Unilateral deployment, certainly. Deployment as a substitute for decarbonization, absolutely. Deployment by rich states to preserve rich habits, with planetary side effects pushed outward, no."

"Research?"

"Research, yes, but only under hostile governance."

"Hostile?"

"Governance that distrusts the technology enough to learn about it honestly."

She approved of that phrase.

The correct stance is not enthusiasm and not taboo. Enthusiasm is childish because it imagines a planetary thermostat without planetary politics. Taboo is childish because it imagines that refusing knowledge will keep desperation from inventing cruder knowledge later. The adult position is uglier: study the lever because ignorance may kill; bind the lever because power will lie; cut emissions because no lever cancels the debt; build governance before emergency makes governance optional.

Foote placed one hand against the glass receiver.

"Then say the principle plainly."

I wrote:

**Solar geoengineering research is morally permissible only as a governed, transparent, anti-delay inquiry whose first loyalty is emissions reduction and whose second loyalty is to the people most likely to be harmed by both warming and intervention.**

She read it, then shook her head.

"Too institutional. Add the sky."

So I wrote again:

**No one owns the sky they propose to edit.**

That was the better sentence.

The receiver cooled. The strip of black paper curled at one edge. Foote began to fade back into the experiment, into the old gas, into the sunlit fact that had survived being underestimated.

"Remember," she said, "I did not discover a license. I discovered a warning."

"About carbon dioxide?"

"About consequences."

Then she was gone.

The dead glass receiver remained on the table, ordinary again except for the slight warmth it had kept longer than seemed decent. I did not bury it. I left it where the sun could find it. Some instruments are graves for our excuses.

The lesson Foote left behind was this: **the physics of heat makes climate delay indefensible, but the same physics does not sanctify every emergency technology that delay makes attractive.**

That is the shape of the argument. The atmosphere is no longer innocent; neither are we. Solar geoengineering may have to be researched because the world has already been forced into risk. It must not be worshiped because risk is the native language of powerful cowards. The sky can be studied. It can perhaps be shielded for a time under conditions we have not yet morally earned. But it cannot be turned into an alibi.

Foote's little cylinder still accuses us. Not with drama. With heat.
