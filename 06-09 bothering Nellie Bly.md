today is 06-09.

I found Nellie Bly in Woodlawn Cemetery in the Bronx, where the National Park Service places the burial of Elizabeth Cochran Seaman, better known as Nellie Bly, the journalist who carried a false madness into Blackwell's Island and brought back a report that still smells of cold bathwater, institutional soup, and male certainty ([National Park Service, "Nellie Bly"](https://www.nps.gov/people/nellie-bly.htm)). This finding is literal only as far as the cemetery is literal. The resurrection was not. For that I used a reporter's trick: I laid a notebook on the grave, wrote the word **committed** in pencil, and rang a little ambulance bell until the dirt remembered the sound of being taken somewhere against your will.

She came up wearing a traveling coat and the expression of someone who had already heard my excuse and disliked its grammar.

"If you brought me back to ask whether asylums are bad," she said, "you are a century late."

"No," I said. "I brought you back because the asylum has changed costumes."

The controversial question I had written on the notebook was this: **Should cities and states expand involuntary psychiatric removal, hospitalization, and court-ordered outpatient treatment for people with severe mental illness who cannot meet basic needs on the street, or is that care merely a refreshed form of custodial violence against poor and disabled people?**

The stakes are not decorative. They are not seminar stakes. They are the person sleeping under an overpass in winter, convinced the outreach worker is an agent of harm; the passenger shoved onto tracks by someone untreated and spiraling; the woman in psychosis cycling from subway platform to emergency room to discharge to platform; the person whose tent is destroyed in a sweep and whose chart now says "refused services"; the family begging a hospital not to release a son who will stop taking medication in two days; the psychiatric survivor who hears "help" and remembers restraints, forced injections, humiliation, and doctors who called disbelief a symptom. HUD's 2025 Annual Homelessness Assessment Report press release, issued May 29, 2026, put the single-night January 2025 count at 745,652 people homeless in the United States, including 266,320 people living unsheltered ([HUD, 2025 AHAR release](https://www.hud.gov/news/hud-no-26-037)). That number does not prove the case for coercion. It proves the field of failure on which the argument now takes place.

Bly looked at the notebook and then at me.

"People without houses," she said, "and you wish to discuss whether they should lose liberty too."

"Some of them are not merely without houses. Some are dying in public because illness has eaten the bridge between need and consent."

"That sentence," she said, "has locked many doors."

She had earned the right to distrust it. In 1887, Bly entered the world of institutional psychiatry by feigning insanity. Once inside Blackwell's Island, she wrote that she stopped performing madness and behaved as she ordinarily did, only to find that sanity itself was interpreted as further evidence against her ([Bly, *Ten Days in a Mad-House*](https://gutenberg.org/files/59899/59899-h/59899-h.htm)). She described women she believed were as sane as herself trapped in the same machinery; she described cold, hunger, fear, boredom, rough handling, and the spiritual violence of being disbelieved by design ([Bly, *Ten Days in a Mad-House*](https://gutenberg.org/files/59899/59899-h/59899-h.htm)). Her book ends with a grand jury investigation and reports that her story helped secure a large new appropriation for the care of the insane ([Bly, *Ten Days in a Mad-House*](https://gutenberg.org/files/59899/59899-h/59899-h.htm)). This is important: Bly was not simply anti-institution. She was anti-cruelty, anti-neglect, anti-medical arrogance, anti-poorhouse psychiatry. She wanted money, scrutiny, better care, and release for people who should never have been confined.

"Then you should support reform," I said.

"I support reform before capture," she said. "You always bring the key first and the blanket later."

The pro-coercion case begins from an accusation against liberal cleanliness. It says that the right to refuse care can become a polite instrument of abandonment. A person may be legally free while medically ruined, psychotic, starving, filthy, terrified, untreated, and unable to use the services that exist in theory. The law can look at this person and say: not dangerous enough, not consenting, not our threshold. The city can then call the result liberty. Mayor Eric Adams' 2022 New York City involuntary-removal directive made this argument in administrative prose: the city said New York law permits removal for psychiatric evaluation when mental illness leaves a person unable to meet basic living needs to an extent that causes harm, even without a recent observed dangerous act ([NYC Mayor's Office, "Mental Health Involuntary Removals"](https://www.nyc.gov/content/dam/nycgov/mayors-office/downloads/pdf/press-releases/2022/Mental-Health-Involuntary-Removals.pdf)). The accompanying legislative agenda argued that a narrow interpretation of "serious harm" leaves out people unable to secure food, clothing, shelter, or medical care because of untreated mental illness ([NYC Mayor's Office, *Psychiatric Crisis Care Legislative Agenda*](https://www.nyc.gov/content/dam/nycgov/mayors-office/downloads/pdf/press-releases/2022/Psychiatric-Crisis-Care.pdf)).

"There," I said. "That is not the asylum as warehouse. That is intervention before a person freezes, starves, or deteriorates into disaster."

"Who determines that?" Bly asked.

"Clinicians. Courts. Statutory standards."

"And policemen in the field."

"Sometimes."

"And a frightened public."

"Often."

"And newspapers."

That was not fair, so naturally it landed.

The strongest argument for involuntary care is not public disgust. It is rescue. If a person is unconscious after a car crash, we do not wait for consent before stopping the bleeding. If a person is delirious from fever and refuses water because he believes it is poison, we do not flatter the refusal as existential authorship. Severe psychosis can sometimes attack the conditions under which consent becomes meaningful. The person saying "no" may be defending autonomy. The person saying "no" may also be trapped inside a terror structure the illness built and then called freedom. Any serious ethics of mental health has to admit both possibilities.

Bly's mouth tightened.

"You are making a door out of compassion."

"Yes," I said. "Sometimes compassion needs a door."

"And sometimes a door needs a story about compassion."

American constitutional law has tried to make that door heavy. In *O'Connor v. Donaldson*, the Supreme Court held that mental illness alone cannot justify indefinite custodial confinement of a person who is not dangerous and can survive safely in freedom, whether alone or with willing help ([*O'Connor v. Donaldson*, 422 U.S. 563](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/422/563/)). In *Addington v. Texas*, the Court required "clear and convincing" proof for indefinite civil commitment, explicitly balancing the individual's liberty interest against the state's interests in care and public safety ([*Addington v. Texas*, 441 U.S. 418](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/441/418/)). Those cases do not abolish involuntary commitment. They make a statement about moral gravity: when the state takes a person under the name of treatment, it is still taking a person.

"A clear standard is not a clean hand," Bly said.

"No. But without a standard, the hand becomes a claw."

She did not smile.

The anti-coercion case begins from a memory older than the current crisis: psychiatry has often converted poverty, femininity, disability, race, nonconformity, grief, anger, addiction, and public inconvenience into pathology. Bly saw poor women moved through courts, hospitals, and asylums by men who claimed expertise and often did not listen. Her Blackwell's Island story is not just about bad nurses or bad food. It is about epistemic captivity: once the institution names you insane, your protest becomes evidence, your fear becomes symptom, your anger becomes confirmation, your quiet becomes vacancy. That mechanism has not died. It has learned liability language.

International disability-rights law presses this wound sharply. The Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities says disability shall in no case justify deprivation of liberty by itself ([CRPD Article 14](https://social.desa.un.org/issues/disability/crpd/article-14-liberty-and-security-of-person)). Article 12 insists that persons with disabilities enjoy legal capacity on an equal basis with others and that supports for exercising legal capacity must respect the person's rights, will, and preferences ([CRPD Article 12](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-12-equal-recognition-before-the-law.html)). Article 25 requires health care of equal quality, including on the basis of free and informed consent ([CRPD Article 25](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-25-health.html)). The World Health Organization's 2021 guidance similarly urges mental health systems to move toward person-centred, rights-based community services aligned with human rights standards ([WHO, *Guidance on community mental health services*](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240025707)).

Bly read the citations over my shoulder.

"Good," she said. "The dead have acquired lawyers."

"The living need psychiatrists too."

"They need homes."

She was right in the way a trapdoor is right: suddenly the whole floor is implicated. Much of the current coercion debate is a fight over what to do after we have refused to build enough ordinary care. We close psychiatric beds without building robust community treatment. We fund crisis response but not stable housing. We create eligibility cliffs. We make outpatient care hard to access unless a judge orders it. We let people deteriorate, then ask whether their deterioration now authorizes force. The state underfunds the soft forms of help and returns holding a hard form, looking morally serious.

Housing First is not a magic spell, but it is a rebuke to the idea that people must first become administratively pleasing before they deserve a floor. The United States Interagency Council on Homelessness describes Housing First approaches as prioritizing affordable housing, rapid rehousing, and supportive housing for people with intense needs in response to strong evidence ([USICH, "The Evidence Behind Approaches that Drive an End to Homelessness"](https://www.usich.gov/guidance-reports-data/federal-guidance-resources/evidence-behind-approaches-drive-end-homelessness)). The National Academies' review of permanent supportive housing is more cautious about health outcomes and costs, but its very caution matters: the evidence landscape is complex, not an excuse to pretend a police ride to an emergency department is a housing policy ([National Academies, *Permanent Supportive Housing*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25133/chapter/11)).

"If the person had an apartment, food, a peer worker, a doctor who came before the crisis, and a clinic that did not treat him like a nuisance," Bly said, "how many involuntary removals would remain?"

"Some."

"How many fewer?"

"Many."

"Then your first reform is subtraction."

That is the first Bly rule: count every coercive intervention against the services that might have made it unnecessary.

Still, subtraction cannot do all the work. There are cases where housing exists and the person cannot remain in it. Cases where medication works and the person repeatedly stops, not from a considered preference but from illness-damaged insight, side effects, paranoia, addiction, or intolerable service relationships. Cases where family members become exhausted guards in private psychiatric wards called apartments. Cases where the choice is not beautiful autonomy versus ugly force, but three ugly options: hospital, jail, or slow public self-destruction. Kendra's Law, New York's assisted outpatient treatment statute, addresses a narrow group of adults with mental illness who have difficulty engaging in care and can pose risk to themselves or others; New York City's health department describes it as a civil-court order for mandated services under specified eligibility criteria ([NYC Health, "Assisted Outpatient Treatment"](https://www.nyc.gov/site/doh/health/health-topics/assisted-outpatient-treatment.page)). A 2009 New York State AOT evaluation found improved outcomes for recipients, including reduced hospitalization and increased receipt of medication and case management, while also warning that the program's benefits were tied to increased service capacity and should not be generalized where services do not increase ([New York State Assisted Outpatient Treatment Program Evaluation](https://omh.ny.gov/omhweb/resources/publications/aot_program_evaluation/report.pdf)).

"There," I said. "The court order can force the system to show up. Not just the patient."

Bly looked interested despite herself.

"Say that again."

"The order does not merely coerce the person. At its best, it coerces the state into delivering a treatment plan."

"At its best," she said, "is where devils lease office space."

But she saw the point. AOT is morally different from simply dragging a person indoors for seventy-two hours, stabilizing the optics, and releasing them to the same sidewalk. If a court order comes with housing support, case management, medication access, peer support, transportation, crisis planning, and a duty on providers to keep working, then the coercion is at least yoked to services. If the order comes without those things, it is a leash attached to air.

The trouble is that every expansion of involuntary care travels under two banners at once. One says: **we owe people care before catastrophe.** The other says: **we want disorder out of sight.** The first is a moral claim. The second is sanitation with a stethoscope. The same ambulance can carry both.

Bly tapped the notebook.

"How will you tell them apart?"

"By the package around the force."

"No. By who can refuse whom."

That was better. A psychiatric system built around real care lets patients refuse particular clinicians, appeal orders, contest facts, report abuse, change medications, write advance directives, receive counsel, bring trusted supporters, access records, and leave when criteria are no longer met. A system built around public removal lets the city refuse the patient: refuse to house, refuse to listen, refuse to fund, refuse to individualize, refuse to admit error, refuse to see the difference between danger and inconvenience. The formal question may be whether the person can refuse treatment. The deeper question is whether the person has ever been offered anything worthy of consent.

"You are becoming less foolish," Bly said.

"Do not get attached."

I tried to sharpen the coercion side once more. "Autonomy is not just noninterference. It requires a minimally functioning self in a minimally hospitable world. If illness destroys agency, a temporary override may restore the person to the possibility of choosing. That is why emergency medicine can be merciful. That is why guardianship, commitment, and mandated treatment exist in law at all. A society that refuses every override may be protecting its own purity, not the person."

"And a society that loves overrides," Bly said, "may be protecting its streets, not the person."

"Yes."

"Then the burden belongs to the captor."

That sentence is the ethical hinge. The burden belongs to the captor. Not to the person sleeping outside to prove perfect rationality. Not to the family to become amateur police. Not to the public to tolerate unlimited danger. Not to the doctor to wave diagnosis like a passkey. The burden belongs to whoever asks for state force. They must prove necessity, specificity, proportionality, a real treatment path, a real rights path, and a real exit path.

So here is the position I could defend to Bly without flinching: involuntary psychiatric intervention can be morally legitimate, but only as a constrained rescue power inside a service-rich system, never as a substitute for housing, never as punishment for poverty, never as a cleanup tool, never on diagnosis alone, never without counsel and review, never without the least restrictive alternative, never without a bed or community placement that is better than the sidewalk it interrupts, and never without measuring whether the intervention actually improved the person's life after the siren stopped.

The phrase "least restrictive alternative" sounds bureaucratic until you remember what it contains. In the New York AOT statute appended to the state evaluation, a court may order assisted outpatient treatment only after finding clear and convincing evidence that the criteria are met and that no appropriate and feasible less restrictive alternative exists ([New York State AOT evaluation, Appendix A](https://omh.ny.gov/omhweb/resources/publications/aot_program_evaluation/report.pdf)). If that phrase is taken seriously, it is radical. It asks whether the state has tried enough ordinary mercy before extraordinary power. If it is not taken seriously, it is a velvet rope outside a cage.

Bly asked for examples, because ghosts hate abstractions.

Fine. A person hallucinating, barefoot, hypothermic, and unable to understand danger on a freezing night may be removed for emergency evaluation. But the removal must be medical, documented, reviewable, and connected to continuing care, not a street-clearing operation. A person with repeated hospitalizations who predictably deteriorates off medication may be placed under outpatient order only if the order brings actual services and respects medication side effects, housing needs, and legal representation. A person who is merely eccentric, poor, loud, dirty, politically inconvenient, addicted, sleeping in public, or refusing a shelter because the shelter is unsafe should not be psychiatrically captured for failing to make the city comfortable. A person with schizophrenia who says no to a particular drug because it numbs, fattens, or sexually injures him is not automatically incompetent; treatment has to negotiate with the body that must live inside the prescription. A person whose advance directive names what helps during crisis should not be treated as a blank object once crisis arrives.

"Now you are writing like someone who has seen a ward," Bly said.

"I have seen paperwork."

"Paperwork is a ward with better lighting."

The line was cruel and accurate. Due process is necessary, but paper can lie in complete sentences. A signed form can say a patient was offered voluntary transport while the patient experienced armed insistence. A chart can say "noncompliant" when the system was impossible to comply with. A discharge note can say "stable" because the hospital needed the bed. A risk assessment can say "low" because no one wanted to own the harder answer. A reform law can say "care" while funding only the front door of coercion and not the back door of life.

This is why Bly matters. She did not expose Blackwell's Island by discovering an evil theory. She exposed a routine. The daily bench. The bad meal. The cold bath. The ignored voice. The doctor who did not know. The nurse who lied because inspection was coming. Cruel systems are often made of boring repetitions defended by official tone. If we expand involuntary care now, Bly's question is not merely "May the state ever force treatment?" Her question is: **What happens on Tuesday afternoon after the press conference?**

Who rides in the ambulance? Who explains the rights? Where is the person taken? How long is the wait? Who checks for injury? Who calls the family only if the patient wants that? Who hears the patient say the shelter is dangerous? Who adjusts the medication instead of blaming the refusal? Who makes sure the court order brings services rather than threats? Who counts racial disparities? Who counts deaths after discharge? Who counts people helped? Who counts people terrified? Who gets fired when "care" becomes custody with fluorescent lights?

Bly's ghost sat on the edge of her grave as if it were a courtroom bench.

"You want a rule," she said.

"Yes."

"Then write this: no coercion without care more concrete than the coercion."

That is the second Bly rule. No coercion without care more concrete than the coercion. If the handcuffs are real, the housing must be real. If the court order is real, the appointment must be real. If the forced injection is real, the side-effect monitoring must be real. If the removal is real, the appeal must be real. If the state can find a person under a bridge at midnight, it can find that person a bathroom, a bed, a lawyer, a peer advocate, and a doctor who returns calls. Anything less is not difficult compassion. It is power with a bedside manner.

There remains a hard residue. Some psychiatric survivors will say no force is ever legitimate. Some families and clinicians will say that view abandons the sickest people to symptoms they would reject if well. Both sides know a truth the other side sometimes insults. The survivor knows that forced treatment can be terror, violation, and long memory. The family knows that nonintervention can be death by principle. The clinician knows that delay can harden illness. The lawyer knows that emergency exceptions breed empires. The neighbor knows fear. The person in crisis knows, or will later know, or may never be able to tell us. The argument is hard because everyone brings a corpse.

I told Bly my conclusion plainly. We should not respond to mass homelessness and public psychiatric crisis by expanding involuntary power first. First comes housing, voluntary services, peer support, crisis respite, mobile care, outpatient access, benefits help, addiction treatment, and rights-respecting community mental health. But where, after that, a person is in serious danger because mental illness has made basic survival or meaningful consent impossible, limited involuntary intervention may be justified. It must be narrow, reviewable, time-limited, evidence-based, and tied to services the state is legally obligated to provide. It must treat force as a moral injury sometimes necessary to prevent a greater one, not as a sign that the system has finally become serious.

Bly considered this.

"You are trying to rescue the asylum from itself."

"No. I am trying to rescue care from the asylum."

"Then remember what I learned inside," she said. "The institution always believes it is the sane one."

Morning had begun to enter the cemetery. The notebook pages moved in a wind I could not feel.

Before she returned to the ground, Bly wrote one last sentence under mine. Her handwriting was brisk, slanted, impatient.

**When the state says a person is too ill to be free, ask whether the state is too cruel to be trusted.**

That is where I left the essay. Not anti-treatment. Not pro-abandonment. Not intoxicated by autonomy while people die in public. Not intoxicated by rescue while the old machinery starts humming. The only defensible politics is one that places the burden on power, the resources before the siren, the person before the diagnosis, and the exit before the door locks.

Nellie Bly sank back into Woodlawn with the satisfaction of someone who had not been reassured.

The ambulance bell was quiet. The notebook stayed open.
