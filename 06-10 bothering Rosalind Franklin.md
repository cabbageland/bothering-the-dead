today is 06-10.

I found Rosalind Franklin in Willesden Jewish Cemetery, where the cemetery's own life-story archive calls her one of its best-known dead and remembers her as a chemist and X-ray crystallographer whose work helped uncover the structure of DNA ([Willesden Jewish Cemetery, "Rosalind Franklin"](https://www.willesdenjewishcemetery.org.uk/life-stories-listing/rosalind-franklin)). Historic England is less lyrical and therefore more useful: it lists her tomb at Willesden Jewish Cemetery and records the sober fact that the tomb commemorates a scientist whose X-ray work on DNA helped lay foundations for molecular biology ([Historic England archive record](https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/photos/item/DP462475)). I came at night, because daylight makes cemeteries too social. I brought a small black envelope, a strip of photographic film, a vial of salt water, and a question with teeth.

The film was blank until I held it over the grave. Then a faint X appeared inside it, not the famous image exactly, but the ghost of the method: a body made legible by diffraction. Franklin and Raymond Gosling's 1953 *Nature* paper, "Molecular Configuration in Sodium Thymonucleate," was only two pages long, but its measurements belonged to the discipline of refusing romance until the pattern earned it ([Franklin and Gosling, *Nature*, 1953](https://www.nature.com/articles/171740a0)). Photograph 51 itself was taken by Franklin and Gosling at King's College London in May 1952, a piece of evidence later turned into myth, resentment, icon, and lesson ([King's College London, "The story behind Photograph 51"](https://www.kcl.ac.uk/the-story-behind-photograph-51)). My resurrection was not scientific. I poured the salt water into the grass, laid the film on the stone, and whispered to the dead woman that her molecule had become a policy problem.

She came up irritated.

"If this is about men taking data," Franklin said, "I have heard the living enjoy that story more than the work."

"It is about work," I said. "And descendants."

The question I wrote on the back of the envelope was this: **Should human heritable genome editing ever be allowed to prevent severe inherited disease, or is editing embryos for reproduction a eugenic line that decent societies should refuse to cross?**

This is not a toy controversy. The stakes are parents who carry lethal or devastating monogenic conditions and want genetically related children who do not inherit them; children who may live inside edits made before they could consent; disabled people who hear "prevention" and reasonably suspect that society means disappearance; scientists who can now alter DNA more precisely than previous generations could imagine; investors who can smell a market before they can spell humility; and states that have repeatedly converted heredity into permission to rank human beings. The argument has one foot in the clinic and one foot in the graveyard.

"You chose me," Franklin said, "because I touched the structure."

"Because structure is the difference between medicine and wishcraft."

She looked at the film. "Then begin there."

The pro-editing case begins with mercy, and mercy should not be mocked just because it has a dangerous vocabulary. If a child will otherwise be born with a serious monogenic disease that causes severe suffering or premature death, and if a precise edit could prevent that disease, why should the future child's biology be treated as sacred damage? We already alter bodies to prevent suffering. We vaccinate. We operate. We transplant. We prescribe. We correct congenital defects after birth when we can. Somatic gene editing, which alters cells in a patient without changing the germline, has already crossed from aspiration into medicine: in December 2023 the FDA approved Casgevy for sickle cell disease in patients 12 and older with recurrent vaso-occlusive crises, calling it the first FDA-approved therapy using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing ([FDA, Dec. 8, 2023](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-gene-therapies-treat-patients-sickle-cell-disease)). The same announcement described sickle cell disease as capable of causing severe pain, organ damage, life-threatening disabilities, and early death ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-gene-therapies-treat-patients-sickle-cell-disease)).

"Do not smuggle the embryo in under the patient's coat," Franklin said. "Somatic editing treats someone. Heritable editing designs someone."

"It may also spare someone."

"That word is not innocent."

No, it is not. But neither is refusal. A ban on germline editing sounds like humility until it is spoken to parents for whom ordinary reproduction means certainty of severe disease. Preimplantation genetic testing for monogenic disorders can already help many people avoid transferring embryos affected by a serious inherited disease; the UK's Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority describes PGT-M as testing embryos created through IVF and transferring embryos free of the condition ([HFEA, PGT-M](https://www.hfea.gov.uk/treatments/embryo-testing-and-treatments-for-disease/pre-implantation-genetic-testing-for-monogenic-disorders-pgt-m-and-pre-implantation-genetic-testing-for-chromosomal-structural-rearrangements-pgt-sr/)). But PGT-M is selection, not repair. It requires embryos without the disease-causing genotype to exist. Some couples will have no such embryos, or very few. A policy that says "select, never edit" may be adequate for many cases and useless in the hardest ones.

Franklin knelt and drew two lines in the cemetery dirt.

"This line is what can be done," she said. "This line is what may be done. People in your century keep mistaking the first for the second because both fit on a slide."

That is Franklin's first rule: **a technique is not an argument.**

The anti-editing case begins with that rule. Heritable genome editing is not merely another therapy with better branding. The 2015 International Summit on Human Gene Editing named the central distinction: edits to embryos or gametes would be carried by the resulting child and passed to later generations as part of the human gene pool ([International Summit Statement, 2015](https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/on-human-gene-editing-international-summit-statement)). The same statement listed the hard risks: inaccurate editing, mosaicism, unpredictable effects across different genetic backgrounds and environments, consequences for future generations, difficulty removing changes once introduced, enhanced inequality, coercive use, and the moral question of deliberately altering human evolution ([International Summit Statement](https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/on-human-gene-editing-international-summit-statement)).

Read that list slowly and it stops sounding like a lab problem. Mosaicism means not all cells in an early embryo may carry the intended edit. Off-target changes mean the edit may cut where it should not. Pleiotropy means a gene does not always do one tidy thing for the convenience of moral philosophers. Environment means a variant's meaning can change with nutrition, infection, stress, ancestry, medicine, and a hundred conditions not included in the sales deck. Future generations mean the consent problem does not end with one child. Enhancement means the clinic door can become a boutique. Inequality means the first edited children may not be the sickest, but the richest.

"You are arguing my side now," Franklin said.

"I am arguing the evidence side."

"Good. It has no friends."

The evidence side is presently severe. The 2020 International Commission on the Clinical Use of Human Germline Genome Editing said embryos whose genomes have been edited should not be used to create a pregnancy until precise genomic changes can be made reliably without undesired changes, and it said those criteria had not been met ([National Academies/Royal Society, *Heritable Human Genome Editing*, 2020](https://www.nationalacademies.org/publications/25665)). Its technical pathway demanded very high efficiency at the intended target, no editing-induced off-target sequence changes, no mosaicism, and normal embryo development comparable to unedited controls before any clinical use could be considered ([National Academies, 2020, ch. 4](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25665/chapter/6)). This is not timidity. This is the difference between surgery and gambling with a person as the ante.

"But suppose," I said, "the technical criteria are eventually met."

Franklin gave the tired look scientists reserve for hypotheticals that have not paid rent.

"Then the question becomes dirtier," she said.

Exactly. Safety is only the first gate. It is not the whole city. The National Academies' 2017 report took a cautiously permissive position: heritable germline editing was not ready for human trials, but caution did not require permanent prohibition; if restrictions were removed, clinical trials should meet stringent criteria, including no reasonable alternatives, serious disease, credible evidence of risks and benefits, rigorous oversight, long-term multigenerational follow-up, public input, and mechanisms to prevent expansion beyond serious disease prevention ([National Academies, 2017 press release](https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/with-stringent-oversight-heritable-human-genome-editing-could-be-allowed-for-serious-conditions)). The 2020 commission narrowed initial possible uses further: serious monogenic disease, no unaffected embryos available or extremely poor options after PGT, and no exposure of embryos without the disease-causing genotype to editing risks ([National Academies/Royal Society, 2020 summary](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25665/chapter/2)).

That is the best version of the permissive case. Not "designer babies." Not height, memory, beauty, docility, athletic probability, or the Silicon Valley delusion that every social cruelty becomes progress once priced as a subscription. The best case is narrow: a devastating single-gene condition, no reasonable alternative for a genetically related child unaffected by that condition, demonstrable precision, accountable oversight, and public legitimacy.

Franklin touched the X on the film.

"A narrow channel can still lead to a sea."

The eugenic fear is not hysteria. It has documents. In *Buck v. Bell*, the U.S. Supreme Court upheld Virginia's compulsory sterilization law for institutionalized people labelled hereditarily "insane" or "imbecile," accepting the state's claim that sterilization could serve both the patient's interest and society's welfare ([*Buck v. Bell*, 274 U.S. 200](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/274/200/)). The opinion is not an alien artifact. It is written in the grammar still available to respectable people: health, welfare, heredity, burden, prevention, safeguards. That is what makes it obscene. It does not say "we hate the weak." It says the public good requires reproductive control over people deemed unfit.

"That was sterilization," I said. "This is disease prevention."

"Every age renames its appetite," Franklin said.

She was unfair, and therefore necessary. The new genetics is not the old eugenics in a lab coat, but it can inherit its reflexes: the fantasy of clean blood, the reduction of persons to traits, the use of medical pity to hide social disgust, the pressure on women to optimize reproduction, the conversion of disability into an error message, the assumption that fewer disabled people is the same thing as more justice. The UNESCO Universal Declaration on the Human Genome and Human Rights states that genetic research and applications must respect human dignity, freedom, and human rights, including the prohibition of discrimination based on genetic characteristics ([UNESCO declaration](https://hrlibrary.umn.edu/instree/Udhrhg.htm)). It also says benefits from advances concerning the human genome should be made available to all with due regard for dignity and human rights ([UNESCO, Article 12](https://hrlibrary.umn.edu/instree/Udhrhg.htm)). Those two claims belong together. A genetic medicine that only the rich can buy is not liberation from heredity. It is heredity with a concierge desk.

The Council of Europe's Oviedo framework draws a harder line. Its Article 13, as clarified by the Council of Europe's bioethics steering committee, limits interventions on the human genome to preventive, diagnostic, or therapeutic purposes and prohibits interventions aimed at introducing modifications into descendants' genomes ([Council of Europe, Article 13 clarification](https://www.coe.int/en/web/human-rights-and-biomedicine/-/genome-editing-technologies-final-conclusions-of-the-re-examination-of-article-13-of-the-oviedo-convention)). That line has a moral elegance the permissive view lacks: heal the person before you, do not engineer the lineage after them.

"There," Franklin said. "A line."

"A line can be cowardice when it abandons the hard case."

"And a hard case can be propaganda when it opens the easy market."

This was the real argument. Not science versus superstition. Not progress versus fear. The argument is whether a society can permit a small, medically serious use without creating the moral and commercial machinery for a larger, uglier one.

I tried the hardest case. Imagine two parents who both carry mutations such that every genetically related child would inherit a severe monogenic disease causing early death. No embryo selection can help because there is no unaffected embryo to select. Somatic treatment after birth would be painful, partial, unavailable, or too late. The edit is precise, well-characterized, and supported by extensive embryo, animal, and clinical evidence. Independent oversight exists. Disability advocates, patient groups, scientists, ethicists, and public representatives have debated the specific use. The child born from the edit will be monitored, but not owned by the study. The intervention prevents a catastrophic disease and does not add an enhancement.

"Why," I asked, "should that child inherit suffering to preserve our symbolic innocence?"

Franklin did not answer quickly. This is why I had bothered her instead of someone more quotable.

"The child does not inherit suffering in order to teach you purity," she said. "That would be monstrous."

"Then you allow it?"

"I allow the question. Do not confuse that with permission."

That is Franklin's second rule: **allowing a question is not surrendering the answer.**

The future child cannot consent to being edited. But the future child also cannot consent to being conceived with a known disease, or to being conceived at all, or to having a genome assembled by ordinary meiosis, which is nature's own casino with better public relations. Consent does not solve reproduction. Parents always make irreversible choices for people who do not yet exist: whether to conceive, when to conceive, which embryo to transfer, whether to use donor gametes, whether to continue a pregnancy, whether to accept medical intervention. The absence of consent is a grave fact, but not automatically a veto. It is a demand that the justification be strong enough to face the person later.

"Face the person later," Franklin repeated.

"Yes."

"That is better than facing humanity in the abstract. Humanity enjoys being flattered."

The person later is the child who might say: you saved me from Huntington's, from Tay-Sachs, from a childhood of pain, from death before language. The person later is also the child who might say: you made me an experiment, you put my descendants under a protocol, you chose a version of me to satisfy your terror, you treated my disability as an ontological mistake. Serious policy has to be answerable to both imagined accusations. If it can answer only one, it is not ready.

The disability critique is especially important because it catches a lie hidden in the phrase "disease-free." Some conditions are catastrophic biological assaults. Some disabilities are made brutal by social architecture, poverty, stigma, inaccessibility, and neglect. Many are both. The UN disability convention's Article 8 requires states to combat stereotypes, prejudices, and harmful practices relating to disabled persons and to promote awareness of their capabilities and contributions ([CRPD Article 8](https://social.desa.un.org/issues/disability/crpd/article-8-awareness-raising)). A genome-editing regime that treats disabled people mainly as warnings to be prevented violates that moral posture even if its lab technique is accurate. The question is not whether suffering exists. It does. The question is whether the society using prevention also respects the living people who have the trait being prevented.

"You cannot honor a living person by declaring her genome a public nuisance," Franklin said.

"You also cannot honor a suffering child by romanticizing the disease."

"Good. Now you have two enemies."

The proper number for this subject.

The pro-editing side often says disease prevention is not eugenics because it is voluntary, parental, and therapeutic. Sometimes that is true. But voluntariness is fragile under social pressure. Once editing exists, insurers may prefer it, clinics may market it, families may be blamed for refusing it, states may quietly favor cheaper prevention over lifelong support, and the unedited child may be treated as a parental failure. A technology can be formally optional and socially mandatory. Prenatal testing already lives in that tension. Germline editing would intensify it because it does not merely select among possible embryos; it announces that a particular genetic sequence should be overwritten before a person begins.

Franklin's voice cooled. "And the market will not stop at mercy."

No. The market never stops at mercy. It stops at addressable demand. If embryo editing becomes a service, the boundary between severe disease and lesser risk will be litigated by desire, fear, and money. Adult-onset disease. Reduced penetrance. Cancer predisposition. Polygenic risk. Deafness. Short stature. Obesity risk. Depression risk. Intelligence fantasy. Athletic fantasy. Compliance fantasy. The 2015 summit statement explicitly worried that permanent genetic enhancements available to subsets of the population could worsen social inequities or be used coercively ([International Summit Statement](https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/on-human-gene-editing-international-summit-statement)). That concern has not become quaint. It has become more legible.

"But you are making slippery-slope arguments," I said on behalf of every impatient technologist.

"Some slopes are slippery because people grease them," Franklin said.

That is the third Franklin rule: **do not call a slope fallacious until you have inspected the incentives.**

The incentives are poor. Fertility medicine is emotionally intense, privately financed in many places, and already wrapped in the language of choice, hope, and optimization. Parents are vulnerable to promises that their child can be spared not only disease but disadvantage. Companies will discover that "serious disease" is narrower than the market. States will discover that national prestige enjoys a baby announcement. Scientists will discover that being first is a drug. Journalists will discover that "edited child" is better copy than "ethics committee requests further dialogue." The result is not inevitable, but it is predictable enough to require guardrails with teeth.

The He Jiankui affair is the warning flare. In 2018, after claims that edited embryos had been implanted and twin girls born, the Second International Summit organizing committee called the procedure irresponsible and said it failed to conform with international norms, citing inadequate medical indication, poor study design, failure to meet ethical standards for research subjects, and lack of transparency ([Second International Summit Statement, 2018](https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/statement-by-the-organizing-committee-of-the-second-international-summit-on-human-genome-editing)). The target was CCR5, the stated rationale was HIV resistance, and the scandal was not only technical. It was moral theater using children as proof of concept.

"The children are not symbols," Franklin said.

"No."

"Then never write policy as if they are."

That sentence is harder than it looks. Public arguments about embryo editing constantly conscript symbolic children: the doomed child who could have been saved, the designer baby with violin lessons in the womb, the disabled child erased by eugenics, the future citizen genetically sorted by class. Symbols clarify and corrupt. The actual child would be singular, unrepeatable, and not reducible to either triumph or warning.

So what should the rule be?

First, no clinical heritable genome editing now. The technical and governance criteria are not met. The National Academies/Royal Society commission's requirements around precision, off-target effects, mosaicism, and embryo development are not optional academic lace; they are minimum conditions for not treating a child as a debugging environment ([National Academies, 2020](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25665/chapter/6)). The WHO's governance framework likewise treats human genome editing as requiring institutional, national, regional, and global oversight mechanisms, not just brilliant hands and a clean pipette ([WHO, *Human genome editing: a framework for governance*](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240030060)).

Second, somatic editing should be pushed hard, publicly, and equitably. If the strongest argument for embryo editing is relief of suffering, then relief that treats existing people without altering descendants deserves priority. Casgevy is not simple, cheap, or universally accessible; the FDA describes it as involving collection of a patient's stem cells, genome editing, transplant back into the patient, and myeloablative conditioning with high-dose chemotherapy ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/news-events/press-announcements/fda-approves-first-gene-therapies-treat-patients-sickle-cell-disease)). That is a medical mountain, not a magic wand. But it points toward the morally cleaner frontier: edit cells in consenting patients where possible before editing embryos who will become unconsenting persons.

Third, research on embryos and gametes may be permissible under strict oversight when not used to establish a pregnancy. The 2015 summit statement supported intensive basic and preclinical research under legal and ethical rules while saying edited embryos or germline cells should not be used to establish a pregnancy ([International Summit Statement](https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/on-human-gene-editing-international-summit-statement)). That distinction matters. Ignorance is not ethical. Neither is premature birth of a technology. Research can reduce uncertainty without producing children as evidence.

Fourth, if any future society permits clinical heritable editing, the permission should be narrow enough to disappoint almost everyone. Serious monogenic disease causing severe morbidity or premature death. No reasonable alternative, including PGT-M, or extremely poor reproductive options after genuine attempts. No editing of embryos that do not carry the disease-causing genotype. No polygenic traits. No enhancement. No nonmedical preference editing. No private boutique exceptions. Mandatory independent review. Public registry. Long-term follow-up designed around the child's welfare, privacy, and freedom rather than the vanity of the researchers. Liability for clinics and sponsors. International coordination. Real participation from patients, disabled people, prospective parents, children born from assisted reproduction, scientists, ethicists, and publics that do not already speak in grant language.

"That is a fence," Franklin said.

"Yes."

"Fences fail."

"So do bans."

This is the part both absolutisms hate. A total ban can be morally clear and practically brittle. If the technology becomes safer and desperate families travel to jurisdictions with weaker oversight, prohibition may export risk rather than prevent it. But permissive enthusiasm is worse: it converts tragedy into a beachhead. The reasonable position is not lukewarm. It is harsh in two directions. Harsh against reckless use. Harsh against lazy refusal to imagine narrow rescue.

Franklin stood by the grave as if listening to the underground.

"You want a criterion."

"I want a sentence that does not embarrass itself."

"Try this: no edit may be made in an embryo unless the person who may be born from it could later be given a truthful account in which her welfare, not our ambition, was the governing reason."

That sentence is not enough for policy, but it is enough to shame policy. Could the edited person later be told: we had no reasonable way to have a genetically related child free of this severe disease; the science had reached a standard of precision that responsible experts and public institutions accepted; the law forbade enhancement; the decision was reviewed by people not paid to sell it; the living community of people with your prevented condition was not treated as disposable; you were monitored for your benefit, not ours; your privacy mattered; your descendants were considered; money did not buy an exception; and if harm came, someone was answerable?

If that story cannot be told, the edit should not happen.

If it can be told, then the question remains painful but no longer obscene.

"You have not solved it," Franklin said.

"No."

"Good. Solved moral questions are often only anesthetized."

The film in my hand had darkened. The X was now crisp enough to look accusatory. I thought of DNA as the molecule people keep trying to turn into destiny because destiny is simpler than politics. But genes are not destiny, and neither are tools. A gene can be a wound, a risk, a relation, a probability, a history, a curse, a source of identity, a site of medical intervention, or an excuse for prejudice. A tool can be medicine in one hand and ranking system in another. The double helix did not command us to edit it. It did not command us to worship it either.

This is the position I can defend at Franklin's grave: **Heritable genome editing should be forbidden for clinical reproduction now. It may become morally permissible only for tightly limited prevention of severe monogenic disease when no reasonable alternative exists, when safety and precision are demonstrated to a standard far beyond present practice, when democratic and international governance has teeth, when disabled people are respected as citizens rather than treated as failed prevention, and when equity is built into access from the beginning. Anything broader is not medicine advancing. It is eugenics learning customer service.**

Franklin considered this and then made one correction.

"Say 'may become.' Not 'will become.'"

"May become," I said.

"Better. The future is not a grant application."

Then she began to return to the ground, slowly, as if sedimenting into data. Before she disappeared, I asked what she thought the living had learned from DNA.

"How to read inheritance," she said. "Not how to deserve power over it."

The X faded from the film. The salt water had dried on the grass. Willesden was beginning to collect the faint gray edge of morning.

I left the black envelope on the grave, empty now except for one rule written inside it:

**When you edit the germline, you are not only changing a sequence. You are making a promise to someone who cannot yet object. Do not touch the molecule unless the promise is stronger than the desire.**
