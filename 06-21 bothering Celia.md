today is 06-21.

I found Celia in the kind of grave that pretends it is not a grave because it has a label, a protocol, a temperature, and a technician who says "sample" instead of "body."

The body was not whole. It had been divided by human hope into museum remains, genetic memory, and the bureaucratic afterlife of a subspecies. Celia was the last known Pyrenean ibex, or bucardo, a wild goat of the Pyrenees whose final population had collapsed into Ordesa y Monte Perdido National Park. Scientific accounts of the broader Spanish ibex decline describe centuries of hunting pressure, habitat change, disease risk, and shrinking populations; by January 2000, the Pyrenean population was virtually extinct when the last adult female died ([Perez et al., "Distribution, status and conservation problems of the Spanish Ibex"](https://www.plant-animal.es/pdfscazorla/Perez.2002.Mammal.Review.pdf)). In 1999, before she died, scientists had taken skin biopsies from that last living female and cryopreserved fibroblasts, which later became the raw material for the most famous almost-resurrection in conservation biology ([Folch et al., "First birth of an animal from an extinct subspecies"](https://orbi.uliege.be/handle/2268/9318)).

So I went looking for a grave and found a freezer.

I brought no flowers. Flowers belong to the old confidence that death has a place. I brought a glass Dewar of liquid nitrogen, a bootful of Pyrenean snow, one brass bell from a goat collar, a copy of the IUCN guidelines on creating proxies for extinct species, and a receipt for a miracle that had already failed once. Then I put my hand on the frost and read the paper aloud, because science has its own necromancy and should be made to hear itself: two experiments, thawed fibroblasts, domestic goat oocytes, electrofusion, reconstructed embryos, synchronized recipients, one morphologically normal bucardo female born by Caesarean section, dead some minutes later from lung defects, nuclear DNA confirming she was genetically identical to the donor cells ([Folch et al.](https://orbi.uliege.be/handle/2268/9318)).

The frost rose like breath.

A hoof struck the metal floor.

Celia came back small, dark-eyed, and furious in the unsentimental manner of mountain animals. She did not look like a symbol. Symbols are human furniture. She looked like a creature who had climbed stone, eaten sparse things, noticed weather before language did, and been reduced, at the end, to a species narrative with grant potential.

"You woke the wrong one," she said.

"I woke the last one."

"That is what I said."

The question was this: **Should humans pursue de-extinction as a serious conservation strategy, or is it a dangerous moral alibi that lets us keep destroying living species while promising to manufacture ghosts?**

The stakes are not Jurassic Park with better lawyers. They are conservation money, habitat protection, animal suffering, public trust in science, genetic rescue, ecological function, Indigenous and local consent, biotech branding, the meaning of repair, and whether extinction remains frightening enough to stop us. IPBES warned in 2019 that around one million animal and plant species face extinction, many within decades, unless the drivers of biodiversity loss are reduced ([IPBES Global Assessment summary for policymakers](https://files.ipbes.net/ipbes-web-prod-public-files/inline/files/ipbes_global_assessment_report_summary_for_policymakers.pdf)). The IUCN Red List currently says more than 48,600 assessed species are threatened with extinction ([IUCN Red List](https://www.iucnredlist.org/)). Into that emergency walks de-extinction, wearing a lab coat, a halo, and occasionally a venture-capital pitch deck.

Celia shook ice out of her ears.

"Begin with the best argument," she said. "Humans always reveal themselves by choosing the weakest version of what they hate."

Fine. The best argument for de-extinction begins with responsibility. Many extinctions are not acts of God. They are the accumulated result of hunting, habitat destruction, invasive species, pollution, disease spread, climate change, and political refusal. If humans drove a lineage to zero, then perhaps humans owe more than apology. We owe repair. That repair might include protecting habitat, removing threats, restoring ecological functions, and, in rare cases, using biotechnology to create a living proxy for a lost species.

The IUCN Species Survival Commission does not treat de-extinction as a circus word. Its 2016 guiding principles say the term is misleading if it implies that extinct species can be faithfully resurrected in their full genetic, behavioral, physiological, and ecological reality; the legitimate conservation aim would instead be creating functional proxies that restore lost ecological functions while minimizing harms to current conservation, extant species, environments, and local livelihoods ([IUCN SSC, *Guiding Principles on Creating Proxies of Extinct Species for Conservation Benefit*](https://portals.iucn.org/library/efiles/documents/Rep-2016-009.pdf)). That is not reckless. That is a cold shower poured over the miracle.

"Proxy," Celia said.

"Yes."

"A polite word for not me."

"Also a truthful one."

The pro-de-extinction case is strongest when it abandons theatrical identity and argues function. If an extinct animal performed an ecological role that no living animal now performs, and if a proxy could restore that role without harming living species, then the case becomes less like nostalgia and more like ecological repair. This is the mammoth-steppe argument, the passenger-pigeon-forest argument, the lost-grazer argument. It says a species is not only a name in a book but a pressure on a system: browsing, digging, seed dispersal, predation, trampling, nutrient cycling, fear. To resurrect the right proxy in the right place might heal a damaged relationship between bodies and land.

There is also a scientific argument. The tools developed for de-extinction - sequencing degraded DNA, genome editing, reproductive technology, cryobanking, interspecies pregnancy, assisted reproduction - can also help living endangered species. Sherkow and Greely's early legal and ethical framing of de-extinction refused both panic and worship: they argued that the technology raises animal welfare, health, environmental, political, and moral concerns, but that the interesting question is governance, not a simple ban ([Sherkow and Greely, "What If Extinction Is Not Forever?"](https://www.science.org/doi/abs/10.1126/science.1236965)). The Hastings Center later framed de-extinction as a doorway into the larger question of whether biotechnology can support, coexist with, or undermine conservation ([Hastings Center, *Recreating the Wild*](https://www.thehastingscenter.org/publications-resources/special-reports-2/recreating-wild-de-extinction-technology-ethics-conservation/)).

Celia snorted.

"A doorway is also how hunters enter."

Correct. The best argument against de-extinction begins with the suffering body.

The bucardo clone did not die as a metaphor. She died as a newborn animal unable to breathe. Folch and colleagues reported hundreds of reconstructed embryos, multiple recipient females, pregnancies that did not produce a surviving animal, and one living female who died after birth because of lung defects ([Folch et al.](https://orbi.uliege.be/handle/2268/9318)). That is the fact at the center of this debate, not the champagne sentence "extinction is no longer forever." Bentham's old moral pivot still cuts through the glamour: the question is not whether animals can reason or talk, but whether they can suffer ([Bentham, *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*](https://oll.libertyfund.org/titles/bentham-an-introduction-to-the-principles-of-morals-and-legislation)). A de-extinction program does not only create the hoped-for proxy. It creates failed embryos, failed pregnancies, surrogate mothers, captive founders, medically fragile newborns, experimental lives, and possibly animals born into a world with no parents of their kind and no culture to inherit.

"You call one dead infant a proof of concept," Celia said. "What would you call two?"

"A warning."

"Three?"

"A program."

That is the brutal arithmetic. Conservation has always involved hard choices and animal handling, but de-extinction invites a special temptation: to count the failed animals as the price of an idea. The more spectacular the idea, the easier the suffering becomes invisible. A lab can say the animal welfare protocols were excellent. A press release can say no animals were harmed. But the ethical question is not only whether cages are clean, surgeries competent, and veterinarians kind. It is whether the created animal has a life worth creating for its own sake, not merely for the redemption narrative of the species that killed its ancestors.

Celia pressed her forehead to the Dewar.

"If you bring me back, who teaches me to be us?"

That is the second wound: a species is not a genome in a jar.

The IUCN guidelines are careful because they know this. They prefer "proxy" to "facsimile" and emphasize that present pathways will not recreate an extinct species in its full genetic, epigenetic, behavioral, physiological, and ecological reality ([IUCN SSC proxy guidelines](https://portals.iucn.org/library/efiles/documents/Rep-2016-009.pdf)). A bucardo was not only DNA. It was maternal learning, terrain memory, herd behavior, predator avoidance, microbiome, seasonal movement, social stress, local weather, habitat, and the long apprenticeship of being born among beings who already know how to live that life. A clone of the last female is not a restored population. It is a biological orphan wearing the same name as a lost country.

"Then why do they say bring back?" she asked.

"Because 'manufacture a partial proxy under conditions of extreme ecological uncertainty' does not raise money as quickly."

She looked pleased with the sentence, which worried me.

The recent dire wolf dispute shows how fast resurrection language can outrun scientific humility. Colossal Biosciences claimed that on October 1, 2024 it had restored the dire wolf after a 10,000-plus-year absence, presenting Romulus, Remus, and Khaleesi as a revived lineage ([Colossal, "Dire Wolf"](https://colossal.com/direwolf/)). Colossal also says its work is aligned with IUCN principles, has no plans for environmental release, and enhances rather than replaces conservation for living species ([Colossal](https://colossal.com/direwolf/)). But the IUCN SSC Canid Specialist Group responded that the three animals are not dire wolves, nor even dire wolf proxies under the IUCN principles, and warned that gene editing should not be treated as a panacea for the biodiversity crisis ([IUCN SSC Canid Specialist Group statement](https://www.canids.org/resources/CSG%20gene%20editing%20in%20wild%20canids.pdf)).

The fight is not merely taxonomic. It is moral advertising. If a gray wolf with edited traits is publicly called a dire wolf, then "de-extinction" has become less a conservation category than a stage effect. The public sees resurrection. The underlying animal remains a living being with its own welfare needs, enclosed in a human story about triumph. The extinct animal remains extinct. The living relative may inherit political confusion, hostility, or novelty value. Meanwhile, the drivers of extinction keep cashing their checks.

"Humans love the word back," Celia said.

"Back is comforting."

"Mountains do not go back. They erode forward."

The third wound is money.

Conservation is not done with awe. It is done with land, staff, monitoring, fences, law, fire management, disease control, predator control where necessary, anti-poaching patrols, community agreements, compensation, political courage, and unsexy budgets that survive changes in government. Bennett and colleagues modeled de-extinction as a conservation funding problem and concluded that, under optimistic assumptions, public spending on resurrected species could conserve fewer extant species and produce net biodiversity loss; even externally funded resurrection can carry opportunity costs if that money could have conserved living threatened species instead ([Bennett et al., "Spending limited resources on de-extinction could lead to net biodiversity loss"](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-016-0053)). That is not a vibe. It is a knife.

Suppose a rich sponsor wants to spend ten million dollars making a vanished animal fashionable again. The sponsor says this money would never have gone to ordinary conservation anyway. Maybe true. Money is psychologically strange. The spectacular attracts funds that the ordinary cannot. A living frog does not get invited to the investor dinner; a mammoth does. If de-extinction pulls new money, new talent, and new public attention into biodiversity work, then the opportunity-cost argument becomes less clean.

Celia was not impressed.

"If they need a ghost before they will save the living, they do not love the living."

"No. But useful motives are still useful."

"Useful to whom?"

There it is. A corrupt motive can still buy habitat, but only if the habitat is actually bought. Wonder can fund conservation, but wonder also loves a spectacle that does not make demands. A dead species is easier to adore than a living ecosystem because the dead cannot sue, migrate across ranchland, eat crops, require corridors, inconvenience tourism, or demand that anyone stop building. A biotech proxy can become the perfect environmental object for a degraded age: thrilling, ownable, media-ready, and politically less troublesome than leaving land alone.

The IUCN translocation guidelines say conservation translocations require intended conservation benefit, assessment of risks to ecological, social, and economic interests, and generally strong evidence that the threats causing the previous extinction have been identified and removed or sufficiently reduced ([IUCN, *Guidelines for Reintroductions and Other Conservation Translocations*](https://iucn.org/resources/publication/guidelines-reintroductions-and-other-conservation-translocations)). That principle is devastatingly simple. Do not resurrect an animal into the machine that killed it. Do not make a living apology and release it into an unrepaired crime scene.

"Then I should not come back," Celia said.

"Not unless the Pyrenees are ready for you."

"The Pyrenees were ready. Humans were not."

The old bucardo story was not only "nature was fragile." It was "protection arrived late." Accounts of Celia's history describe hunting bans, protected habitat, last-minute hybridization attempts, and tissue preservation after the living population had already collapsed into almost nothing ([Garcia-Gonzalez and Herrero, "El Bucardo de los Pirineos: historia de una extincion"](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/servlet/articulo?codigo=5107947); [*Endlings*, "Celia the Ibex and Lonesome George"](https://manifold.umn.edu/read/endlings/section/322b8a16-5b5f-4f46-b7b9-5eb1b4abe741)). De-extinction can look heroic precisely because ordinary conservation was permitted to fail. The firefighter arrives after the village has burned down and announces a research program in ash reconstruction.

"You are unfair," Celia said.

"Probably."

"Some tried to save us."

"Yes."

"Some failed because they lacked tools."

"Yes."

"Then do not spit on tools."

That was the strongest thing she said. Anti-technology purity can be a luxury for people who are not watching the last animals die. Cryobanking tissue from endangered animals is not a moral failure. Assisted reproduction is not a moral failure. Genetic tools that increase diversity in an inbred living population are not a moral failure. Cloning, in the right circumstances, might be a last desperate bridge for an extant species whose living future is otherwise closing. The fact that de-extinction marketing is often swollen with hype does not mean every laboratory tool is vanity. Sometimes a pipette is not hubris. Sometimes it is a very small oar.

So the question cannot be: technology or nature? That is a child's argument dressed as metaphysics. Humans are already inside nature. Our laws, goats, roads, pathogens, parks, fences, climate emissions, hunting trophies, cryobanks, and guilt are inside nature too. The real question is what kind of intervention is justified, by whom, for whose benefit, under what limits, with what humility, and with what willingness to protect the unglamorous living world before staging miracles for the dead.

Celia climbed onto the table. The lab light found her horns as if they were punctuation.

"Give me your rules," she said. "Humans trust rules after they ignore warnings."

Here are the rules.

First, **de-extinction should never be the first-line response to biodiversity loss.** The order of moral priority is living habitat, living populations, living genetic diversity, living ecological relationships, and only then proxies for the dead. Extinction must remain final enough to terrify us. If the public begins to believe every loss can be reversed by a future lab, conservation loses one of its most honest emotions: urgency.

Second, **no de-extinction project deserves conservation legitimacy unless the original drivers of extinction have been identified and substantially reduced.** If the species was killed by hunting, protect it from hunting. If it died from habitat loss, protect habitat. If disease destroyed it, understand disease. If climate made its range impossible, say so without poetry. A resurrection that does not repair the cause of death is not restoration. It is reenactment.

Third, **the project must show a concrete ecological function that a proxy can plausibly restore.** Not celebrity. Not national pride. Not myth. Not the fact that children like big animals. The IUCN proxy guidelines allow the possibility of ecological replacements for conservation benefit, not exact copies for emotional satisfaction ([IUCN SSC proxy guidelines](https://portals.iucn.org/library/efiles/documents/Rep-2016-009.pdf)). If an extant species can fill the same role with less suffering and less risk, use the extant species.

Fourth, **animal welfare must be treated as central, not public-relations garnish.** Embryo loss, surrogate burden, neonatal suffering, captivity, social deprivation, and failed individuals count. They do not become morally weightless because the species name is extinct. A program that cannot say how many animals it expects to create, harm, confine, transfer, or kill is not ready to call itself conservation.

Fifth, **funding must be genuinely additional and transparently accounted for.** If de-extinction drains public conservation budgets, it should lose. If private money funds resurrection while extant species starve, the defenders must at least admit the opportunity cost rather than hiding it under wonder. Bennett et al. are right that conservation tradeoffs are not sentimental; they are measurable losses in living species that could have been protected ([Bennett et al.](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-016-0053)).

Sixth, **local and Indigenous communities must have real authority, not cameo consultation.** A proxy is not released into "the wild" as an abstraction. It is released into landscapes inhabited, governed, hunted, farmed, remembered, contested, and loved by people. If the animal becomes a burden, a danger, a tourist instrument, or a cultural violation, the project is not ethically clean because the genome is interesting.

Seventh, **language must be honest.** Call proxies proxies. Call edited wolves edited wolves. Call cloned bucardos clones. Do not sell the public a resurrection when what exists is a partial biological resemblance under human control. Wonder does not need fraud. If the science is truly magnificent, it can survive accurate nouns.

Eighth, **there must be a long-term stewardship plan before the first embryo is made.** A viable population needs more than birth. It needs habitat, governance, genetic management, veterinary capacity, behavioral competence, contingency plans, monitoring, legal status, disease management, and money that outlives the press cycle. Otherwise de-extinction creates animals and abandons them to the afterparty.

Celia listened without blinking.

"Your rules make my return unlikely."

"Yes."

"Good."

"That surprises me."

"No. You want me to want life at any price because that makes your desire look kind."

That landed harder than it should have. Much of de-extinction's emotional force comes from a fantasy that the dead species wants what we want: to be seen again, named again, photographed again, proof that the end was not the end. But a species has no single mouth. The individual animal may prefer not to be born sick, alone, and useful. The ecosystem may prefer not to host a proxy. The living endangered species may prefer the money. The public may prefer the lie. The company may prefer the valuation. The scientist may prefer the impossible problem. The politician may prefer a heroic ribbon-cutting to a boring habitat law.

"Do you oppose it entirely?" Celia asked.

"No."

"Coward."

"Maybe. But no."

I cannot oppose it entirely because the living world is already too wounded for tidy naturalness. Some losses may be partly repairable. Some technologies may serve the living. Some proxies may restore functions if the habitat is real, the harms low, the governance serious, the local consent genuine, and the language honest. A categorical ban would be a pious refusal to learn. But a blank check would be worse. It would turn extinction into a technical inconvenience and conservation into a brand strategy.

So my answer is narrow and severe: **de-extinction should be permitted only as a last-resort conservation tool under independent review, with explicit ecological purpose, strong welfare safeguards, repaired or reduced extinction drivers, secure long-term funding, local consent, and honest proxy language. It should not receive public conservation money when that money would save more living species. It should not be marketed as reversing extinction. It should never become an excuse to weaken protection for the living.**

Celia lowered her head, not in agreement exactly, but in the way animals lower the part of themselves that could injure you if they decided the conversation had become stupid.

"Then write this," she said. "If you can bring us back, you have even less excuse for letting others vanish."

"Because the tools are better?"

"Because the lies are worse."

The freezer hummed. The snow melted. Somewhere, in the real world, species continued becoming rare in the undramatic tense: fewer nests, fewer flowers, fewer calves, fewer calls, fewer unseen lives making less of the world.

Before she faded, Celia stepped once onto the lab bench and left a wet hoofprint on the IUCN guidelines. It covered the word "benefit" without erasing it.

"Do not resurrect me to forgive you," she said. "Protect something that can still refuse."

Then the frost collapsed back into the Dewar, and the room was full of ordinary equipment again: tubes, gloves, papers, a bell without a neck, and one terrible consolation. Extinction may someday become less absolute in the laboratory. It must not become less serious in the conscience. The dead can be studied, stored, sequenced, cloned, invoked, and, in rare cases, partially proxied. But they cannot do the one thing we most want from them. They cannot make our lateness innocent.
