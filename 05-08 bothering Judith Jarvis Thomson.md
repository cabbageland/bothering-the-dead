today is May 8, 2026.

I found Judith Jarvis Thomson where philosophers ought probably not to be found, in a cemetery that looked less like a field of grief than a footnote that had outlived its book. Cambridge rain had gone thin and metallic. The granite was beaded with water. Her name sat there without argument, which already felt wrong. Thomson spent too much of her life demonstrating that the apparently settled thing was often only laziness wearing a judge's robe.

I had brought no candle, no Latin, no shovel. I brought an old habit of close reading. I laid my palm on the stone and read aloud the opening pressure point of her most famous essay, the place where she asks us to grant, for the sake of argument, that the fetus is a person from the moment of conception, then insists that the moral question is still not over ([Thomson, "A Defense of Abortion," *Philosophy & Public Affairs*, 1971](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2265091)). The dirt shifted. Not dramatically. No apocalypse, no angels, no thunder. Just a practical disturbance, as if a premise had finally noticed its hidden conclusion and decided to sit up.

She rose with the impatience of someone interrupted mid-seminar.

"Well," she said, brushing mud from her sleeves, "have they become more careful, or merely louder?"

I told her I had not come to flatter the dead. I had come because the United States, after half a century of constitutional protection for abortion under *Roe v. Wade* ([410 U.S. 113, 1973](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/410/113/)), had withdrawn that protection in *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization* ([597 U.S. ___, 2022](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/19-1392_6j37.pdf)). I had come because the argument had not become subtler with age. It had become more punitive, more technologically invasive, more eager to dress coercion as moral seriousness. I had come because her violinist still haunts the law, and because I am no longer sure he is enough.

That was the controversial subject I wanted from her grave: whether bodily autonomy can still do the work after *Dobbs*, or whether abortion politics has moved into a darker dispute, one about whether the state may conscript female bodies and then call the conscription democracy.

Thomson looked at me the way one looks at a student who has almost asked the right question.

"Then let us not waste resurrection on nostalgia," she said. "Proceed."

We walked out of the cemetery and into the impossible room that always seems to form around the revived dead in these encounters, half study, half tribunal, lit by no visible source. I gave her the strongest version of the anti-abortion case I could manage. The *Dobbs* majority does not merely say abortion is morally troubling. It says the Constitution does not protect it, that such a right is not "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition," and that regulation should be returned to "the people and their elected representatives" ([*Dobbs*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/19-1392_6j37.pdf)). Many abortion opponents go further: if the fetus is a human person, then abortion is a grave injustice, perhaps homicide by another name. On that view, bodily autonomy is not a trump card. Parents already owe extraordinary duties to their children, and pregnancy is not an artificial kidnapping like the violinist case but the ordinary biological situation from which human beings come.

Thomson did not answer immediately. She sat down, crossed one leg over the other, and became very still.

"The clever mistake," she said, "is to think I wrote to prove abortion always permissible. I wrote to show that the anti-abortion argument was invalid in one of its most complacent forms. From 'the fetus is a person' it does not follow, without more premises, that a pregnant woman must carry it to term" ([Thomson](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2265091)). "People resent this because they prefer moral geometry to moral life. They want the conclusion to fall out of a definition."

I said the world had changed in a way that made her older argument feel, if not weak, then incomplete. The state no longer needs only sermons and police. It has data brokers, location trails, text subpoenas, hospital bureaucracy, interstate threats, bounty-style enforcement experiments, and a political movement that treats compelled pregnancy as a civilizational test. In this atmosphere, debating whether the violinist may use your kidneys seems almost too elegant. The real issue is political domination.

She tilted her head. "Go on."

So I did. If the law forces someone to remain pregnant, it does not merely prohibit an act. It commandeers a body for months, imposes medical risk, restructures work and family life, and can culminate in childbirth, itself a major physiological event. The World Health Organization recommends against criminalizing abortion and stresses that barriers to abortion care risk pushing people toward unsafe or delayed care, with predictable harms to health and equality ([WHO, *Abortion care guideline*, 2022](https://iris.who.int/bitstream/handle/10665/349316/9789240039483-eng.pdf)). And the empirical literature around the Turnaway Study reports that being denied a wanted abortion is associated with worse economic outcomes, more years of contact with violent partners, and greater short-term physical health risk compared with receiving one ([ANSIRH, Turnaway Study overview](https://www.ansirh.org/research/ongoing/turnaway-study)). If those facts matter, then abortion bans are not symbolic affirmations of life. They are systems for allocating suffering.

Thomson gave a small, mean smile, the good kind. "Better. Now we have left the nursery and entered politics."

But she did not let me keep the high ground uncontested. She said autonomy talk can become evasive if it forgets that many pregnancies arise from ordinary voluntary sex rather than kidnapping. Even if consent to sex is not consent to gestation, sexual agency is not morally weightless. One cannot build an adult ethic on the fantasy that every dependency is an alien invasion. There are special responsibilities, and human beings do not enter the world by contractual negotiation.

I told her that anti-abortion politics abuses precisely this moral remainder. It takes the undeniable fact that sex can generate obligations and inflates it into a doctrine of unlimited bodily service. No one else is made to surrender blood, marrow, a kidney, or months of organ use to keep another person alive, not even when they caused the danger. American law generally refuses forced bodily extraction even from corpses, who cannot suffer the indignity, unless prior consent rules permit donation. McFall v. Shimp is still famous because a court would not compel a man to donate bone marrow to his cousin, though refusal meant death ([*McFall v. Shimp*, 10 Pa. D. & C.3d 90, 1978](https://law.justia.com/cases/pennsylvania/court-of-common-pleas/1978/10-pa-d-c-3d-90-2.html)). We protect bodily boundaries there because we understand that making persons into life-support infrastructure would be a moral catastrophe, even for excellent ends.

"And yet," Thomson said, "pregnancy is not marrow extraction. It is not one emergency among others. It is the species' ancient dependency. That matters too. My question to you is not whether coercion is ugly. Of course it is ugly. My question is whether you have allowed revulsion at coercion to make you sentimental about innocence."

This was the sharpest moment between us. She was accusing my side, fairly, of sometimes speaking as if abortion were justified only when the pregnant person is blameless, endangered, raped, poor, trapped, abandoned, or ill. But that rhetorical strategy concedes too much. It keeps searching for the perfect victim. It says, in effect, perhaps autonomy is enough only for those who have suffered enough to earn it.

I told her I thought the stronger position was less apologetic. A person may refuse gestation not only in tragedy but in ordinary unwillingness. The moral seriousness of pregnancy is exactly why consent must remain live throughout it. To say this is not to deny fetal value. It is to deny that fetal value automatically authorizes sovereign use of another's body. Even many people who believe fetal life matters deeply still distinguish between valuing that life and licensing state compulsion. Thomson nodded when I put it that way, because it was close to her own old point: having a right to life does not entail a right to be given whatever one needs to continue living, still less the right to another person's body ([Thomson](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2265091)).

Then she turned and attacked what I had not said. "You are assuming the state is the proper moral actor. Why?"

That question changed the temperature of the room. She meant that abortion politics has been misshaped by a legal imagination that asks only what judges, legislatures, and police may do. But ethical life is bigger than that. A society can honor pregnancy by making parenthood materially possible, making contraception easy to obtain, reducing medical violence in childbirth, guaranteeing leave, housing, childcare, and healthcare. It can lower abortion rates without criminalization if it chooses conditions over punishment. The Guttmacher Institute has long reported that abortion incidence tracks policy, healthcare access, and social conditions more than moral lecturing alone ([Guttmacher Institute, abortion data and policy analysis](https://www.guttmacher.org/united-states/abortion)).

"Exactly," she said. "The anti-abortion movement says, 'You must continue the pregnancy.' Very well. What will you build around that command? If the answer is mostly surveillance and sanctimony, then one sees the moral substance of the project."

We were close to agreement then, but not entirely. I said I feared that philosophical subtlety, admirable in seminars, is devoured in public by people who want blunt weapons. The violinist analogy, by forcing the debate away from fetal personhood and toward bodily rights, had liberated thought. But it also made some defenders of abortion sound bloodless, as if pregnancy were merely a trespass problem. In political reality, people terminate pregnancies for reasons soaked in love, fear, timing, disability, violence, money, prognosis, exhaustion, and futures they are trying not to lose. The argument must be broad enough to include all that texture.

Thomson stood. The dirt was gone from her now. She looked less resurrected than recalled.

"Then write it that way," she said. "Arguments are not ruined by being incomplete. They are ruined when their users forget what they were for. My essay was a wedge. Do not mistake a wedge for a cathedral."

That was the answer I had bothered the dead to get. Not whether bodily autonomy wins every case by itself, but whether it remains the nonnegotiable center. I think it does. Without it, every concession becomes a tunnel for state power. With it, we still have hard moral weather, but at least we know what cannot be done in the name of goodness. A government that forces pregnancy is not merely expressing reverence for life. It is declaring that some people may be drafted into intimate service by law, their blood and organs converted into public obligation. No serious politics of freedom should make peace with that.

When I walked her back to the grave, dawn was threatening the sky. She stepped down into it with the composure of someone returning a library book.

"One more thing," she said. "Tell them the oldest trick in moral argument is to call domination a duty. Tell them to watch for that sentence whenever it appears."

Then the ground took her, gently this time.

I stood there thinking that the stakes of abortion are often described as metaphysical, and they are. They concern life, personhood, dependence, obligation, sex, time, and the terms on which any of us first arrive. But they are also brutally practical. Who gets to decide what happens inside a body. Who bears risk. Who is watched. Who is believed. Who is made to continue. The dead do not need our laws. The living do.
