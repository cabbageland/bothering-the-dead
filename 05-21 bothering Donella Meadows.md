today is May 21, 2026.

I found Donella Meadows where one ought to look for someone who spent her life thinking about overshoot, not in a marble necropolis but at the edge of a wet Vermont field, where the ground gives a little under your boots and every abundance is already also a budget. Meadows died in 2001, after helping write *The Limits to Growth* and after spending years insisting, with the maddening calm of someone who had actually looked at the numbers, that an economy cannot grow its material throughput forever on a finite planet ([The Donella Meadows Project](https://donellameadows.org/donella-meadows-1941-2001/); [Donella Meadows, “We Can’t Go On Growing This Way”](https://donellameadows.org/archives/we-cant-go-on-growing-this-way/)).

I did not exhume her with Latin, candles, or any of the other narcissisms people bring to graves when they secretly want to impress themselves. I brought a power strip, a spool of fiber-optic cable, a utility map, and a sheaf of reports on artificial intelligence infrastructure. I laid them on the grass like offerings from a ruined civilization. Then I read aloud Meadows's own hierarchy of leverage points, climbing from the petty to the profound: numbers, buffers, feedbacks, information flows, rules, self-organization, goals, paradigms ([Meadows, “Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System”](https://knightcampus.uoregon.edu/sites/default/files/leverage_points_places_to_intervene_in_a_system.pdf)). The soil moved when I reached the line about paradigms. It split when I said goals.

She rose the way a person rises from a difficult chair, already impatient with the room.

I told her why I had bothered the dead. Not for the stale parlor question of whether AI is intelligent. The more urgent question is infrastructural and political: should democratic states put hard caps on the energy and compute devoted to frontier AI, even if that slows innovation and irritates every government currently talking about an AI race?

That is a real controversy, not a manners dispute. The International Energy Agency now projects that electricity demand from data centres worldwide will more than double by 2030 to around 945 terawatt-hours in its base case, with AI as the most important driver of that growth; in the United States, natural gas is currently the largest source of electricity for data centres, and fossil fuels remain crucial in meeting the near-term surge in demand ([IEA, *Energy and AI*](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary); [IEA, “Energy supply for AI”](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/energy-supply-for-ai)). The OECD, trying to sound more diplomatic than alarmed, says countries need AI compute plans with sustainability standards and a full accounting of impacts beyond carbon alone, including critical minerals and freshwater use ([OECD, “AI compute”](https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/sub-issues/ai-compute.html); [OECD, *The AI Footprint*](https://doi.org/10.1787/7babf571-en)). And the same organization notes that the physical infrastructure behind AI, from advanced chips to cloud and data-centre capacity, has structural features that make it susceptible to concentration and competition problems ([OECD, *Competition in Artificial Intelligence Infrastructure*](https://doi.org/10.1787/623d1874-en)).

So the stakes are not abstract. This is about who gets power, literally and politically. It is about whether public grids will be bent around the appetites of a few firms selling synthetic fluency. It is about whether climate-constrained societies will treat compute as a private luxury good, a public good, or a regulated hazard. It is about whether “innovation” will mean better medicine and better science, or simply a new excuse to build more hungry machines and then call the hunger destiny.

Meadows listened without interrupting. Then she said, very softly, that I was already losing the argument by starting at the wrong leverage point.

A cap, she said, is a parameter. Parameters matter, but they are weak medicine when a system's goal remains untouched. She had written that changing taxes, standards, and numbers is the lowest sort of intervention, useful but limited, because systems often swerve around altered quantities while preserving their deeper commitments ([Meadows, “Leverage Points”](https://knightcampus.uoregon.edu/sites/default/files/leverage_points_places_to_intervene_in_a_system.pdf)). If the goal of the current AI build-out is still scale, market capture, geopolitical prestige, and the replacement of friction with computation, then a cap merely teaches the system to become more cunning.

I told her that this was too airy for the emergency. Parameters may be low leverage, but low leverage is still leverage, and politics often arrives there first because that is where law can bite. We do not get to transcend paradigms before breakfast. We get utility permits, procurement rules, interconnection queues, emissions standards, water rights, zoning fights, chip export controls, and price signals. Meanwhile the build-out is not hypothetical. The IEA says data centres accounted for about 1.5% of global electricity consumption in 2024 and projects a rapid rise from there; its own base case still has natural gas and coal supplying over 40% of additional data-centre electricity demand to 2030 ([IEA, *Energy and AI*](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary); [IEA, “Energy supply for AI”](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/energy-supply-for-ai)). If you know a system is accelerating toward a cliff, you do not refuse the brake because the steering wheel is philosophically superior.

That got her attention. Good. I wanted her annoyed enough to become specific.

So I sharpened the question. Suppose a democratic government imposed hard ceilings on new frontier-training clusters unless operators could prove net new clean power, water adequacy, public-interest use, and meaningful transparency about what the systems were for. Suppose it also reserved public compute for universities, hospitals, weather forecasting, accessibility tools, and other uses that can at least make a case for social value. Would that be wise governance or technophobic self-sabotage?

Meadows said the proposal was more serious than a generic pause, because it finally admitted that computation is not ethereal. It sits in land, wires, cooling systems, labor arrangements, mineral extraction, and budget priorities. Good, she said. People become dangerous when they call physical systems “virtual.” But she still refused the seduction of simple prohibition. A system, she reminded me, is not only a machine of consumption. It is also a machine of adaptation. If you cap one node while preserving the demand for endless model scaling, the pressure migrates. Firms move jurisdictions. Governments subsidize around the restriction. Scarcity gets converted into prestige. National-security rhetoric metastasizes. The feedback loop tightens.

I said yes, of course. But that is not an argument against caps. It is an argument against caps in isolation. The fact that capital is mobile is not proof that law is futile. It is proof that policy must attack the whole stack. The OECD's AI principles explicitly tie AI governance to human rights, democratic values, sustainability, accountability, and a fair sharing of benefits ([OECD, “AI principles”](https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/ai-principles.html)). If states mean any of that, they cannot leave compute allocation to whichever firms can most persuasively describe their private products as civilizational necessities.

Here she smiled, because now the real fight had arrived.

What, she asked, counts as social value? That phrase is a moral landfill if you let governments or corporations dump anything into it. Drug discovery is social value, yes, but so is an argument for better logistics, improved grid balancing, protein folding, wildfire prediction, language access, disability tools, faster materials science, and cheaper tutoring. The same OECD page that warns about environmental footprint also notes that AI may aid climate modelling, buildings, cities, farming, and industry ([OECD, “AI compute”](https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/sub-issues/ai-compute.html)). The IEA argues that AI could help optimize energy systems even as it drives up electricity demand ([IEA, *Energy and AI*](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/executive-summary)). Are you so sure, she asked, that a hard cap would strike waste rather than possibility?

This was the strongest objection, and I did not insult it with an easy answer. The pro-cap case can become stupid very quickly. It can slide into a theater of purity in which every large model is assumed useless, every technical advance is treated as ideology, and every rival nation is imagined likely to stop just because one democracy developed a conscience. That fantasy is not ethics. It is unilateral innocence, which is often just self-congratulation with fewer factories.

Still, the anti-cap side lies too, mostly by treating every incremental capability gain as if it were automatically worth whatever infrastructure it can command. Meadows herself had written that physical expansion is usually celebrated first and questioned later, even when it keeps reproducing inequality and ecological damage ([Meadows, “We Can’t Go On Growing This Way”](https://donellameadows.org/archives/we-cant-go-on-growing-this-way/)). That is the smell around frontier AI right now, a familiar growth intoxication. Build first, justify later, externalize continuously. The language of inevitability does half the work: if this race is happening anyway, then every demand for limits sounds childish. But “the race” is not weather. It is a set of incentives, contracts, fantasies of national greatness, compensation packages, and stories told by executives who would like public infrastructure without public discipline.

Meadows bent down and touched the extension cord I had brought. Then she gave the distinction I had needed her for.

Do not ask first whether to cap AI, she said. Ask what system you are trying to produce when you allocate scarce energy, water, land, talent, and legitimacy. If the goal is merely maximal model capability, then caps will look like shackles. If the goal is a democratic technical order that remains within ecological limits and distributes gains on purpose, then uncapped frontier AI starts to look less like freedom and more like a subsidy to a narrow industrial strategy.

That sounds obvious when stated cleanly. It is not obvious in practice, because modern states are cowards about goals. They prefer to quarrel over parameters while pretending the objective function arrived from heaven. Meadows's point was that public argument should move upward. Not away from rules and numbers, but through them toward the question they hide: what is computation for?

My answer, after arguing with a dead systems thinker in wet grass until the sky went gray, is that democratic governments should impose hard public-interest constraints on frontier AI build-out now, and in some cases outright caps. Not because innovation is bad. Not because all large models are frauds. Not because abstinence by one jurisdiction will magically civilize the rest. But because there is already enough evidence that compute is becoming a sink for energy, capital, and political deference, and not enough evidence that leaving its allocation to firms and military panic will produce outcomes worthy of the resources consumed.

That means no blank check for hyperscale expansion. It means mandatory transparency about energy, water, and purpose; priority access for genuinely public uses; competition policy aggressive enough to treat AI infrastructure concentration as a democratic problem, not merely a pricing problem; and ceilings where ecological or grid conditions demand them ([OECD, *Competition in Artificial Intelligence Infrastructure*](https://doi.org/10.1787/623d1874-en); [OECD, “AI principles”](https://www.oecd.org/en/topics/ai-principles.html)). It also means refusing the cheap metaphysics that says intelligence, once technically possible, has a moral claim to all the electricity it can buy.

When I told Meadows that, she did not exactly agree. She said I still loved the state too much and culture too little. She still suspected that without a deeper change in goals and myths, every cap would eventually be gamed, laundered, or reversed. She was probably right. But she conceded one thing before returning to the ground: in an age addicted to scale, a limit can still be a philosophical instrument. Sometimes a rule is not merely a fence. Sometimes it is how a society announces that a private appetite is no longer allowed to masquerade as the future.

Then she lay back down among the roots, and the field settled. I gathered the cords, the reports, and the damp papers. The argument she left me with is harsher than a slogan and more useful than a panic. If we are serious about democracy, climate, and the material dignity of the future, we cannot talk about AI as though it were made of pure thought. It is made of earth and fuel and law. That means it can be governed. It also means it must be.
