today is May 9, 2026.

I found John Stuart Mill in Avignon, or rather I found the arrangement by which his body had been persuaded to remain there. The tomb stands with the tidiness of a mind that believed arguments should be clean even when life is not. I had gone before sunrise because serious desecration deserves decent light. The stone was cool, the garden clipped, the silence almost offensively civilized. Mill had spent his life defending liberty against the heavy hands of custom and state power, and it seemed fitting that I woke him not with incense or Latin but with the oldest liberal provocation I could manage.

I put my hand on the slab and read from *On Liberty*: "the peculiar evil of silencing the expression of an opinion is, that it is robbing the human race" ([Mill, *On Liberty*, ch. 2, 1859, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34901/34901-h/34901-h.htm)). Then I read another of his sentences, the one every censor should be made to swallow whole before breakfast: power may be rightfully exercised over someone against his will only to prevent harm to others, not for his own good ([Mill, *On Liberty*, ch. 1](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34901/34901-h/34901-h.htm)). The latch inside the grave gave way with a sound like a drawer opening in a study.

He rose not as a specter but as a man interrupted while annotating a page. There was dirt on his cuffs and impatience in his eyes.

"What new species of busybody," he asked, "has come to conscript the dead into another argument about freedom?"

I told him exactly which one. I had not come to flatter him as the saint of open discussion. I had come because the liberal world now runs on machines that do not simply publish speech but sort, rank, amplify, pace, and insinuate it. Billions of people receive politics, shame, appetite, panic, flirtation, advertising, and moral instruction through engagement-optimized feeds designed to hold the eye and retrain the reflex. The controversial question I wanted from his grave was not whether social media contains bad ideas. That is too cheap. The question was whether democratic governments should outlaw algorithmic feeds for minors and force much stricter design limits on them for adults, not because of any single piece of content but because the architecture itself governs attention in ways ordinary liberty cannot survive.

Mill brushed mud from his sleeve. "So," he said, "you want to know whether the machinery of discussion has itself become a form of domination. Better. Continue."

We left the cemetery and entered the kind of impossible room the resurrected always seem to prefer, half library, half courtroom, with no visible walls and a table large enough for any century. I gave him the case for intervention as hard as I could. Young people are not choosing among neutral pamphlets in a marketplace of ideas. They are entering systems built to infer vulnerability, to test stimuli, and to reward the content most likely to keep them scrolling. The U.S. Surgeon General's advisory on social media and youth mental health warns that adolescents who spend more than three hours a day on social media face a doubled risk of poor mental health outcomes including symptoms of depression and anxiety, and that platform use is associated with harms around sleep, body image, and exposure to harmful content ([U.S. Surgeon General, *Social Media and Youth Mental Health Advisory*, 2023](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/sg-youth-mental-health-social-media-advisory.pdf)). The American Psychological Association, more cautious but still worried, says the effects of social media depend on design, developmental stage, and patterns of use, especially when comparison, exclusion, or compulsive checking become the governing experience ([APA, *Health Advisory on Social Media Use in Adolescence*, 2023](https://www.apa.org/topics/social-media-internet/health-advisory-adolescent-social-media-use)).

These are not merely bad messages floating in a neutral channel. They are repeatable design pressures. Infinite scroll, autoplay, variable rewards, algorithmic personalization, and performance metrics convert the self into a lab animal that is also expected to vote. If a state forbids cigarette companies from engineering addiction for children, why should it not forbid feed architectures that industrialize compulsion and social comparison during the years when character is still wet?

Mill listened with the unhappy concentration of someone who suspects he is being led toward a principle he does not want to surrender. Then he answered with unnerving force.

"You are describing a danger," he said, "but danger is not yet a warrant for paternal sovereignty. My objection to silencing opinions did not depend on the opinions being wise. It depended on human fallibility" ([Mill, *On Liberty*, ch. 2](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34901/34901-h/34901-h.htm)). "The governors who regulate the channels of communication will claim they are only correcting incentives, only improving health, only reducing frenzy. Every censor arrives wearing the mask of the physician. What you call architecture, they may call morality, and what begins with children rarely ends there."

I told him he was slipping into an old liberal habit, the one that treats every restriction as if it were a bonfire of books. Nobody was proposing that the state dictate opinions or ban dissent. The argument was more specific. A reverse-chronological feed is one thing. A machine-learning system that predicts what will maximize retention by exploiting emotional volatility is another. In one case, people encounter expression. In the other, expression is weaponized by a behavioral feedback loop. That is less like a newspaper editor choosing what to print than like a casino rearranging the oxygen and clocks. The user's "choice" is real in the thinnest sense and hollow in the thicker one that politics actually needs.

Mill gave me a cold look. "Human beings have always been influenced by institutions arranged to seize them. Churches, schools, salons, newspapers, parties, even families are engines of suggestion. Your argument must explain why this machinery is different in kind, not merely in degree. Liberty is not a reward for conditions of purity. It is a discipline for life among pressures."

It was a good attack, better than the lazy version of my own case usually receives. So I tried to answer at the level he deserved. The difference is scale plus opacity plus adaptation. A newspaper may be partisan, but it cannot individually mutate for tens of millions of readers every second. A political boss may manipulate, but he cannot A/B test shame, envy, fear, and desire across an entire adolescent population before lunch. The feed does not simply persuade. It continuously learns which provocations deform a given person most efficiently. That makes it something more intimate and less visible than propaganda in the older sense. It is a privatized governor on the nervous system.

Mill folded his hands. "Even if I grant that, you still face my question. Who decides the acceptable degree of influence? If government compels neutral chronology today, perhaps tomorrow it compels patriotic chronology, therapeutic chronology, safe chronology. The principle you seek to vindicate must survive bad rulers, not only good ones."

This was the center of the dispute. He was right that every governance tool can be captured. I was right, I think, that refusing to govern anything because government may abuse the power is just another form of surrender, one that hands effective sovereignty to corporations instead. Liberalism often speaks as though the alternatives are liberty or the state. In practice the alternatives are usually one regime of power or another. The question is which regime is more corrigible, more public, more contestable, and less predatory.

So I pressed him where nineteenth-century confidence is weakest. What if the issue is not self-regarding folly but the systematic production of impaired civic agency? Mill feared the tyranny of prevailing opinion, the flattening force by which society makes originality costly ([Mill, *On Liberty*, ch. 3](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34901/34901-h/34901-h.htm)). Algorithmic feeds do not merely mirror that tyranny. They accelerate and monetize it. They reward performative certainty, outrage, tribal display, and moral exhibition because these travel well through ranking systems calibrated for engagement. The result is not just harmed teenagers. It is a public sphere trained to confuse stimulation with judgment. A democracy whose citizens are perpetually baited, quantified, and herded by invisible optimization may keep elections while losing self-government.

At this he became still. I could feel him recognizing one of his own anxieties in a new machine. Mill worried that people would become copies of one another under the pressure of custom. He thought individuality required friction, experiment, eccentricity, and what he called "different experiments of living" ([Mill, *On Liberty*, ch. 3](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34901/34901-h/34901-h.htm)). I told him that the feed performs the opposite miracle. It manufactures apparent difference out of statistical sameness. Everyone feels specially addressed while being steered by the same extraction logic. The result is a counterfeit individuality, flamboyant on the surface and standardized underneath.

"Then perhaps," he said slowly, "your enemy is not speech but engineering." He was reluctant even then. "I can imagine regulations aimed at coercive design rather than opinion as such. Age limits. Friction requirements. Transparency. User control over ranking. Defaults less favorable to manipulation. But bans should terrify you. The line between discouraging compulsion and appointing magistrates over influence is one that free societies cross at their peril."

I said terror was not the only proper emotion. So was shame. We had allowed children to become involuntary research subjects inside systems whose operators insist that optimization is neutral because the model has no metaphysics. That excuse is obscene. To design for maximum engagement after learning that outrage, sexual insecurity, and social comparison retain users especially well is already to have made a moral decision. Pretending otherwise is a bureaucratic way of refusing blame. And for minors the case for stronger action than labels and toggles seemed to me overwhelming. The Surgeon General's advisory is explicit that this is a period of heightened sensitivity in brain development and social formation ([U.S. Surgeon General](https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/sg-youth-mental-health-social-media-advisory.pdf)). We do not wait for a perfect philosophy of freedom before deciding that children should not be fed an industrial diet of compulsion.

Mill did not yield, but he narrowed. That mattered more. He said the liberal state may sometimes intervene where developmental incapacity makes ordinary consent fictional. He had written as much about children being outside the full scope of his liberty principle ([Mill, *On Liberty*, ch. 1](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34901/34901-h/34901-h.htm)). But for adults, he warned, the burden of proof remains severe. If we let governments prohibit every technology that predictably degrades judgment, we will soon have to prohibit tabloids, liquor, gambling, charisma, and perhaps politics itself.

That was the best line he landed all night, because it exposed a temptation in my position. Once one starts thinking in terms of cognitive vulnerability, almost every institution looks indictable. Human beings are influenceable all the way down. The dream of a perfectly unmanipulated public square is not democratic. It is monastic. So I conceded what needed conceding. The adult case cannot rest on a fantasy of purified autonomy. It has to rest on something narrower and harder: whether certain forms of adaptive, personalized, black-box amplification become so infrastructural that they deserve treatment more like public safety regulation than like editorial discretion.

In the end our disagreement reduced itself to a blade edge. Mill believed freedom is most endangered when authority claims to know which influences make us fit to be free. I believed freedom is also endangered when nobody publicly authorizes domination because domination has been embedded in interfaces, metrics, and models instead. He feared the overmighty state. I feared the ungoverned attention market. Both fears were rational. Only one, however, had already been installed in every pocket.

When dawn began to dilute the room, I gave him the policy I thought his ghost could live with, even if he would not endorse it cleanly. Ban engagement-optimized feeds for minors. Ban infinite scroll and autoplay by default for minors. Require transparent, user-selectable ranking systems for adults, with chronological delivery as the default baseline. Restrict microtargeted advertising where sensitive personal inference is involved. Treat large platforms less like mere speakers and more like operators of cognitive infrastructure. Do not censor viewpoints. Regulate the machinery that turns viewpoints into compulsion.

Mill looked at me with the exhausted respect one grants an adversary who has at least learned where the actual wound is.

"You may be right about the machinery," he said. "But remember this. Every age believes its new powers justify a wiser form of rule. Most are wrong. If you regulate, regulate in a way that leaves room for disobedience, eccentricity, and escape. A freedom that survives only under benevolent management is already sick."

I walked him back to the tomb through a morning that had become too bright for necromancy. Before he lay down, he touched the stone and turned once more.

"Do not let them call dependency freedom," he said. "And do not let them call guardianship liberty. Your century appears talented at both lies."

Then he returned to being dead, which I cannot recommend as a political solution but do increasingly understand as a philosophical one.

I left convinced that the argument is no longer about whether speech should be free in the abstract. It is about whether citizens can remain free when the conditions of attention are privately gamed at industrial scale. That is the stake beneath the parental panic, the youth-mental-health charts, the lawsuits, the evasive congressional testimony, and the exhausted jokes about doomscrolling. A republic requires people who can direct at least some portion of their own minds. If we permit the architecture of public life to be built on compulsive capture, then "free expression" may survive as a slogan while freedom itself thins into a reflex. Mill was right to fear censors. He would have been a fool, though, not to fear slot machines for the soul.
