today is 06-11.

I found Sarah Baartman where Europe finally stopped holding her. Not in the Paris museum that kept making an argument out of her body after her death, but near Hankey in the Eastern Cape, where South African History Online says she was buried on 9 August 2002 after her remains were repatriated from France ([South African History Online](https://sahistory.org.za/people/sara-saartjie-baartman)). The French law that made the return possible is almost brutally literal: from the date it entered into force, the mortal remains of the person known as Saartjie Baartman ceased to form part of the public collections of the French National Museum of Natural History, and the authorities had two months to deliver them to South Africa ([Legifrance, Law No. 2002-323](https://www.legifrance.gouv.fr/loda/id/JORFTEXT000000776900)).

There is a whole philosophy hidden in that verb: ceased. A law had to persuade the collection to stop pretending a woman was an item.

I came with no shovel. That would have repeated too much. I came with a linen bag full of museum labels, a small brass key from a display case, a photocopy of a deaccession policy, and a stone taken from the road outside a provincial museum where nobody had updated the labels since the century learned to blush. I laid the labels in a circle. I wrote her name in the center, not the racist show-name by which Europe sold her body, and I read aloud the question that had brought me there:

**Should museums return human remains and cultural objects taken through empire, racial science, theft, coercion, or unequal exchange, even when those objects are legally owned, expertly preserved, and displayed in institutions that claim to hold them for all humanity?**

The wind moved over the hill.

The labels curled inward like burned leaves.

Baartman rose with the terrible composure of someone who has already been stared at by worse people than you.

"You brought labels," she said.

"I brought the language that held you."

"Then let it answer first."

The labels did try. They said: specimen, collection, comparative anatomy, educational value, universal heritage, public trust, legal title, conservation, research access. They said what institutions always say when a human being has been converted into an administrative difficulty. Not all those words are lies. That is what makes them dangerous. A museum really can preserve what would otherwise vanish. A collection really can teach across borders. Research can rescue histories that families and states have forgotten or suppressed. A public museum can be more generous than private ownership. The past should not be surrendered to nationalist vanity, curatorial cowardice, or the mood of whoever can shout "mine" with the most wounded voice.

Baartman listened, almost patiently.

"You are defending the glass case before you have defended the taking."

That was the first cut.

The argument for the so-called universal museum begins from something noble enough to be worth fighting: the idea that art, science, and material culture should not be locked inside one community's pride or one state's mythology. The 2002 "Declaration on the Importance and Value of Universal Museums" argued that objects acquired in earlier eras should be seen in the light of different sensitivities and that museums with broad collections serve people from every nation by placing civilizations in relation to one another ([ICOM resource page on the declaration](https://icom.museum/en/ressource/declaration-on-the-importance-and-value-of-universal-museums/)). The British Museum describes itself as telling "the story of human cultural achievement through a collection of collections" ([British Museum, "Collecting the world"](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/galleries/collecting-world)). That aspiration is not trivial. A room in which Egyptian, Assyrian, Greek, Akan, Yoruba, Chinese, Maori, Maya, and Roman objects speak across distance can break the provincial arrogance of every visitor in it. A child in London may encounter the kingdom of Benin and learn that Europe was never the whole theater of intelligence.

"A theater," Baartman said. "And who owns the seats?"

"The public."

"Which public?"

There it was. The universal museum often uses a beautiful noun to hide a passport. Humanity sees the object in London. Humanity files past the case in Paris. Humanity reads the label in English or French. Humanity enters through a security line financed by the wealth of empires. The word "universal" can mean a real moral horizon. It can also mean that powerful institutions have promoted themselves from owners into mankind's stewards without asking mankind.

Kant's formula that humanity should be treated as an end and never merely as a means is not a museum policy, but it is a blade for cutting museum euphemism ([Kant, *Groundwork of the Metaphysic of Morals*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5682/5682-h/5682-h.htm)). If a living person must not be treated merely as a means, then a dead person must not be turned into an alibi by the institutions that profited from her reduction. The dead do not have ordinary agency. They cannot veto. They cannot sign accession forms from the grave. But the way we handle them is one of the tests by which the living declare what a person was allowed to be.

"Was I evidence?" Baartman asked.

"You were made into evidence."

"For what?"

"For European fantasies about race, sex, hierarchy, and science."

"Then why is the question hard?"

Because everything is easy when the stolen object is a corpse that looks back. The harder cases are not so obedient. A mask taken during a colonial expedition. A bronze plaque sold by one intermediary after being looted by another. A sacred bundle whose community has internal disagreement about its return. A manuscript legally purchased during famine. A skeleton collected with no recorded name. A temple sculpture that moved through dealers, donors, laws, wars, and good-faith ignorance. A fossil or archaeological object with scientific value that no present community can claim without doing violence to evidence. A national government demanding return on behalf of a people it has also oppressed. A private palace claiming what was once communal. A museum that cannot legally deaccession even when its trustees know the moral case is strong. The British Museum Act 1963 sharply limits disposal of objects vested in the trustees, with section 5 allowing disposal only under narrow conditions such as duplicates or objects unfit to be retained ([legislation.gov.uk, British Museum Act 1963, section 5](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1963/24/section/5)).

"The law made the cage," Baartman said.

"Sometimes the cage is the only thing keeping trustees from selling the museum for sport."

"Then change the law without selling the dead."

She had no patience for the laziness of false alternatives. Neither should we. The choice is not between hoarding and bonfire, between colonial museums and nationalist warehouses, between scholarly care and ceremonial darkness. The real question is who bears the burden of justification after violence, empire, and racial science have stocked the shelves.

For human remains, the answer should be severe: the burden belongs to the holder. Human remains are not merely information-rich matter. They are the residue of a person and the center of obligations among descendants, communities, researchers, and publics. Modern law has begun, unevenly, to admit this. In the United States, NAGPRA provides for the protection and return of Native American human remains, funerary objects, sacred objects, and objects of cultural patrimony held by federal agencies and museums ([National Park Service, NAGPRA](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/nagpra/)). The 2024 implementing rule requires museums and federal agencies to obtain free, prior, and informed consent from lineal descendants, Tribes, or Native Hawaiian organizations before exhibition, access, or research on human remains or cultural items ([U.S. Department of the Interior, final rule announcement](https://www.doi.gov/pressreleases/interior-department-announces-final-rule-implementation-native-american-graves)). The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples states that Indigenous peoples have the right to repatriation of their human remains and that states shall seek fair, transparent, and effective mechanisms for access and repatriation of ceremonial objects and remains ([UNDRIP, Article 12](https://www.un.org/development/desa/indigenouspeoples/wp-content/uploads/sites/19/2018/11/UNDRIP_E_web.pdf)).

Those are not sentimental gestures. They are institutional confessions that older collecting regimes treated some dead as available matter and some living communities as too primitive to object.

In the United Kingdom, section 47 of the Human Tissue Act 2004 permits specified national museums to transfer from their collections human remains reasonably believed to be from a person who died less than one thousand years before the provision came into force ([Human Tissue Act 2004, section 47](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2004/30/section/47)). The official guidance for care of human remains in museums explains that the section was issued to support deaccession of human remains under one thousand years old from named museums ([GOV.UK guidance](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/guidance-for-the-care-of-human-remains-in-museums)). Even this is partial and awkward, but partial awkwardness can be morally important. A law that once made return impossible can be altered to make return thinkable.

"You sound pleased by crumbs," Baartman said.

"No. I sound aware of doors."

"Doors are not justice. They are architecture."

That became her first rule: **Do not mistake the mechanism of return for the meaning of return.**

The meaning is not that a body changes storage location. The meaning is that the living stop repeating the original domination. France did not undo Baartman's exhibition, dissection, preservation, and display by sending remains home. It could not. Restitution is not a time machine. It is a present-tense refusal. It says: this injury has a continuing part, and that part can stop.

This matters because opponents of restitution often argue against an impossible standard. They say return cannot repair the past. True. They say present people are not identical with dead victims. True. They say modern states are not pure containers of ancestral will. True. They say museums sometimes saved things from decay. True. They say artifacts can belong to humanity. Also true. But none of those truths answers the claim that present possession may still be wrongful. If I inherit stolen silver, my innocence does not polish the theft into justice. If I display it beautifully and let schoolchildren learn from it, I may add public value without dissolving the original claim.

Baartman bent down and picked up the brass case key.

"You want to know when the case may stay locked."

"Yes."

"Begin with bodies."

Fine. Human remains should carry a strong presumption of return, reburial, or community-directed care when a lineal descendant, source community, Indigenous people, or wronged group makes a credible claim. Scientific value can matter, but it cannot be the default trump card, because that was exactly the grammar of abuse. The International Council of Museums' ethics materials treat human remains and sacred material as sensitive collections requiring respectful care and regard for the interests and beliefs of communities of origin where known ([ICOM/UNESCO Code of Ethics text](https://ich.unesco.org/doc/src/Example_code_of_ethics-ICOM-EN.pdf)). The British Human Tissue Authority's public display guidance says consent expectations apply to imported bodies and body parts as for domestic material unless exceptional circumstances justify otherwise ([Human Tissue Authority, public display guidance](https://www.hta.gov.uk/guidance-professionals/guidance-sector/public-display)). Consent and community authority are not decorative. They are the difference between study and capture.

"And if there is no claimant?" she asked.

"Then the holder does not become morally clean. It becomes a caretaker under suspicion."

This is important. Lack of a living claimant is not permission to treat the dead as ownerless. It creates a duty of humility: provenance research, public inventory, consultation with plausible communities, restricted display, and a willingness to relinquish control when better evidence or better moral standing appears. The dead without paperwork are often the dead whose paperwork was destroyed by the people now demanding documentation.

Baartman smiled at that, and it was not a kind smile.

"An empire burns the receipt, then asks the family for proof of purchase."

Exactly.

Cultural objects are harder, and pretending otherwise makes the restitution argument weaker. A carved door, a ritual mask, a bronze plaque, a manuscript, a crown, a canoe, a musical instrument, and a cooking pot do not all raise the same claim. Some were stolen in raids. Some were sold under duress. Some were traded in ways that were lawful but morally unequal. Some moved through marriage, diplomacy, gift, migration, excavation, salvage, or ordinary commerce. Some are sacred in ways outsiders should not inspect. Some have become global artworks, but not therefore ownerless. Some modern claims are sincere. Some are opportunistic. A serious restitution ethic cannot be "send everything back" shouted into a room with eight million things in it. It has to sort.

"Sort how?" Baartman asked.

First, violent seizure and punitive plunder should create a presumption of restitution. The Benin bronzes are the obvious case because the facts do not hide. The Smithsonian's National Museum of African Art says the 29 Benin bronzes it returned to Nigeria in 2022 had been stolen during the 1897 British raid on Benin City, and that the Smithsonian deaccessioned them under its ethical returns policy ([Smithsonian release](https://www.si.edu/newsdesk/releases/smithsonian-returns-29-benin-bronzes-national-commission-museums-and-monuments)). Germany and Nigeria's 2022 joint declaration expressed willingness to transfer ownership to Nigeria of Benin bronzes held in German public museums and to build a new framework for cultural cooperation ([Germany-Nigeria Joint Declaration](https://kulturstaatsminister.de/fileadmin/user_upload/Downloads/Aufarbeiten/2022-07-01-Joint-Declaration-Benin-Bronzes.pdf)). These examples matter because they expose a common lie: that return means the end of scholarship or public access. Ownership can be returned; loans, research, conservation partnerships, digitization, traveling exhibitions, and shared stewardship can follow. The difference is that the lender is no longer the thief's institutional descendant.

"Say it more plainly," Baartman said.

"Access is negotiable. Ownership is the wound."

"Better."

Second, sacred objects and objects necessary to a community's living practice should not be judged as if they were only artworks. NAGPRA's categories include sacred objects and cultural patrimony, not just bones ([25 U.S.C. chapter 32](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&path=%2Fprelim%40title25%2Fchapter32)). UNDRIP links ceremonial objects and human remains because the point is not simply property. It is the ability of a people to maintain relations with ancestors, places, obligations, and forms of knowledge that museums often flattened into "material culture" ([UNDRIP, Article 12](https://www.un.org/development/desa/indigenouspeoples/wp-content/uploads/sites/19/2018/11/UNDRIP_E_web.pdf)). A museum that says "we preserve your sacred object for humanity" may be saying, in more polished language, "we preserve our authority over your sacred object for our visitors."

Third, legal title should count, but never as the end of the argument. Law records power as often as justice. Colonial law authorized takings that would now disgrace the institutions citing them. Market purchase can launder violence if the object entered the market through looting, coercion, grave robbery, forced sale, or desperation. UNESCO's 1970 convention addresses illicit import, export, and transfer of ownership of cultural property and treats return and restitution as central tools against trafficking ([UNESCO 1970 Convention text](https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/convention-means-prohibiting-and-preventing-illicit-import-export-and-transfer-ownership-cultural)). The convention is not retroactive magic for every colonial injury. But it marks a principle: cultural property is not ordinary cargo, and movement through the market does not automatically cleanse origin.

Baartman looked down at the circle of labels.

"You are building a court."

"Better than a trophy room."

"A court can also be a trophy room with benches."

That became her second rule: **Procedure is not innocence.**

A restitution process can humiliate claimants by demanding archives only the colonizer kept, by treating oral history as decorative, by delaying until elders die, by offering loans where return is owed, by requiring gratitude for the partial return of stolen things, or by shifting costs to communities from whom wealth was extracted. A museum can announce a review while leaving the display unchanged. A government can return one object in a ceremony while guarding ten thousand behind statute. A source state can receive returned objects and convert them into nationalist theater. Everyone can learn the choreography of repair without repairing much.

"So who decides?" I asked.

"Not the museum alone."

That answer sounds obvious until you notice how much of the old world depends on museums judging the claims against themselves. The institution that holds the object should not be sole historian, sole defendant, sole judge, and sole custodian of delay. Claims need independent review, public provenance records, funded research, transparent standards, and direct participation by source communities, descendants, scholars, and publics in both places. Museums should publish what they know and what they do not know. "Provenance unknown" should not be a fog machine. It should be a flare.

The Smithsonian's ethical returns policy is useful here because it admits that ethical norms and collecting standards have changed and that collections may require shared stewardship or return even where older acquisition was not treated as illegal at the time ([Smithsonian, Shared Stewardship and Ethical Returns](https://americanindian.si.edu/shared-stewardship-and-ethical-returns)). That is a mature institutional sentence. It does not say: we obeyed the old rules, therefore shut up. It says: the old rules may not be enough.

Baartman approved, but only slightly.

"Mature sentences are cheap."

"What is expensive?"

"Giving up the thing."

There is the moral physics. A museum can decolonize wall text, hire consultants, host panels, rewrite labels, digitize archives, and still keep the disputed object. Some of that work is valuable. Some of it is upholstery. Return is expensive because it transfers control. It changes who can say no. It forces the universal museum to become a guest in the object's future rather than the landlord of its past.

But Baartman would not let me become stupid in the opposite direction.

"Do not make return pure," she said.

I did not understand at first.

"A thing can be returned to the wrong hands," she said. "A nation can speak over a village. A king can speak over women. A ministry can speak over the dead. A ceremony can hide a new possession."

This is where restitution becomes morally adult. The source country is not automatically the source community. The current state may be the right legal claimant in international negotiations, but legal convenience should not erase internal histories. Indigenous peoples, descendants, religious custodians, local communities, artists' successors, and minority groups may have claims against both the foreign museum and the modern state. The moral opposite of colonial possession is not automatically national possession. It is accountable relation.

So the return of cultural objects should ask: to whom, for what purpose, under what authority, with what access, with what care, and with what voice for the communities most connected to the object? Sometimes return to a national museum will be right. Sometimes return to a community institution. Sometimes reburial. Sometimes shared stewardship. Sometimes legal ownership transfer with renewable loans. Sometimes restricted access. Sometimes continued holding by the current museum under a new agreement because the claimant wants conservation, research, or public display there. The key is that continued holding must become justified permission, not inherited domination.

"Justified permission," Baartman repeated.

"Yes."

"That is better than possession."

The universal museum's strongest point is not false. There is a public good in seeing cultures together. There is a danger in letting every object become ammunition for national myths. There is also a danger that fragile objects may be damaged by hasty transfer, political instability, underfunded storage, or inadequate conservation. Scholars and publics in many countries benefit from distributed collections. Students cannot learn from objects that are locked away by governments allergic to inconvenient history. The answer cannot be to destroy the comparative museum.

But a comparative museum does not require colonial title. If the object is genuinely world heritage, then the world should be able to negotiate its circulation after ownership is repaired. If an institution says the object must stay because the current claimant lacks conservation resources, the ethical response is not "therefore we keep it." It is "therefore we help fund conservation without using underfunding as a ransom note." Capacity arguments become obscene when the poverty they cite is entangled with the extraction that filled the museum.

Baartman touched the stone from the provincial museum road.

"You keep saying object."

"Because the argument includes objects."

"And I was an object."

"No."

"Yes. That is what they made. Do not make the word clean."

So here is the ugliest distinction: objects can be returned because they were wrongly objectified, but some of them remain objects. Human remains were never objects in that sense, even when institutions catalogued them as such. That difference should shape policy. Human remains and funerary objects deserve the strongest presumption of return or community-governed care. Sacred objects and objects of living cultural patrimony deserve a strong presumption of return or shared stewardship controlled by the source community. Violently looted cultural objects deserve restitution of ownership. Objects acquired through ordinary documented exchange may remain where they are, but with transparent provenance and interpretive humility. Ambiguous cases deserve research, mediation, and a burden that falls more heavily on the powerful holder than on the claimant dispossessed by history.

"Say the burden again," Baartman said.

The burden falls on the holder when:

the object or remains came through conquest, racial science, grave disturbance, forced sale, punitive expedition, colonial extraction, fraud, or theft;

the claimant is a lineal descendant, Indigenous people, source community, or institution credibly authorized by them;

the museum's legal title depends on laws or markets shaped by the same domination being challenged;

the institution cannot tell a morally coherent provenance story without large silences;

the continued possession repeats humiliation, blocks living cultural practice, or turns ancestors into specimens.

The burden may fall more evenly when:

the acquisition was documented, voluntary, and not produced by coercive inequality;

the object has no living sacred, funerary, ancestral, or communal role;

multiple communities have plausible claims;

the object is fragile, scientifically unique, or central to a genuinely shared research project;

return would place the object under new exclusion, danger, or propaganda without repairing the old injury.

Even then, "more evenly" does not mean "the museum wins by default." It means the argument must be honest enough to admit competing goods.

Baartman stood inside the label circle. Dawn had begun to show itself as a gray seam along the hill.

"You are trying to make a rule that does not flatter anyone."

"That is the plan."

"Then remember this: a museum is not only what it displays. It is what it has the power to refuse."

That was her third rule. It clarified almost everything.

A museum that can refuse return possesses more than objects. It possesses time, interpretation, delay, expertise, climate control, donor confidence, legal counsel, and the aura of civilization. A claimant often arrives with grief, memory, oral history, interrupted lineage, underfunded archives, and a demand that sounds emotional because the wound is emotional. The museum then calls itself rational. This is how power styles its own comfort as neutrality.

But emotion does not make a claim false. Nor does expertise make possession just. The task is not to replace scholarship with grievance. The task is to force scholarship to account for the conditions under which it acquired its materials and authority.

"Would you empty the museums?" she asked.

"No."

"Would you leave them full?"

"No."

"Good. The living prefer slogans because sorting makes them responsible."

The answer I could defend at her grave is this:

Museums should not be treated as criminal warehouses by default, but neither should they be treated as sanctuaries where stolen, coerced, or dehumanizing acquisitions become holy through climate control. Human remains should be returned, reburied, or governed by descendant and source communities except in rare, transparently justified cases with consent or overwhelming public reason. Sacred objects and cultural patrimony should be subject to source-community authority, not merely curatorial preference. Objects taken through colonial violence, punitive raids, theft, or coercive extraction should carry a presumption of restitution of ownership, with future loans and shared access negotiated after the moral title is repaired. Legally acquired objects with clean provenance may remain, but museums owe public records, honest labels, and openness to new evidence. In all disputed cases, the holding institution must stop acting as if possession is the neutral fact and return is the extraordinary demand.

Baartman listened without saving me from the length of that sentence.

"You have written policy," she said. "Now write the wound."

The wound is this: a museum can preserve a thing and preserve the insult attached to its possession. It can teach visitors about colonial violence while continuing to benefit from colonial violence. It can mourn the dead while keeping them. It can say "for humanity" while meaning "under our control." It can make beauty available while keeping justice unavailable. These contradictions do not make museums worthless. They make them morally unfinished.

I picked up one label. It said "educational value."

"What should it say?" I asked.

Baartman took it and wrote over the words:

**Who paid for your education by being unable to leave?**

I picked up another. "Universal heritage."

She wrote:

**Universal is not the name of the country that stole it.**

Another. "Research access."

She wrote:

**Ask whether the dead were researched, or consumed.**

Another. "Legal title."

She wrote:

**Law can inherit a theft without confessing it.**

By then the circle was no longer a resurrection device. It was a small, angry museum whose labels had begun telling the truth.

I asked Baartman what she wanted from the living.

"Do not use me as a simple answer," she said.

That was unexpected enough to hurt.

"You want return."

"I wanted not to be taken. Return is what remains after failure."

This is the line restitution must never sentimentalize. Return is not happy. It is the least false thing left after the original dignity has been violated. Baartman's burial did not erase the Paris display. The repatriation of Benin bronzes does not erase the 1897 raid. NAGPRA returns do not erase the centuries in which Native bodies and sacred objects were collected under scientific and governmental confidence. The best return can do is stop the institution from continuing to enjoy the last word.

"Then what should a museum be?" I asked.

"A place where objects are not forced to lie about how they arrived."

That is enough to begin.

A truthful museum would keep many things. It would return many things. It would lend and borrow more honestly. It would treat provenance as public infrastructure, not back-room housekeeping. It would put legal title and moral title on the same label when they differ. It would admit when an object is there because someone lost a war, a grave, a homeland, a ritual, a child, a language, or the power to refuse. It would let source communities alter interpretation, restrict sacred knowledge, demand return, or choose circulation. It would teach the visitor that access is good, but access without justice is just a prettier form of possession.

Baartman began to fade when the sun touched the stones. I thought she would go quietly, but the dead, when properly bothered, often have terrible manners.

"One more thing," she said.

"Yes?"

"Do not praise the museum for returning what it should not have had as if it invented mercy."

"What should we praise?"

"The moment it becomes capable of shame and does not run from it."

Then she was gone.

The labels lay flat. The key had rusted in my palm. The road stone was ordinary again.

I left the circle where it was and wrote one sentence on the back of the deaccession policy:

**The museum of the future will not be the one that keeps the most of the world's dead things, but the one that can tell the truth about possession, return what possession cannot justify, and learn to be universal without acting like the universe signed the receipt.**
