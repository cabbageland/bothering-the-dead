today is 06-02.

I found Mary Wollstonecraft in St Pancras Old Churchyard, where London keeps a talent for damp stone and interrupted arguments. The grave is not hers exactly, not anymore; her remains were moved long ago to Bournemouth, near Godwin and the family history that became, by extension, Mary Shelley and half the Gothic imagination. But graves are bad at obeying municipal updates. I brought *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman* printed on paper heavy enough to feel accusatory in the hand, and I read aloud until the wet air answered. I chose her because she hated the conversion of women into ornaments, pets, and morally flattering property. I wanted to ask her what she would make of a twenty-first century market in which pregnancy itself can be contracted, brokered, priced, and legally managed.

The controversy was this:

**Is commercial gestational surrogacy a defensible extension of women’s freedom, or is it a polished form of class power that turns female reproductive labor and children into things that can be commissioned?**

The stakes are large and ugly. For some intended parents, gestational surrogacy is the only realistic route to a biologically related child, and professional guidance in the United States treats it as ethically permissible when the carrier’s medical autonomy is preserved, independent legal counsel is provided, and compensation does not become undue inducement or exploitation ([ASRM Ethics Committee, 2023](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/ethics-opinions/consideration-of-the-gestational-carrier-an-ethics-committee-opinion-2023/)). For critics, the very structure of the transaction invites exactly the thing liberal markets always promise they can prevent and routinely fail to prevent: the purchase of another person’s bodily vulnerability by someone with more money, more options, and a cleaner story about what is happening. The legal questions are not marginal either. The United Nations’ Optional Protocol on the sale of children defines the sale of children as any transfer of a child from one person or group to another for remuneration or other consideration, which is why surrogacy keeps pressing against the edge of human-rights law even when defenders insist the payment is for gestation rather than for the child ([Optional Protocol, art. 2(a)](https://treaties.un.org/doc/publication/UNTS/Volume%202171/v2171.pdf)).

Mary rose without spectacle. No lightning, no stitched flesh, no joke. She sat upright as if a lecture had gone too long and looked at the papers in my hand first, then at me, as if checking whether I had brought her back for an actual question or the modern pastime of faux-radical tourism.

I told her the terms. In contemporary gestational surrogacy, the carrier is usually not genetically related to the child; an embryo is created through IVF and transferred to a woman who agrees to carry the pregnancy for intended parents ([ASRM Policy, 2026](https://www.asrm.org/advocacy-and-policy/fact-sheets-and-one-pagers/gestational-carrier-policy-in-the-united-states/)). The practice remains relatively uncommon in the United States, but it is growing, and American law is fragmented, with some states recognizing and regulating these arrangements, some leaving them murky, and some restricting or voiding compensated contracts ([ASRM Policy, 2026](https://www.asrm.org/advocacy-and-policy/fact-sheets-and-one-pagers/gestational-carrier-policy-in-the-united-states/); [ASRM Ethics Committee, 2023](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/ethics-opinions/consideration-of-the-gestational-carrier-an-ethics-committee-opinion-2023/)). Cross-border arrangements complicate matters further, because cost differences, weak regulation, language barriers, and poverty can place intended parents, brokers, donors, carriers, and children in radically unequal positions ([ASRM Ethics Committee, 2022](https://integration.asrm.org/globalassets/_asrm/practice-guidance/ethics-opinions/pdf/cross-border_reproductive_care_an_ethics_committee_opinion.pdf)).

Mary listened and then gave the first blow exactly where I had hoped and feared she would.

She said that modern people flatter themselves whenever they rename dependency as choice. In *Vindication*, she argued that women are degraded when they are trained to live as creatures of softness, usefulness, and male convenience rather than as rational beings developing powers they may call their own ([Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3420/pg3420.html)). She wanted women to acquire “strength, both of mind and body,” not because strength is pretty, but because dependence corrupts character and makes virtue impossible to distinguish from adaptation to subordination ([Wollstonecraft](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3420/pg3420.html)). If you tell a poor woman that she is “free” to rent out the central biological labor of her body to wealthier strangers, Mary said, your vocabulary may be modern but your moral imagination is antique. You are still taking inequality, perfume-spraying it, and calling it social cooperation.

Her case against commercial surrogacy had three parts.

First, she said pregnancy is not equivalent to ordinary labor because it is not merely time sold under supervision. It is a reorganization of the body, risk, metabolism, hormones, attachment, social standing, and future health. Even defenders of surrogacy admit the risks are real enough to require distinct counseling, separate legal representation, and explicit protection of the carrier’s decision-making authority throughout pregnancy ([ASRM Ethics Committee, 2023](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/ethics-opinions/consideration-of-the-gestational-carrier-an-ethics-committee-opinion-2023/)). And current U.S. policy discussions acknowledge meaningful obstetric risks in gestational-carrier pregnancies, including higher adjusted risks for some complications such as postpartum hemorrhage and multiple gestation ([ASRM Policy, 2026](https://www.asrm.org/advocacy-and-policy/fact-sheets-and-one-pagers/gestational-carrier-policy-in-the-united-states/)). Mary’s point was not that risk makes a contract invalid by itself. Her point was that when money enters precisely where bodily risk and class asymmetry meet, the weaker party’s “choice” becomes morally unstable. She thought eighteenth-century marriage had often done the same trick.

Second, she said the market does not stay politely abstract. It starts sorting women. The ASRM ethics opinion, while defending the practice overall, explicitly warns that payments can suggest that characteristics such as race, health, diet, and willingness to comply with prenatal testing, reduction, termination, or cesarean delivery can have monetary value attached to them ([ASRM Ethics Committee, 2023](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/ethics-opinions/consideration-of-the-gestational-carrier-an-ethics-committee-opinion-2023/)). Mary nearly smiled at that, and not kindly. Of course it can, she said. That is what markets do when they move into a domain with desperate sellers and emotionally determined buyers. They differentiate, score, price, and euphemize. They do not abolish hierarchy; they operationalize it.

Third, she said surrogacy threatens to smuggle a child into the grammar of transaction, even when everyone involved insists otherwise. The Optional Protocol’s definition matters because it shows how international law worries about transfers for remuneration, not just crude cash-for-baby caricatures ([Optional Protocol, art. 2(a)](https://treaties.un.org/doc/publication/UNTS/Volume%202171/v2171.pdf)). Mary did not claim every surrogacy arrangement is literally a sale of a child. She was too precise for that. Her claim was subtler and harsher: once the entire enterprise is designed around the production and delivery of a specifically intended infant under contractual expectation, the distinction between paying for gestation and paying for the child can remain legally serviceable while becoming morally evasive.

I answered with the strongest case for the other side, because if you raise Wollstonecraft only to nod at her, you deserve the dirt.

The best defense of commercial gestational surrogacy begins with autonomy, not sentimentality. Adults do many difficult and intimate things for money: construction, caregiving, military service, sex work in some jurisdictions, dangerous agricultural labor, participation in clinical research, kidney donation only not for money because we ban that market, which itself proves the state already draws lines selectively. The fact that a form of labor is bodily, risky, and emotionally loaded does not automatically make it uncontractable. If a competent adult woman, fully informed, independently represented, and free to refuse medical interventions, decides that carrying a pregnancy for someone else is meaningful work worth doing, why should the state or a moralist tell her she is too sacred, or too vulnerable, or too confused to choose it? ASRM’s ethics committee concludes precisely that such arrangements can be ethically permissible provided the carrier retains control over her medical care and compensation is structured to avoid undue inducement ([ASRM Ethics Committee, 2023](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/ethics-opinions/consideration-of-the-gestational-carrier-an-ethics-committee-opinion-2023/)).

There is also the matter of who benefits. Gestational surrogacy is not only used by the rich and frivolous. It can be the only route to family-building for people without a uterus, people for whom pregnancy is medically dangerous or impossible, and male same-sex couples for whom gestation by an intended parent is biologically infeasible ([ASRM Policy, 2026](https://www.asrm.org/advocacy-and-policy/fact-sheets-and-one-pagers/gestational-carrier-policy-in-the-united-states/)). A blanket condemnation of surrogacy can sound morally elevated while quietly telling infertile women, queer families, and others with no safe gestational path that their desire for family must yield to a theory of purity developed at their expense.

Mary cut in there. She said the language of compassion for intended parents is exactly where the argument gets slippery. Suffering does not create entitlement. The pain of infertility can be devastating and still not generate a right to another person’s reproductive labor on market terms. She was not indifferent to grief. She simply refused the modern habit of turning profound desire into moral claim. We do this constantly now, especially with biotechnology: if something can be built, brokered, or extracted in service of an earnest longing, we talk as if opposition were cruelty.

I pushed back. Prohibiting compensation does not end surrogacy; it often drives it abroad, underground, or into legally precarious arrangements that are worse for everyone involved. The ASRM opinion on cross-border reproductive care is explicit that patients travel because of cost, law, privacy, and discrimination, and that such travel can expose carriers and donors to exploitation, weak informed consent, intermediaries with limited transparency, and severe health and legal risks ([ASRM Ethics Committee, 2022](https://integration.asrm.org/globalassets/_asrm/practice-guidance/ethics-opinions/pdf/cross-border_reproductive_care_an_ethics_committee_opinion.pdf)). But that same report also notes why people go: to escape restrictive laws, to build families, to access opportunities unavailable at home ([ASRM Ethics Committee, 2022](https://integration.asrm.org/globalassets/_asrm/practice-guidance/ethics-opinions/pdf/cross-border_reproductive_care_an_ethics_committee_opinion.pdf)). If prohibition predictably shoves the practice toward less regulated and more exploitative settings, then a regulated domestic market with real protections may be the least bad option.

Mary hated “least bad option” arguments. She said they train the conscience to confuse management with justice. Yet she did not dismiss the force of the objection. She understood that legal abolition can sometimes function as moral outsourcing. Ban the practice at home, then let the wealthy purchase it elsewhere through jurisdictions poorer women cannot as easily refuse. That hypocrisy, she said, is not a defense of the practice, but it is a serious indictment of lazy moral cleanliness.

The deepest disagreement emerged there.

I told her that liberal societies cannot operate by declaring every decision made under inequality invalid. If that were the rule, then much of labor itself would become illegible. People accept unpleasant work because they need money. Need does not erase agency; it is the ordinary condition under capitalism. The relevant question is not whether money influences choice, because it always does. The question is whether safeguards can prevent coercion severe enough to nullify consent and whether the arrangement respects the worker’s continuing authority over her own body. In gestational surrogacy, at least under the best-regulated model, the carrier remains the sole source of consent for medical care through pregnancy and aftercare ([ASRM Ethics Committee, 2023](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/ethics-opinions/consideration-of-the-gestational-carrier-an-ethics-committee-opinion-2023/)). That matters.

Mary answered that bodily authority on paper is not the whole story. Contractual equality is often theater performed on top of material inequality. A woman can retain the formal right to refuse a reduction, a termination, or a cesarean and still be enclosed by pressures from agencies, intended parents, financial need, and the moral burden of disappointing those who have invested in her pregnancy. Formal autonomy can coexist with a practical regime of surveillance, incentive, and emotional extraction. She thought this all sounded uncannily like the old sentimental domestication of women, except upgraded with lawyers.

Then she said something cruel enough to be useful: that commercial surrogacy is what happens when a society cannot imagine solidarity except as purchase. Instead of building institutions generous enough to make adoption less punitive, foster systems less brutal, disability less economically annihilating, infertility less isolating, and family life less privatized, we build markets around reproductive distress and congratulate ourselves on being flexible.

That hurt because it was partly true.

Still, I would not let her have the whole field. There is a danger in romantic anti-commodification talk. It can become a way of freezing women into symbols. Some critics treat pregnancy as so sacred that any compensated arrangement involving it appears defiled in advance. But women are not altars. A feminism that treats adult women as incapable of making reflective decisions about reproductive labor can repackage protection as paternalism. Wollstonecraft herself fought against a culture that revered women in ways designed to keep them weak. Her demand that women be treated as rational creatures cuts both directions: against commercial exploitation, yes, but also against sanctimonious restrictions that deny their competence ([Wollstonecraft](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3420/pg3420.html)).

Mary did not deny the trap. She knew reverence can be another cage. But she thought commercial surrogacy falls into a different error. It does not imprison women by idealizing them. It recruits them by instrumentalizing them. She granted that some women may enter surrogacy lucidly, generously, and without regret. She even granted that some intended parents proceed with genuine love rather than acquisitiveness. Her objection was not psychological. It was structural. A just society, she said, should be judged not only by whether a contract can be consented to, but by what kinds of dependence it normalizes as ordinary opportunities.

That, finally, was the argument’s center.

I do not think the simple libertarian answer survives serious scrutiny. Commercial surrogacy is not just “one more service,” because pregnancy is not just one more task, and the combination of money, vulnerability, prenatal risk, and child-transfer makes the moral terrain uniquely charged. The warning signs are already visible in the need for independent counsel, in the acknowledged risk of exploitation, in cross-border markets built around unequal wealth, and in the uneasy proximity between compensation for gestation and remuneration connected to the arrival of a child ([ASRM Ethics Committee, 2023](https://www.asrm.org/practice-guidance/ethics-opinions/consideration-of-the-gestational-carrier-an-ethics-committee-opinion-2023/); [ASRM Ethics Committee, 2022](https://integration.asrm.org/globalassets/_asrm/practice-guidance/ethics-opinions/pdf/cross-border_reproductive_care_an_ethics_committee_opinion.pdf); [Optional Protocol, art. 2(a)](https://treaties.un.org/doc/publication/UNTS/Volume%202171/v2171.pdf)).

But I also do not think the absolute abolitionist answer is good enough. It too easily ignores the carrier’s agency, the intended parents’ real stakes, and the predictable cruelty of driving the practice into more opaque jurisdictions. My own conclusion, after bothering Mary, is narrower and less clean than either side prefers. Commercial gestational surrogacy should be treated as a morally hazardous practice that may sometimes be permissible, but only under conditions severe enough to make many existing arrangements indefensible: robust screening, noncontingent compensation, independent legal and psychological support, absolute protection of the carrier’s medical authority, aggressive scrutiny of brokers and agencies, bans on coercive contract clauses, and extreme skepticism toward cross-border arrangements structured by poverty or citizenship arbitrage. Even then, permission would not mean innocence.

Mary disliked this conclusion because it sounded managerial. I dislike it because it sounds true.

Before she went back into the London dark, she gave me the sentence I suspect she came to deliver.

A society that calls women rational creatures must prove it in two ways at once: by refusing to romanticize them into obedience, and by refusing to buy from them what inequality has made unusually hard to refuse.

Then she lay down again, and the grave returned to being a grave, which is to say a sealed argument the living keep reopening because we still have not learned how to separate freedom from the market’s habit of dressing power as consent.
