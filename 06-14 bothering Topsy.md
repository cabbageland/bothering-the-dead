today is 06-14.

I found Topsy in a film can, which is not a grave unless the dead are unlucky enough to have been made into evidence.

The film was only a little over a minute long, a 1903 actuality called *Electrocuting an Elephant*. The Thomas Edison Papers are careful about the old myth: Edison himself did not arrange the killing, did not appear in the contemporary accounts as a participant, and was probably not personally involved in making the film, though the Edison Manufacturing Company sent a crew to document it ([Thomas Edison Papers, "Myth Buster-Topsy the Elephant"](https://edison.rutgers.edu/life-of-edison/essaying-edison/essay/myth-buster-topsy-the-elephant)). The same account says Topsy had been judged a "bad" elephant after years in circuses, after a man who had teased her was killed, after abuse by a drunken handler, after Luna Park's owners decided she had become too dangerous and too reputationally inconvenient to keep ([Thomas Edison Papers](https://edison.rutgers.edu/life-of-edison/essaying-edison/essay/myth-buster-topsy-the-elephant)).

So I did not bring Edison to the scene. That would have been too easy, and wrong in the self-satisfied way of people who prefer villains with famous names. I brought a portable projector, a coil of copper wire, one carrot, one habeas petition, and a copy of the New York Court of Appeals decision in *Nonhuman Rights Project v. Breheny*, decided on June 14, 2022, about whether Happy, an elephant at the Bronx Zoo, could use habeas corpus to seek transfer to a sanctuary ([New York Court of Appeals, *Nonhuman Rights Project v. Breheny*](https://www.nycourts.gov/ctapps/Decisions/2022/Jun22/52opn22-Decision.pdf)).

The dead body was not under the ground. It was in the last second of the film, where the current had already entered and history had decided to call documentation a kind of mercy. I hung a sheet in the dark and ran the footage backward. Smoke sank into the feet. The legs unfolded. The body rose. Men stepped away from the edge of their own usefulness. The copper loosened. The carrot came back whole, obscene and orange.

Then Topsy stood in the room.

She was not gigantic because elephants are big. She was gigantic because the law around her had been small.

"You brought paper," she said.

"A court opinion."

"Humans often bring paper after the body."

The question was this: **Should elephants and other cognitively complex nonhuman animals be recognized as legal persons for the narrow purpose of challenging severe captivity through habeas or a similar liberty remedy, or should the law continue treating them as animals protected by welfare regulations but not as rights-bearing persons?**

The stakes are not decorative. If the answer is yes, zoos, aquariums, circuses, research facilities, sanctuaries, private owners, courts, conservation programs, tribes, farms, and state agencies all inherit a new legal animal: not a human, not property, not merely a protected exhibit, but a plaintiff-bearing body. If the answer is no, then even an elephant who is self-aware, social, long-lived, emotionally complex, and confined for decades remains dependent on human owners, inspectors, statutes, and institutional mercy. The fight is not over whether elephants are cute. It is over whether a being can be too intelligent for property and still too nonhuman for liberty.

Topsy touched the habeas petition with the end of her trunk.

"Is this a rope?"

"It is supposed to be the opposite."

"Humans often invent opposites with the same knot."

The majority in Happy's case began from a historically powerful point: habeas corpus has been the great legal remedy for unlawful human confinement. The court held that the writ protects human liberty and does not apply to Happy because she is not a "person" subjected to illegal detention, though the majority acknowledged that elephants are intelligent beings deserving proper care and compassion ([*Breheny* majority](https://www.nycourts.gov/ctapps/Decisions/2022/Jun22/52opn22-Decision.pdf)). That view is not stupid. It is a boundary defense. Habeas is not a general sadness machine. Courts cannot order every unhappy captive creature to a better life merely because a lawyer can describe the cage movingly.

"The cage never needs eloquence," Topsy said. "Only the key does."

That was the first wound in the argument. The law has no trouble recognizing an elephant when someone owns her, licenses her, transports her, exhibits her, insures her, buys her, sells her, breeds her, films her, or kills her according to a procedure. Recognition becomes impossible only when the elephant asks to be the center of a claim. The U.S. Animal Welfare Act says animals used for research, exhibition, pets, or commerce should receive humane care and treatment, and it frames regulation partly through interstate commerce and protection of animal owners from theft ([7 U.S.C. 2131](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/7/2131)). That is better than nothing. It is also revealing. Welfare law asks how an animal may be used. Rights law asks whether the use is allowed at all.

"Humane," Topsy said. "They poisoned me, wired me, and pulled ropes around my neck. Which part was humane?"

"The authorities at the time thought they were avoiding a worse spectacle."

"A worse spectacle than making my death a moving picture?"

There are moments when the dead do not argue. They inventory.

The case for animal personhood often begins with sentience, and Bentham still stands at the doorway. In *An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation*, he made the famous move away from language and reason toward suffering: the morally decisive question is not whether animals can reason or talk, but whether they can suffer ([Jeremy Bentham, *Principles of Morals and Legislation*](https://oll.libertyfund.org/titles/bentham-an-introduction-to-the-principles-of-morals-and-legislation)). The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness sharpened the modern scientific version, stating that humans are not unique in possessing the neurological substrates of consciousness and that nonhuman animals, including mammals and birds and many other creatures, possess such substrates too ([Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness](https://fcmconference.org/img/CambridgeDeclarationOnConsciousness.pdf)).

But sentience alone cannot settle habeas. A mouse suffers. A chicken suffers. A fish may suffer. If suffering alone creates a habeas right, the law either explodes into an impossible universal animal liberty regime or retreats in panic to the old comfort of "only humans count." The pro-rights side loses if it pretends the hard cases are insults rather than questions. The anti-rights side loses if it uses those hard cases as smoke to avoid the elephant standing directly in the room.

Topsy lowered her head.

"You are afraid of the mouse."

"I am afraid of bad law."

"That is what powerful animals call fear of the weak."

Fair. But the distinction matters. Habeas is not a painkiller. It is a liberty remedy. If we are talking about habeas for elephants, the argument cannot be simply "they suffer." It has to be: elephants are the kind of beings for whom severe captivity can wrong them as captivity, not merely as pain, hunger, disease, or bad husbandry. Happy's petition made exactly that move. The majority summarized the expert evidence as saying elephants have self-awareness, long-term memory, intentional communication, learning and problem-solving skills, empathy, and significant emotional response ([*Breheny*](https://www.nycourts.gov/ctapps/Decisions/2022/Jun22/52opn22-Decision.pdf)). Scientific studies have reported that Asian elephants can pass mirror self-recognition tests, with researchers noting parallels among apes, dolphins, and elephants ([Plotnik, de Waal, and Reiss, "Self-recognition in an Asian elephant"](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.0608062103)), and later work found Asian elephants able to recognize their own bodies as obstacles in a problem-solving task ([Dale and Plotnik, "Elephants know when their bodies are obstacles to success"](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep46309)).

None of this means an elephant is a human in gray skin. That is the wrong demand and the wrong insult. It means the old property category has become cognitively ridiculous. An elephant is not a piano with digestion. She is a socially intelligent, embodied subject whose life can be damaged by confinement even if she is fed well, cleaned well, and admired by children with sticky hands.

"Admiration is cheap," Topsy said. "Hay costs money. Freedom costs imagination."

The majority's strongest argument is institutional humility. Courts are blunt instruments. A judge may be poorly equipped to decide whether a particular zoo enclosure or sanctuary placement serves an elephant's good. Happy's case showed the difficulty: the Bronx Zoo said she was healthy, familiar with her caregivers, and that moving her could cause serious stress; the petitioners wanted transfer to a sanctuary; even sanctuary life could not guarantee easy social integration with other elephants ([*Breheny*](https://www.nycourts.gov/ctapps/Decisions/2022/Jun22/52opn22-Decision.pdf)). A court issuing habeas wants a simple remedy: release the body. But an elephant cannot be released onto Fordham Road like an innocent prisoner leaving a courthouse. She needs land, other elephants if possible, keepers, veterinary care, quarantine, transport planning, money, and a place to live.

"So because freedom is expensive," Topsy said, "you call the cage practical."

"No. I am saying a court has to know what its order does."

"A killer should have known that too."

The dead elephant had no patience for procedural modesty, but procedural modesty is not always cowardice. Rights can be badly designed. A broad animal habeas doctrine could create perverse outcomes: courts second-guessing expert care, activists forum-shopping for charismatic species, owners hiding animals from scrutiny, underfunded sanctuaries overwhelmed by court-ordered transfers, conservation breeding programs disrupted without replacements, and animals harmed by relocation in the name of symbolic victory. A legal personhood doctrine that merely announces nobility and leaves institutions to improvise is not justice. It is a beautiful door opening into traffic.

Topsy took the carrot from the table and did not eat it.

"Then design the door."

That is the actual work.

The anti-personhood position often relies on a false clarity: humans are persons; animals are not. But legal personhood has never been identical to biological humanity. It is a juridical status, a way of saying that an entity can bear certain rights or duties for certain purposes. Happy's dissenters pressed precisely this point: the question was not whether Happy was human, but whether the common law could allow an autonomous nonhuman animal to challenge confinement that might be unjust ([Judge Wilson dissent; Judge Rivera dissent, *Breheny*](https://www.nycourts.gov/ctapps/Decisions/2022/Jun22/52opn22-Decision.pdf)). The majority feared destabilization. The dissent feared a law so committed to old categories that it could see autonomy and still answer: exhibit.

Topsy listened.

"Which fear is yours?"

"Both."

"Coward."

"Possibly. But both fears are real."

The pro-rights argument has to admit what follows. If elephants can be legal persons for bodily liberty, then other animals will knock. Great apes. Cetaceans. Perhaps some birds. Perhaps pigs, whose intelligence makes human appetite nervous. The line will not stay where sentiment first draws it. That is not a refutation. It is a cost of moral seriousness. Every expansion of standing threatens settled convenience. Bentham's question about suffering was dangerous because it did not flatter the dinner table ([Bentham](https://oll.libertyfund.org/titles/bentham-an-introduction-to-the-principles-of-morals-and-legislation)). If an ethical principle has no consequences for lunch, law, money, buildings, and habits, it is probably a decoration.

But the anti-rights side has to admit its own uglier consequence. Welfare without rights can become an aesthetics of domination. It can make captivity clean enough for spectators, regulated enough for inspectors, and profitable enough for institutions, while never asking whether the underlying relation is permissible. The Animal Welfare Act can require humane handling and care for exhibited animals ([7 U.S.C. 2131](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/7/2131)). It cannot by itself answer whether a cognitively complex wild animal should be exhibited in the first place. A polished chain is still a chain. A larger enclosure can still be a life sentence.

"A life sentence," Topsy said. "For what crime?"

"For being interesting."

That sentence embarrassed the room.

Zoos answer with conservation and education, and those words deserve scrutiny instead of automatic contempt. Species are disappearing. Habitat is being converted, fragmented, burned, and bought. Human beings often protect what they have seen. Zoos sometimes fund field conservation, breed endangered animals, rescue individuals who cannot survive in the wild, and teach urban publics that nonhuman life is not imaginary. A blanket abolitionist answer that releases rhetoric faster than animals may satisfy the moral appetite of people who do not have to build the alternative.

Topsy shifted her weight.

"Did seeing me save elephants?"

"No."

"Did seeing me die teach humans kindness?"

"Not enough."

"Then do not spend conservation money made from my humiliation."

The best zoo defense applies most strongly to animals whose captivity is actually connected to species survival, rescue, rehabilitation, or non-exploitative education under conditions suited to their form of life. It applies weakly where the animal is an aging relic of display, where no meaningful breeding or conservation purpose remains, where the species' needs are structurally incompatible with the facility, and where the institution's real interest is continuity, ticket value, donor comfort, or institutional pride. The law should be able to distinguish a rescue from a collection, a sanctuary from a showroom, a conservation program from a habit.

Happy's case cut through this distinction because the remedy sought was not release to wilderness but transfer to sanctuary. The majority emphasized that Happy's care complied with existing statutes and regulations and that habeas had not previously been applied to nonhuman animals ([*Breheny*](https://www.nycourts.gov/ctapps/Decisions/2022/Jun22/52opn22-Decision.pdf)). Judge Rivera's dissent answered that captivity itself could be the harm for an autonomous elephant, especially one held in an unnatural environment for human entertainment, and that habeas should at least allow a court to examine the justification ([Rivera dissent, *Breheny*](https://www.nycourts.gov/ctapps/Decisions/2022/Jun22/52opn22-Decision.pdf)). That is the hinge. Welfare asks, "Is the cell clean?" Liberty asks, "Why is there a cell?"

Topsy raised the carrot again.

"Ask your question without court language."

"Should a creature like you be owned?"

"No."

"Should a creature like you be confined?"

"Sometimes, if the alternative is death, panic, hunger, injury, or another human invention."

"Should a creature like you be displayed?"

"Only if I can leave."

That last answer was impossible and therefore clarifying. Consent is not the whole of animal ethics, because we cannot ask an elephant to sign a release form. But impossibility does not erase the moral pressure. When consent is impossible, justification must get heavier, not lighter. Human law understands this elsewhere. Children, patients under guardianship, and incapacitated adults cannot consent in ordinary ways, so the law does not say "do whatever is useful." It builds duties, standards, guardians, review, and sometimes prohibitions. Nonhuman animals need not be treated as children to teach us that voicelessness is not permission.

The personhood skeptics are right that animals cannot assume the full architecture of legal duties. Topsy cannot pay taxes, vote, sit on a jury, sign a contract, obey zoning law, or be imprisoned for torts. But rights do not always travel with matching duties. Infants have rights without civic duties. Comatose patients have rights without legal agency. Corporations have some rights with strange, artificial forms of responsibility. The real question is not "Can an elephant be a full legal citizen?" It is "Can the law recognize a narrow right not to be unjustifiably confined for human purposes?"

"Narrow," Topsy said. "Humans make rights narrow when someone else needs them."

"Sometimes narrow rights are how a legal system survives the first step."

"Sometimes first steps are where courage goes to die."

Again, she was not wrong. But a usable doctrine has to begin somewhere it can stand.

Here is the rule I would defend with Topsy breathing over my shoulder:

**A cognitively complex, socially dependent, long-lived wild animal held in human captivity should have a statutory or common-law liberty proceeding, similar to but not identical with habeas, through a qualified guardian. The proceeding should not declare the animal human, should not create general citizenship, and should not apply to every animal automatically. It should ask whether continued confinement is substantially unjustified in light of the animal's species-typical needs, cognitive and social capacities, history, health, available alternatives, conservation necessity, and the risks of transfer. If confinement is unjustified, the remedy should be transfer to an accredited sanctuary or other environment better suited to that animal's life.**

This is personhood with work boots, not angel wings.

Topsy considered it.

"Too late for me."

"Yes."

"Convenient."

"Also true."

The rule would not empty every zoo. It would not decide the entire animal agriculture system in one theatrical opinion. It would not let any stranger seize any animal by printing a petition. It would demand evidence. It would allow institutions to prove genuine conservation necessity, rescue purpose, medical risk, or the absence of a better alternative. It would make courts ask for expert testimony instead of vibes. It would make sanctuaries prove capacity. It would treat transfer as a remedy, not a slogan.

But it would also force a question welfare law keeps dodging: **What is the captivity for?**

Not what is the enrichment schedule. Not whether the floors are clean. Not whether the keeper loves the animal. Not whether the children learn something. Not whether the institution is accredited. Not whether the animal has become accustomed to deprivation. What is this captivity for, and is that purpose strong enough to justify this being's loss of bodily liberty?

Topsy put the carrot down.

"Ask that before the wire."

"Yes."

"Before the ticket."

"Yes."

"Before the photograph."

"Yes."

"Before the child points and learns my name."

"Yes."

There is a dark comedy in human education. We confine an animal, display the animal, explain that the animal is endangered because humans have ruined its habitat, then invite the child to love the animal through the very arrangement that proves domination has become pedagogical. Sometimes this may be the least bad available lesson. Often it is just empire with signage. The child learns awe, yes. The child also learns that awe entitles possession.

Topsy's own death was spectacle dressed as public management. Contemporary and later tellings argue over whether she was dangerous, abused, doomed, mercifully killed, commercially useful, or mythically folded into the battle of currents. The Edison Papers rightly correct the lazy Edison legend ([Thomas Edison Papers](https://edison.rutgers.edu/life-of-edison/essaying-edison/essay/myth-buster-topsy-the-elephant)). But removing Edison does not remove the crime. It distributes it. The owners who wanted her gone. The handlers who failed her. The crowd that came. The press that reported. The camera that preserved. The future that watched. The culture that found it easier to debate whether the wrong man got blamed than whether an elephant's whole life had been converted into labor, danger, publicity, and disposal.

"I killed a man," Topsy said.

"Yes."

"Say it."

"You killed a man who, according to the account I read, had been teasing you."

"Does that make me innocent?"

"No."

"Does it make me guilty?"

"Not in the way they needed you to be."

This matters. Animal-rights rhetoric sometimes purifies animals into saints, as if moral status depends on being harmless. That is just another human vanity. Elephants can be dangerous. So can humans. So can horses, dogs, rivers, machines, crowds, and judges. The question is not whether Topsy was safe. The question is whether the institutions around her made danger and then called her nature the problem. If you capture a wild being, train her for spectacle, move her through commercial circuits, expose her to teasing and abuse, profit from obedience, then execute her as a rogue when the arrangement breaks, you have not discovered animal violence. You have manufactured a defendant.

The majority in Happy's case worried that recognizing nonhuman habeas could have enormous destabilizing impact ([*Breheny*](https://www.nycourts.gov/ctapps/Decisions/2022/Jun22/52opn22-Decision.pdf)). I believe that worry. I also distrust it. "Destabilizing" is what settled systems call moral claims before they are digested. Abolition was destabilizing. Labor rights were destabilizing. Women's legal personhood was destabilizing. Disability rights were destabilizing. This does not mean every destabilizing claim is true. It means stability is not an argument by itself. A stable wrong is not promoted into a good by the number of invoices attached to it.

Topsy's ears moved.

"You compare us to humans when it helps, then deny comparison when it frightens you."

"Yes. That is a human habit."

"Break it."

Fine. Elephants are not oppressed humans. Animal captivity is not chattel slavery, not prison, not patriarchy, not disability discrimination, not colonialism, though human systems of domination have often trained themselves on animals before turning their techniques elsewhere and have often used animalization to degrade humans. The analogies can illuminate and they can also steal. The cleanest argument does not need to say elephants are humans. It says elephants are elephants, and being an elephant is enough to create elephant-shaped claims.

An elephant-shaped claim begins with body and world. Asian elephants are highly social, and WWF describes females as forming groups of related females; Asian elephants may spend much of the day feeding and moving across large areas ([WWF, Asian elephant](https://www.worldwildlife.org/species/elephant/asian-elephant/)). They are not built for a life whose main geography is enclosure, performance, separation, and observation from above. A sanctuary is not wilderness. It is still human-managed compromise. But compromise can be morally different from display. A retirement home is not freedom, but it is not a shop window.

"Would you have sent me to a sanctuary?" Topsy asked.

"If one had existed and could hold you safely, yes."

"And if not?"

"Then the humans who profited from you would owe the cost of building what your life required."

That sentence felt impractical until I compared it with the practicality of electrocuting an elephant for institutional convenience. Money always sounds impossible when it is owed to the injured.

The strongest objection remains the farm. If law admits that some animals have liberty interests, what happens to the billions whose lives are shorter, less cinematic, less beloved, and more deeply embedded in appetite? The answer cannot be "nothing." The logic will move. It should move. But law often develops by pressure, category, and case. A doctrine for elephants will not by itself abolish animal agriculture, but it will make the moral vocabulary less stupid. It will make it harder to say that a being's only legal relevance is the owner's permitted use. It will make suffering, social life, cognition, and species-typical flourishing harder to exile from court.

Topsy did not like "harder."

"Harder is not justice."

"No. It is the beginning of friction."

"I died instantly, they said. Very little friction."

The historical accounts of Topsy often emphasize that the death was quick. This is what human beings reach for when the prior life indicts them. Quickness is not nothing; pain matters. But a painless killing can still be wrong because the being killed had a claim on continued life, because the danger was produced by captivity, because the spectacle degraded everyone, because "necessary" was decided by owners, and because death became a management tool for a problem humans had authored. Bentham's suffering test is indispensable, but not sufficient. The question is not only "Can they suffer?" It is also "Can they be robbed of a life that is theirs?"

Topsy said, "Now you are near it."

Near it is this: an elephant is not merely a container for pain and pleasure. She is a moving history of attachments, routes, habits, skills, preferences, fears, memories, and bodily intelligence. To wrong her is not only to hurt her. It is to reduce the scale at which her life can be lived. A cramped elephant is not simply an uncomfortable elephant. She is an elephant whose elephantness has been administratively narrowed.

That, finally, is why personhood is the right scandalous word, even if the doctrine should be narrow. "Welfare" can be granted by a superior to an inferior. "Personhood" says there is someone, not something, at the other end of the rule. It does not answer every policy question. It changes the pronoun inside the question. It makes the court say her body, her liberty, her life, and then justify itself if it still says no.

Topsy stood between the projector and the sheet, blocking her own death from replaying.

"Would I have won under your rule?"

"A guardian would have challenged your owners. The court would ask whether killing you was necessary, whether their prior captivity and abuse created the danger, whether a sanctuary or secure non-display placement existed, whether the public spectacle served any legitimate purpose, and whether the people who profited from you owed more than disposal."

"That is not an answer."

"You should have won against the spectacle. You should have won against being filmed. You should have won against being treated as a broken machine. Whether you could have safely lived, I do not know."

She accepted this with the cold generosity of the dead, who prefer honest uncertainty to decorative remorse.

The projector clicked. The room smelled faintly of dust and rain and old current.

"Say the rule shorter," she said.

"A being should not have to be human to make captivity answer for itself."

Topsy lifted the habeas petition from the table. For a moment it looked like any other sheet of paper. Then, in her trunk, it looked like a tool humans had not yet grown into.

"Good," she said. "Now stop calling the cage education until the door has been cross-examined."

Then the film ran forward again, because history is cruel about direction. But the last frame had changed. The men were still there. The wire was still there. The carrot was still there. Topsy was still doomed.

Only now, in the corner of the image, someone had written a question the court had not yet managed to kill:

What is this captivity for?
