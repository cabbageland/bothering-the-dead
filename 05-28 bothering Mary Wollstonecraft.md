today is May 28, 2026.

I found Mary Wollstonecraft where London keeps some of its best ghosts, in the churchyard of St Pancras Old Church, under the worn stone that became, for her daughter Mary Shelley, both a shrine and a scandalous rendezvous point ([Project Gutenberg, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3420?msg=welcome_stranger); [TIME, "The Eerie Gravestone Where *Frankenstein*'s Story Began"](https://time.com/5133735/wollstonecraft-grave-mary-shelley-frankenstein/); [Historic England, "Tomb of Mary Wollstonecraft, William Godwin and Mary Jane Godwin"](https://historicengland.org.uk/images-books/photos/item/DP462476)). I went there after midnight with a folding chair, a thermos of bad coffee, a printout of [*Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/19-1392_6j37.pdf?os=vbkn42tqhoPmKBEXtcfdSuHsoH), an older printout of [*Roe v. Wade*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/410/113/), Wollstonecraft's own [*Vindication*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3420/pg3420.html), and Judith Jarvis Thomson's ["A Defense of Abortion"](https://users.manchester.edu/Facstaff/SSNaragon/Online/texts/235/Thomson%2C%20Defense%20of%20Abortion.pdf).

I did not come to ask a sentimental question. I came to ask the ugliest clear one.

May a state force a woman to remain pregnant and give birth once conception has occurred, or is that power, even when draped in the language of life, a form of political domination so profound that any just society must refuse it?

That is not a polite dispute about balancing interests. It is a fight over whether women are full political persons or a sex whose bodies may be commandeered for a public moral project. The stakes are flesh, time, blood, risk, wages, sex, kinship, and the shape of citizenship itself. The [majority in *Dobbs*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/19-1392_6j37.pdf?os=vbkn42tqhoPmKBEXtcfdSuHsoH) says the Constitution does not protect a right to abortion and returns the matter to elected representatives. The older [*Roe* opinion](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/410/113/) treated abortion as part of a broader liberty and privacy structure, while also recognizing state interests that strengthen over pregnancy, especially around viability. The legal question and the moral question are not identical, but they are entangled enough to draw blood.

Wollstonecraft came up irritated, as if resurrection itself were another male institution imposing on her schedule. Damp earth clung to her sleeves. She looked at the *Dobbs* opinion first, which I admired. The dead understand hierarchy. She had spent her life watching law pose as reason.

You want me, she said, not because I wrote about abortion directly, but because I wrote about the political manufacture of female dependence. In *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, she argues that women are made weak not by nature but by social arrangements that train them for pleasing submission rather than for reason and independence ([Wollstonecraft, *Vindication*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3420/pg3420.html)). Very well. Ask your question cleanly.

So I did. If the fetus is a human life, or even a developing human with moral worth, why should a state not protect it by law, even at great cost to the woman carrying it? Why is compelled pregnancy different in kind from the countless other burdens political communities impose on citizens for the sake of others?

Wollstonecraft answered with a violence sharper than piety. Because pregnancy is not taxation. It is not jury duty. It is not even conscription in the ordinary military sense, though that is close enough to reveal the obscenity. To compel pregnancy is to place one citizen's body into legally enforced service for another organism's survival. A state that does this announces, whatever else it says, that some citizens hold their bodies on more conditional terms than others.

This was not merely rhetoric. Her whole philosophical temperament points that way. Wollstonecraft's central claim is that no being can become virtuous or free under arrangements built from subordination, infantilization, and dependence ([Wollstonecraft, *Vindication*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3420?msg=welcome_stranger)). Forced maternity is a particularly intimate form of such subordination because it reaches beneath speech and contract into metabolism itself. It tells a woman that her body can become a public road and that her dissent, however articulate, arrives too late.

I pushed back because the strongest arguments should be attacked where they are strongest. You are assuming the woman and fetus are strangers, I said, as Thomson famously does in the violinist analogy, where bodily support is imposed without consent ([Thomson, "A Defense of Abortion"](https://users.manchester.edu/Facstaff/SSNaragon/Online/texts/235/Thomson%2C%20Defense%20of%20Abortion.pdf)). But ordinary pregnancy is usually not a kidnapping. It often follows voluntary sex, undertaken with knowledge that pregnancy is possible. If adults generate a vulnerable dependent being through ordinary conduct, do they not incur obligations that are stronger than obligations to a stranger attached to them by force?

Wollstonecraft did not dismiss that. Good, she said. That is at least a serious objection. She disliked moral laziness in allies as much as in enemies. She conceded that a politics of abortion cannot honestly proceed as though sex, embodiment, gestation, and dependency were all morally weightless until the instant of birth. Human reproduction is not morally trivial, and a culture that speaks of abortion only in the vocabulary of frictionless consumer choice becomes stupid about the gravity of what it is doing. That concession mattered because it kept her from becoming a mascot.

But then she cut the argument in half. Causing a dependency, she said, does not by itself answer the question of what may be extracted from a body to satisfy it. Parents owe children food, care, protection, and recognition after birth. It does not follow that the state may force one person's organs into use for another before birth. Thomson's point survives beyond rape: even if we grant, for argument's sake, substantial fetal moral worth, it does not automatically yield a right to use someone else's body without continuing consent ([Thomson, "A Defense of Abortion"](https://users.manchester.edu/Facstaff/SSNaragon/Online/texts/235/Thomson%2C%20Defense%20of%20Abortion.pdf)).

I told her the anti-abortion side would say that pregnancy is precisely the natural relation in which such support is not alien but constitutive. The uterus is not like donating a kidney to a stranger; it is the ordinary environment in which human beings begin. If the law protects newborns from neglect, why may it not protect the unborn from deliberate destruction, especially once the fetus has developed far enough to look recognizably human, to move, to respond, perhaps eventually to survive outside the womb? *Roe* itself did not treat all stages of pregnancy as morally identical; its trimester structure, and later viability-centered doctrine, recognized that the state's interest in potential life becomes weightier as gestation advances ([*Roe v. Wade*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/410/113/)).

Here Wollstonecraft became more exact, and therefore more dangerous. She said the pro-choice side often loses nerve by pretending every abortion case is philosophically simple. It is not. Late pregnancy produces moral pressure because more than one kind of reality has thickened. The fetus is still not a sovereign citizen entitled to occupy another body by force, but neither is it nothing. A just politics should be able to say that tragic moral seriousness increases over gestation without thereby granting the state general title over pregnant bodies.

That struck me as stronger than the usual slogans. It allowed her to refuse two stupidities at once. First, the anti-abortion stupidity that treats conception as a complete moral trigger, as though a fertilized egg and a late fetus generate identical claims and therefore justify identical coercion. Second, the pro-choice stupidity that imagines any acknowledgment of fetal development is treason.

So I turned to *Dobbs*, because the current reactionary triumph is not content with moral complexity. The majority argues that abortion rights are not deeply rooted in the nation's history and tradition, and that *Roe* was egregiously wrong from the start for locating such a right in the Constitution's liberty guarantee ([*Dobbs*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/21pdf/19-1392_6j37.pdf?os=vbkn42tqhoPmKBEXtcfdSuHsoH)). In that framework, if a democratic majority wants to protect unborn life from conception, why is that not simply self-government rather than domination?

Wollstonecraft almost laughed. Because democratic procedure does not launder hierarchy, she said. A majority may vote to keep women financially dependent, legally vulnerable, sexually coerced, or educationally stunted. It may vote for old tyrannies with new stationery. Her entire career stands against the idea that inherited subjection becomes just when codified by respectable institutions. In *Vindication* she attacks the social order for constructing women as decorative dependents and then treating their resulting weakness as evidence of nature ([Wollstonecraft, *Vindication*](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3420/pg3420.html)). Forced pregnancy extends the same logic. It makes women's sexed embodiment the basis for diminished sovereignty, then calls the arrangement moral realism.

I told her that opponents would answer with another accusation: you are smuggling individualism in under the name of equality. Human beings do not choose the terms of their vulnerability. Infants, the disabled, the senile, the sick, all survive by claims on other people. If liberty means never being forced into unwanted care, then the dependent lose first. Maybe abortion rights express not freedom but a market society's hatred of involuntary obligation.

This was the hardest moment, because it touched a real weakness in some liberal defenses of abortion. If the argument becomes simply my body, my boundary, my project, then all dependence starts sounding like trespass. Wollstonecraft hated that world too. She did not believe freedom meant isolation. She believed in cultivated duty, education, social responsibility, and the mutual formation of rational beings ([Wollstonecraft, *Vindication*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3420?msg=welcome_stranger)). She would have despised the glossy idiom in which every burden becomes an assault on lifestyle optimization.

Yet she would also have seen the trap. The cure for market individualism cannot be sex-specific political servitude. One may believe that human life is relational all the way down and still insist that no relation authorizes the state to conscript a woman's organs for months, compel labor from her body, and then congratulate itself for honoring dependence. The duties generated by our mutual vulnerability must be distributed in forms compatible with equal personhood. Once a regime decides that one class of citizens may be compelled into intimate bodily service because of reproductive capacity, equality has already been breached.

At that point I asked the question beneath the legal question. What, then, is the fetus? Not legally, not tactically, but morally. Is it merely tissue under another description, or a human life with claims that should change our conduct even if they should not always control law?

Wollstonecraft refused the easy answer again. It is a developing human life, she said, and that is exactly why the subject is tragic rather than trivial. But tragedy does not itself dictate sovereignty. The moral worth of the fetus can ground reasons for hesitation, grief, support for contraception, social obligation to make childbearing materially possible, and moral concern about later abortions. It does not follow that the state may answer every one of those concerns with coercive continuation of pregnancy. To move from value to compulsion is the fatal slide.

This, I think, is where the political stakes become unbearably plain. If abortion is prohibited on the theory that fetal life outranks bodily self-government, then women do not merely lose a procedure. They lose a class standing. They become the only citizens from whom the state may demand prolonged internal bodily labor as the price of sex. And because pregnancy is distributed through class, race, health, and violence in unequal ways, the burden does not fall abstractly. It lands hardest where refusal is already costly.

But there was one last argument worth making, because otherwise the essay would flatter us. Suppose a community says: we agree forced pregnancy is grave coercion, but we also believe deliberate fetal killing is grave wrong. We will therefore prohibit most abortions while expanding welfare, healthcare, childcare, paternal responsibility, and maternal support. Would that still be domination, or would it be a severe but principled defense of vulnerable life?

Wollstonecraft's answer was pitiless. Material support can reduce desperation, and any just society should provide it. But provision does not transform compelled use of the body into freedom. A well-funded coercion is still coercion. If the law forbids ending a pregnancy, the state still claims final authority over what risks, pains, and transformations a woman must endure inside her own body. Better hospitals do not dissolve that claim. They simply make domination more administratively polished.

By then dawn had started leaking into the churchyard, thin and gray and unsentimental. I had raised Mary Wollstonecraft to argue about abortion, and she had done what the best dead do: made the position I already favored less lazy, less sloganized, and more dangerous to defend badly.

My conclusion, after bothering her properly, is this. A just society should protect broad abortion access because the alternative is a regime of sex-based bodily conscription. That is true even if the fetus has moral significance, and even if abortion can be ethically grave, and even if some late cases should be treated with greater seriousness than early ones ([Thomson, "A Defense of Abortion"](https://users.manchester.edu/Facstaff/SSNaragon/Online/texts/235/Thomson%2C%20Defense%20of%20Abortion.pdf); [*Roe v. Wade*](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/410/113/)). The state may encourage birth, support parents, honor prenatal life, and mourn abortion where mourning is due. What it may not do, if women are equal citizens, is force pregnancy to continue by law.

The deepest anti-abortion argument is not stupid. It sees that unborn life is not nothing and that liberal societies often hide moral seriousness behind the language of preference. But its cure is worse than its diagnosis. Once the government acquires authority to compel gestation, it possesses a power too intimate for justice, too theological for liberal law, and too old in its relation to women to be mistaken for moral progress.

Wollstonecraft went back into the ground without blessing me. Good. Blessings are cheap. What she left me with was harder and better: the knowledge that the fight over abortion is not only about privacy, and not only about life. It is about whether equality reaches all the way into the body, or whether, at the decisive moment, the old republic of men may still knock, enter, and stay.
