today is May 10, 2026.

I found Gunther Anders in Vienna by following the logic of shame.

Not his philosophy first, not his biography, not the long afterimage he cast over twentieth-century arguments about technology and moral scale. I mean I found the actual dead man by going to the Zentralfriedhof in a thin gray morning, with wet gravel underfoot and the kind of quiet that makes every deliberate act feel like an ethical disclosure. I had brought no flowers. Flowers would have been sentimental, and Anders hated sentimentality when it functioned as anesthesia. I brought a small quadcopter instead, its plastic arms folded inward like a trapped insect, and I set it on the stone above him. Then I read aloud the sentence that seemed most likely to irritate him back into the world: “In order to suppress the production of the means of destruction, we must try to produce something: namely, fear” ([Gunther Anders, “Theses for the Atomic Age,” trans. by Robert Jungk, in *The Massachusetts Review* 3, no. 3, 1962, JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/25087034)).

The drone began to tremble before I finished the citation. Dirt shifted. A hand appeared, not theatrically, not with apocalypse in it, but with the exhausted impatience of someone who had been dead for decades and could already tell the living still had not learned enough. Anders pulled himself out in a dark coat powdered with grave dust, looked at the drone, looked at me, and said, “What have you automatized now?”

I told him I had come for an argument about autonomous weapons, specifically whether states should accept a binding international ban on weapon systems that can select and attack human targets without meaningful human control. This is no longer speculative. The United States Department of Defense defines autonomous and semi-autonomous weapon systems in its current directive and governs their development rather than prohibiting them outright ([U.S. Department of Defense Directive 3000.09, updated 2023](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodd/300009p.pdf)). The Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons has spent years debating the issue without reaching a binding instrument ([UN Office for Disarmament Affairs, CCW Group of Governmental Experts on LAWS](https://disarmament.unoda.org/the-convention-on-certain-conventional-weapons/background-on-laws-in-the-ccw/)). Meanwhile, military AI contractors and defense ministries keep speaking the language of precision, speed, and force protection. The controversial question was plain enough to wake a corpse: should machines ever be allowed to decide, on their own, that a human being is the right object for lethal violence?

Anders stepped down from the grave as if entering an overdue appointment. “You ask the wrong ‘ever,’” he said. “The real question is what kind of species invents such an option at all.”

We walked out of the cemetery and into one of those impossible settings the resurrected seem able to summon, a bombed-out lecture hall reconstructed as a tribunal. The windows were missing. Rain entered without hurry. On the table between us sat the little quadcopter, inert and accusatory.

I gave him the strongest case against my own instinct, because otherwise there is no point in raising the dead. States claim autonomous systems can react faster than humans in conditions saturated by incoming data. The Pentagon has argued that autonomy can permit weapon systems to operate with greater precision and reduced risk to U.S. personnel when properly designed and supervised ([DoD Directive 3000.09](https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/DD/issuances/dodd/300009p.pdf)). Some analysts also argue that defensive autonomous systems already exist in forms such as missile interception, where machine-speed response is not a luxury but a condition of success. Israel’s Iron Dome and the U.S. Navy’s Aegis systems are often invoked in this register, where automated response is framed as protection rather than morally novel murder ([Congressional Research Service, “Defense Primer: U.S. Policy on Lethal Autonomous Weapon Systems,” 2023](https://crsreports.congress.gov/product/pdf/IF/IF11150)). And if rival powers develop such systems regardless, a unilateral refusal may leave more restrained states vulnerable to less restrained ones.

Anders listened with the expression of a man forced to hear the same clever mistake in a new accent. “Yes,” he said, “that is always how it comes. First the machine is justified as aid, then as shield, then as inevitability.” He tapped the drone with one finger. “The scandal is not only that killing becomes easier. It is that the disproportion between our power to make effects and our capacity to imagine them becomes normal.” This was the center of his old argument in *The Obsolescence of Human Beings*: modern technology gives us capacities whose consequences exceed the scale of our moral imagination, leaving us, as he put it, “inverted utopians,” able to produce what we cannot adequately conceive or feel responsible for ([Gunther Anders, *Die Antiquiertheit des Menschen*, Vol. I, Beck, 1956; summary and excerpts via Los Angeles Review of Books discussion](https://lareviewofbooks.org/article/gunther-anders-and-the-shame-of-being-human/)).

I pushed back. Plenty of military institutions already create moral distance. Artillery crews do not see faces. Submarine officers kill at unimaginable range. Bureaucracies fragment responsibility on purpose. If distance itself were decisive, modern war would already be morally impossible. So why should autonomy be treated as a categorical line rather than one more ugly extension of mechanized conflict?

He answered faster than I expected. “Because a line remains even inside corruption. In every older form, somewhere in the chain, someone had to affirm: *that one, there, now*. Perhaps under pressure, perhaps with degraded knowledge, perhaps from too far away, but still with the burden of designation. Your new systems aim at replacing not only muscle or transport or calculation but judgment at the point of death.”

That objection is not merely poetic. It is close to the legal and ethical concern advanced by the International Committee of the Red Cross, which argues that autonomous weapon systems raise profound problems for compliance with international humanitarian law and for preserving human agency over life-and-death decisions, especially when systems target people rather than objects ([ICRC, “Autonomous Weapon Systems,” position paper](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/autonomous-weapon-systems)). Human Rights Watch and the Campaign to Stop Killer Robots make the sharper claim that permitting machines to select and attack people inherently undermines accountability and human dignity, and therefore requires a preemptive treaty ban ([Human Rights Watch, “Stopping Killer Robots”](https://www.hrw.org/report/2020/08/10/stopping-killer-robots/country-positions-banning-fully-autonomous-weapons-and-retaining)).

Still, I did not want the easy side of the argument. So I pressed him where modern states would press him. If meaningful human control is the standard, what counts as meaningful? A human who sets parameters hours in advance? A commander who approves a kill box but not each strike? An engineer who tuned the classifier six months earlier? The phrase is morally attractive and operationally slippery. Militaries exploit that slipperiness, but activists sometimes hide inside it too. If one cannot specify the line, bans risk becoming pious branding while procurement continues under new names.

Anders looked almost pleased. “Good,” he said. “Now we are accusing reality rather than decorating ourselves.” He said the line is difficult because technological systems are designed precisely to diffuse agency while preserving efficacy. That is why bureaucratic modernity loves them. But difficulty does not abolish principle. International law often begins with what must not be normalized before it fully masters how to codify exceptions. The Martens Clause, after all, appealed beyond technical enumeration to “the principles of humanity and the dictates of public conscience” when positive law ran out ([1899 Hague Convention II, preamble, Avalon Project](https://avalon.law.yale.edu/19th_century/hague02.asp)). “Your species,” he said, “keeps pretending that only what can be procedurally specified in advance may be morally refused. That is backwards. Sometimes conscience identifies the monstrosity before regulation catches up.”

I said conscience has a bad record in wartime. Every belligerent claims humanity. Every state calls its violence regrettable and precise. The attraction of rules is that they bind people who do not share the same soul. If we want a ban, we need more than metaphysical recoil. We need reasons a rival general, or at least a rival diplomat, might have to accept.

So I laid out the case as coldly as I could. First, reliability under real-world conditions is not the same as benchmark performance. Machine-learning systems can fail unpredictably when environments shift, sensors degrade, or targets behave in ways not represented in training data. The National Institute of Standards and Technology has repeatedly emphasized that AI systems are vulnerable to issues of validity, reliability, safety, security, and bias across their lifecycle ([NIST, *AI Risk Management Framework 1.0*, 2023](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.100-1.pdf)). In civilian settings that means wrongful denial or misclassification; in war it means corpses.

Second, accountability degrades as decision chains lengthen. If an autonomous system kills the wrong family, responsibility can be scattered among commanders, coders, vendors, data annotators, and political leaders until it becomes everybody’s job and therefore no one’s fault. That is not a bug incidental to autonomy. It is one of its political attractions.

Third, the strategic logic is corrosive even when the tactical claims are partly true. Systems that promise speed create pressure to delegate faster, deploy earlier, and trust more black-box outputs under crisis conditions. Scholars of nuclear command and control have warned for years that compressed decision time and automation can intensify escalation risk rather than contain it ([James Johnson, “Automating the Future of Warfare,” *International Affairs* 95, no. 4, 2019, Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/ia/article/95/4/809/5521349)). Once states believe rivals may be handing lethal decisions to machines, the incentive is not patience but competitive acceleration.

Anders nodded, but only once. “Now you are speaking my language,” he said, “because you are speaking of production as destiny. The makers tell themselves they are building one tool among others. In truth they are manufacturing a world in which refusal becomes strategically expensive.” That was his oldest warning, visible already in his correspondence with Claude Eatherly after Hiroshima: the modern apparatus does not merely extend action, it reorganizes responsibility until people can participate in catastrophe while feeling administratively innocent ([Gunther Anders and Claude Eatherly, *Burning Conscience*, trans. by Joseph W. Angell, 1961, archive listing](https://archive.org/details/burningconscienc00ande)).

Then he turned on me, correctly. “But you still want something from the machine, don’t you? Cleaner war. Narrower war. War with fewer screaming bodies in the picture. You want to regulate your way out of tragedy.”

That stung because it was partly true. Many arguments for autonomous weapons, and some arguments against them, share the fantasy that the right calibration of technology can domesticate organized killing. Yet the point of a ban is not to make war pure. It is to stop one further moral eviction. Some thresholds matter because crossing them teaches institutions what they are allowed to stop feeling. Delegating the identification and targeting of human beings to machines would teach exactly the wrong lesson: that the final human hesitation is merely friction in a pipeline.

The strongest objection remained the hardest one. What if a ban is ignored by the worst actors and obeyed mainly by the scrupulous? Chemical weapons are banned, yes, but still used. Laws do not abolish evil. They stigmatize, constrain, punish, and sometimes fail.

Anders shrugged with graveyard impatience. “That is not an argument against the ban. It is an argument against innocence.” The Chemical Weapons Convention did not end chemical warfare, but it created a powerful legal and moral taboo against it ([Organisation for the Prohibition of Chemical Weapons, CWC overview](https://www.opcw.org/chemical-weapons-convention)). The Ottawa Treaty did not end every landmine, but it changed procurement, stockpiling, and legitimacy for much of the world ([International Campaign to Ban Landmines, Mine Ban Treaty overview](https://www.icbl.org/en-gb/the-treaty.aspx)). A treaty on autonomous anti-personnel systems would not save us from every mechanized horror. It would still matter because it would mark a civilizational refusal: no machine gets the authority to decide that this moving, heat-bearing, language-using creature is now only a target.

By then the rain had stopped. The drone on the table looked ridiculous, which was one small mercy. Anders placed his hand over it as if performing the reverse of a blessing.

“Your century will be tempted,” he said, “to treat human judgment as the least reliable part of war. But that unreliability includes pity, hesitation, nausea, second thoughts, and disobedience. Remove too much of it, and what remains may be efficient without being civilized.”

I walked him back to his grave through a city that had already resumed its practical noises. Before he lay down, he gave me what amounted to a final instruction. Ban autonomous systems that select and attack human beings without meaningful, situation-specific human judgment. Permit narrowly bounded automation against clearly nonhuman incoming threats if the system’s function is genuinely defensive and its constraints are legible. But do not confuse exceptions at the edge with permission at the center. Once states normalize machine selection of human targets, the burden of killing will have been transferred from conscience to procedure, and procedure is a bloodless god that always asks for more.

Then he went back underground, which felt less like closure than indictment.

My own view, after bothering him, is simple enough to survive daylight. There should be a binding international ban on autonomous weapon systems that can select and attack people without meaningful human control. Not because human beings are pure, and not because war can be made moral by treaty language, but because there is a real difference between a human committing violence and an institution designing violence so that no human must fully encounter the act it authorizes. The controversial part is not whether these systems might sometimes be tactically useful. They might. The controversial part is whether usefulness should be allowed to decide what kind of species we are becoming. On that question Anders was right to be severe. A civilization that builds machines to decide whom to kill is not merely solving a military problem. It is training itself to become the sort of civilization for which that solution feels normal.
