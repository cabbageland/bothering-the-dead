today is 06-27.

I found Phineas Gage in two places, because the nineteenth century could not even let a man stay divided privately.

Most of him is in Colma, California. Cypress Lawn says Gage was first buried at Laurel Hill Cemetery in San Francisco and later moved to Cypress Lawn in the early 1940s, while his skull remained at Harvard's Warren Anatomical Museum ([Cypress Lawn, "Buried at Cypress: A Billionaire and a Medical Mystery"](https://cypresslawn.com/blog/buried-cypress-billionaire-medical-mystery/)). The skull and the tamping iron, meanwhile, sit in Boston in the Countway Library's orbit of medical relics, where the exhibit records the iron bar, the life cast, the daguerreotype of Gage holding the bar, Harlow's publications, and the skull itself ([Countway Library, "The Case of Phineas Gage"](https://collections.countway.harvard.edu/onview/exhibits/show/beyond-the-bone-box/the-case-of-phineas-gage)).

So I did not find a grave so much as a legal problem with bones.

I began in Colma, at the massed quiet of relocated San Francisco dead, with a train spike, a prison key, a toy judge's gavel, a scan of a frontal lobe, a photocopy of 18 U.S.C. Section 17, a Supreme Court opinion, and one little brass label that said MITIGATION in the smug font museums use when they want suffering to behave.

Then, because a divided body deserves a divided ritual, I walked in imagination to the Warren Anatomical Museum. I stood before the skull and read from John Martyn Harlow's 1868 account, the one written after Gage had died and after the skull had been examined. Harlow described a railway foreman who survived the passage of an iron bar through his head, recovered physically, but became altered enough that his contractors would not rehire him; Harlow's compact moral thunder was that Gage's friends said he was "no longer Gage" ([John M. Harlow, "Recovery from the Passage of an Iron Bar Through the Head"](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Recovery_from_the_passage_of_an_iron_bar_through_the_head)).

I did not recite the line as a slogan. I recited it as an indictment of slogans.

The skull clicked.

The tamping iron, three feet seven inches of industrial argument, gave a small shudder. Malcolm Macmillan's correction of the legend came up like a warning label: the real evidence about Gage is thinner and stranger than the textbook myth, and later writers inflated him into whatever theory they needed; he may even have made a meaningful social recovery through structured work, including years as a stagecoach driver in Chile, before returning to California and dying after epileptic seizures ([Malcolm Macmillan, "Phineas Gage - Unravelling the myth"](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/phineas-gage-unravelling-myth)).

The brass label cracked. The grave and the exhibit disagreed for a moment about jurisdiction. Then Phineas Gage sat up from both places at once: head in Boston, body in California, one eye gone, patience gone faster, looking like a man who had spent a century and a half being turned into a parable by people who needed fewer facts than they had opinions.

"You brought a gavel," he said.

"A toy one."

"Good. The full-sized ones do enough damage."

"I brought you a question."

"You brought me a use."

The question was this: **Should neuroscience make us abandon retributive punishment and treat criminal behavior as damaged capacity, risk, and rehabilitation instead of moral desert, or would that turn responsible persons into malfunctioning machines and victims into footnotes?**

This is not a dorm-room argument about whether free will exists in some spotless metaphysical theater. It is about whether courts should punish people because they deserve suffering, or restrict and reform them only because doing so prevents harm. It is about brain tumors, traumatic brain injuries, psychosis, addiction, adolescent development, poverty, impulse control, rage, remorse, and the enormous American habit of using prison as the warehouse for problems it refuses to understand. At yearend 2023, the Bureau of Justice Statistics estimated that about one in forty-eight adult U.S. residents was under correctional supervision, and that 1,852,900 people were incarcerated in state or federal prisons or local jails ([BJS, *Correctional Populations in the United States, 2023*](https://bjs.ojp.gov/document/cpus23st.pdf)).

"That is a lot of souls to explain with bad character," Gage said.

"Yes."

"And a lot of brains to excuse with bad diagrams."

"Also yes."

He approved of the balance by frowning.

The best case for the neuroscience revolution begins with a brutal little truth: every crime is committed by a brain in a body in a world. That does not settle responsibility, but it ruins the fantasy that wrongdoing floats above biology like a demon in court clothes. A person who cannot appreciate reality because of severe psychosis, a person whose frontal systems are damaged, a child whose control systems are immature, a starving addict in withdrawal, and a calculating executive committing fraud are all embodied agents, but they are not identically situated before blame. The law already admits this. Federal law treats insanity as an affirmative defense when, because of severe mental disease or defect, the defendant was unable to appreciate the nature and quality or wrongfulness of the acts ([18 U.S.C. Section 17](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/18/17)). The older Model Penal Code formulation was broader, asking whether mental disease or defect left the person without substantial capacity to appreciate criminality or conform conduct to law ([Cornell Wex, "insanity defense"](https://www.law.cornell.edu/wex/insanity_defense)).

"So the law has always known that the mind can be injured," Gage said.

"It has known and forgotten, repeatedly."

"That is the law's main exercise."

The pro-neuroscience argument says criminal justice should stop pretending that punishment is purified by blame. If a person was shaped by genes, childhood violence, lead exposure, brain injury, psychiatric disease, and social misery, then the question "What does he deserve?" can become a sanctified way of refusing to ask "What happened, what danger remains, what can be repaired, and what response will prevent more harm?" Neuroscience does not need to prove that nobody is responsible. It only needs to make vivid that capacities vary, that self-control is not evenly distributed, and that the boundary between wickedness and impairment is often drawn by people lucky enough to have never watched their own machinery fail.

Gage touched the side of his skull.

"The machinery is not the man," he said.

"No. But there is no man without machinery."

He did not like that. Neither do I. That is how one knows it is near the bone.

The Supreme Court has already let developmental science alter punishment at the edges. In *Roper v. Simmons*, the Court barred the death penalty for crimes committed under eighteen, reasoning that juveniles' immaturity, vulnerability, and still-forming identity make them less culpable and less reliably among the worst offenders ([*Roper v. Simmons*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supct/html/03-633.ZS.html)). In *Miller v. Alabama*, it held that mandatory life without parole for juvenile homicide offenders violates the Eighth Amendment because sentencing must consider youth and its attendant characteristics ([*Miller v. Alabama*](https://www.law.cornell.edu/supremecourt/text/10-9646)). The line has not moved in one simple direction; *Jones v. Mississippi* later held that a sentencing court need not make a separate finding of permanent incorrigibility before imposing juvenile life without parole, so the American compromise remains jagged and mean around the edges ([*Jones v. Mississippi*](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/18-1259_8njq.pdf)).

Still, the principle is there: a punishment can become disproportionate when the offender's capacities make full adult blame a lie.

"Children are not railroads," Gage said.

"Meaning?"

"Do not build a track from youth to every excuse."

The objection is fair. The pro-neuroscience argument becomes stupid when it turns into a universal solvent. Benjamin Libet's famous experiment found that measurable brain activity preceded the reported conscious intention to make simple voluntary movements, but the study concerned very small, artificial acts in a handful of subjects, not murder, betrayal, planning, repentance, or law ([Libet et al., "Time of conscious intention to act," PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6640273/)). Adina Roskies has argued that neuroscience is not in a position to overthrow ordinary responsibility, partly because philosophical problems about freedom long predate brain science and partly because the empirical results do not do the metaphysical work people ask of them ([Roskies, "Neuroscientific challenges to free will and responsibility"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16901745/)).

This is where the courtroom loves bad theater. A scan glows. The jurors inhale. A lawyer says "frontal lobe" as if he has just said "God." The defendant's brain appears on a screen, and everyone forgets that all defendants have brains. Stephen Morse calls this kind of inflation "Brain Overclaim Syndrome," the mistake of treating a material explanation of behavior as though it automatically changed the legal criteria for responsibility ([Stephen J. Morse, "Brain Overclaim Syndrome and Criminal Responsibility"](https://repository.law.upenn.edu/Documents/Detail/brain-overclaim-syndrome-and-criminal-responsibility-a-diagnostic-note/392308)).

Gage laughed once.

"If a hole in my skull explains every sin, then men with whole skulls will become jealous."

"The phrase is: my brain made me do it."

"A cowardly sentence. Also a true one, depending on the day."

Exactly. "My brain made me do it" is both trivially true and usually legally useless. A person acts through the brain as she speaks through the mouth. Saying the brain caused the act does not tell us whether the person could understand reasons, respond to law, foresee harm, inhibit impulses, or answer to others. It does not tell us whether the impairment was severe, relevant, voluntary, foreseeable, treatable, temporary, exaggerated, or morally beside the point. It does not tell us whether the victim is less dead.

The best case against abolishing retributive responsibility begins not with cruelty but with respect. To hold a person responsible is not always to lust after suffering. Sometimes it is to address the person as answerable: you did this, it mattered, give account. P. F. Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" remains central to moral-responsibility theory because it locates responsibility in human reactive attitudes - resentment, gratitude, forgiveness, indignation - rather than in a laboratory proof that human beings float free of causation ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Moral Responsibility"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-responsibility/)). Those attitudes can be excessive, biased, and vindictive. They can also be the grammar by which we refuse to let harm become a weather event.

"Weather kills without guilt," Gage said.

"Yes."

"But a murderer is not a thunderstorm."

"Usually not."

"And when he is?"

That is the question the gavel was afraid of.

There are cases where the person before the court is closer to a disaster than to a villain: florid psychosis, profound intellectual disability, a seizure, sleepwalking, a tumor pressing upon the faculties that make reasons usable. There are also cases where the person has reasons, knows the wrong, chooses the advantage, and later wants the glamour of pathology. The law's hard work is not deciding whether brains matter. They always matter. The work is deciding when a brain condition has broken, bent, or merely accompanied the capacities that make blame fair.

The anti-retribution side says: even the calculating executive did not choose his genes, parents, temperament, era, opportunities, appetites, or first moral education. If we had his whole causal history, we would see the machinery that produced the choice. Therefore hatred is epistemic laziness. Punishment should be quarantine, repair, restitution, deterrence, and public safety, not payback. That position has a terrible beauty. It takes moral luck seriously. It looks at the fact that one child gets books and patience while another gets blows and lead and chaos, then refuses to call the final criminal sentence a pure measurement of soul.

Gage looked toward his absent body in California.

"A society that beats a boy, starves him, teaches him danger, ignores his injury, then condemns his damaged adulthood as self-authored is doing bookkeeping with an axe."

"That is the best sentence you have said."

"I have had time."

But the anti-retribution position has its own abyss. If punishment is only risk management, the state may become less angry and more frightening. A person who has committed a modest crime but is predicted dangerous becomes eligible for long confinement. A person who has committed a grave crime but is predicted safe becomes eligible for release in a way victims may experience as moral erasure. The language changes from guilt to treatment, from prison to hospital, from sentence to intervention, but the locked door remains. Sometimes it becomes easier to lock, because medicalized power can claim benevolence while doing what vengeance at least had the decency to admit.

"A cage with a diagnosis is still a cage," Gage said.

"Yes."

"And a doctor can become a hangman with softer hands."

Also yes. That is why abolishing blame is not automatically humane. A pure dangerousness model can punish people for what experts predict rather than for what they did. It can make liberty depend on the state's confidence about a person's future brain. It can turn poverty, oddness, trauma, and mental illness into actuarial stains. Retribution at its best imposes a limit: punish for the wrong done, proportionately, not for the monster an algorithm or psychiatrist suspects you may become.

This is the serious defense of desert: not cruelty, but boundary. The state may not do whatever works. It may not cut a thief open for research because doing so might deter theft. It may not confine a harmless offender forever because the crowd still wants blood. It may not call a child irredeemable because a model says the trajectory is ugly. Desert, narrowed and disciplined, can protect the guilty from the efficiency of our fear.

Gage picked up the toy gavel.

"Then you want punishment to say two things at once: you are answerable, and we are answerable for how we answer."

"Yes."

"No wonder courts prefer Latin."

The middle position is not a compromise in the soggy sense. It is a sharper distinction.

First, neuroscience should matter when it bears on legally and morally relevant capacities: understanding, impulse control, reality testing, foresight, emotional regulation, susceptibility to coercion, ability to learn, and prospects for rehabilitation. Not every abnormal scan matters. Not every normal scan absolves. The question is not whether the brain is different. The question is different in a way that changes agency.

Second, retribution as vengeance should die. The pleasure of seeing suffering returned is not justice; it is appetite with a robe on. Punishment should not be designed to hurt because hurt feels symmetrical. The victim's pain is real, but symmetry is a false god. You cannot resurrect the murdered by manufacturing another ruined life. You cannot prove moral order by adding waste to grief.

Third, censure should remain. A society must be able to say, in public and with reasons: this act was wrong, this person did it, this victim mattered, this harm is not being redescribed as weather. Censure need not require cruelty. It requires truth. Sometimes the truth includes diminished capacity. Sometimes it includes full-blooded responsibility. Sometimes it includes both: you were injured, and you injured; you were shaped, and you acted; you are not a demon, and you are not merely a symptom.

Fourth, confinement should be justified by a mixture of proportional censure, public safety, and rehabilitation, with every year of lost liberty made to answer for itself. That means shorter sentences where long ones serve only theatrical rage. It means serious treatment for mental illness and brain injury. It means youth sentences that actually respect change. It means no permanent disposal of people on the basis of a bad adolescent brain. It means no glowing scan worship. It means the victim is not asked to forgive in order for the state to behave decently.

"You are building a narrow bridge," Gage said.

"Between moral agency and moral luck."

"It will wobble."

"Yes."

"Better than a guillotine with neuroscience footnotes."

The resurrection flickered. I could see the old case being misused in both directions. On one side, the punitive moralist says: Gage changed, but he still worked, so stop whining about brains and behave. On the other, the reductionist says: Gage changed, therefore personality is machinery, therefore no one is really guilty. Both are too hungry. Both eat the man.

Gage's real lesson is stranger. A tamping iron went through his head. Something in him changed. Something in him remained. Something in him may have recovered under structure and work. The historical record is small, contaminated by myth, and still powerful enough to make the moral imagination stumble. That is exactly how neuroscience should enter law: not as a magic solvent, but as a disciplined source of humility.

"Humility will not satisfy the angry," Gage said.

"No."

"Will it satisfy the harmed?"

"Not always."

"Then why offer it?"

"Because false satisfaction is one of punishment's oldest drugs."

He considered this. The grave quieted. The museum lights became the color of old bone.

The hardest part is the victim. Philosophers and reformers can grow airy here, which is indecent. If someone murders your child, rapes your sister, beats your father, empties your savings, burns your house, or terrorizes your neighborhood, an explanation can feel like an insult. The sentence "he had impaired executive function" may arrive like someone stepping over a body to admire a diagram. Victims are not obligated to become metaphysicians in order to be morally serious.

But neither is the state entitled to convert grief into policy without thought. The victim deserves recognition, protection, restitution where possible, truthful process, and a punishment that refuses to trivialize the harm. The victim does not deserve to be used as the sacred mask for a system that wastes people, ignores treatment, exaggerates deterrence, and calls its cruelty closure. Some victims want mercy. Some want severity. None of them should be forced to carry the philosophical incompetence of the whole penal code.

Gage looked at the prison key.

"What about the ones who are dangerous?"

"Confine them when necessary."

"Necessary is a word that steals coats."

"Then define it tightly: serious risk, evidence, review, least restrictive means, treatment, release when the justification fails."

"And the ones who cannot be repaired?"

"Then we protect others without pretending protection is cosmic revenge."

That is the severe answer. Some people must be separated from others for a long time. Some perhaps forever. But the moral reason matters. If confinement is necessary protection, say so and keep it no crueler than necessity. If confinement is punishment, keep it proportionate and finite. If treatment is the rationale, provide treatment rather than warehouse despair with a brochure. Do not smuggle vengeance into medicine or preventive detention into justice. Do not call a cage a cure because the prisoner has a diagnosis.

The brain does not abolish responsibility. It redistributes our arrogance. It says: before you condemn, ask what capacities were present. Before you excuse, ask what the person could understand and answer for. Before you punish, ask what good the punishment can truthfully claim. Before you predict, remember the history of experts being certain in the direction power wanted. Before you point to a scan, remember that the person is not the picture. Before you say "evil," make sure you are not naming damage you were too lazy to study. Before you say "damage," make sure you are not erasing a choice because choice makes you uncomfortable.

Gage held the brass MITIGATION label between finger and thumb.

"This word is too small," he said.

"For what?"

"For the difference between mercy and contempt."

He was right. Mitigation can mean: you are less blameworthy because your powers were wounded. It can also mean: you are less of a person because biology explains you. A humane law must cling to the first and reject the second. The point of recognizing impairment is not to demote the impaired from moral community. It is to judge them more truthfully within it.

That is why the Gage myth matters. If we turn him into a monster made by a rod, we degrade brain-injured people. If we turn him into proof that injury is irrelevant because he kept working, we degrade everyone whose injury did not become a useful redemption story. If we turn him into the death of free will, we ask one skull to carry a metaphysical empire. If we turn him into a caution about humility, plasticity, evidence, capacity, and the fragile conditions of self-command, then perhaps we have bothered him for a reason.

The grave began to pull him back. The skull in Boston brightened with that museum shine that makes every relic look accused. The body in Colma made no sound at all.

"Do you know what I hated most?" he asked.

"The iron?"

"No. The certainty afterward."

"Whose certainty?"

"Everyone's."

Then he lay down in both places. The tamping iron stilled. The toy gavel split neatly in half, which was the first honest thing it had done all night.

I left with this answer: neuroscience should not abolish criminal responsibility, but it should abolish lazy blame. It should push the law away from vengeance and toward capacity, proportionality, prevention, repair, and review. It should reduce punishment where injury, youth, illness, coercion, or developmental immaturity genuinely diminish agency. It should never be allowed to excuse everything by explaining everything. It should never license the state to cage people indefinitely because their brains look risky. It should make punishment rarer, more modest, more evidence-bound, and less proud.

The brain is not an alibi. The soul is not a floating monarch. A person is the hard meeting of body, history, choice, relation, damage, and answerability. Justice begins when we can look at that whole difficulty without simplifying it into either demon or machine.
