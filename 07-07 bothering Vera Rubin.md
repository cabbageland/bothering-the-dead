today is 07-07.

I found Vera Rubin in a tomb that had just started taking pictures.

Not her literal grave. I did not pretend that a human body in the ground is a prop for my convenience. I found the more public tomb: the machine with her name on it, high on Cerro Pachon, one week after the NSF-DOE Vera C. Rubin Observatory announced that its ten-year Legacy Survey of Space and Time had officially begun on June 30, 2026 ([Rubin Observatory, "Action! NSF-DOE Vera C. Rubin Observatory Begins Capturing the Greatest Cosmic Movie Ever Made"](https://rubinobservatory.org/news/action-rubin-lsst-begins)). A telescope is not usually a mausoleum, but this one had the correct haunted architecture. It was built to keep asking the question that survived Rubin: what, exactly, is doing all this gravitating in the dark?

The observatory's public account is appropriately grand: a vast camera, a decade of repeated scans, the southern sky made into a time-lapse record, dark matter and dark energy placed among the central mysteries ([Rubin Observatory, "Science Goals"](https://rubinobservatory.org/explore/science-goals)). Grandness is tolerable when the instrument has to lift a whole civilization's ignorance. The problem is that ignorance, once funded and named, can become proud of itself.

So I made a grave from the correct debris. I laid out Rubin and Kent Ford's 1970 Andromeda paper, where spectra of emission regions in M31 helped establish a rotation curve that did not fall away as the visible matter alone made one expect ([Rubin and Ford, 1970](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1970ApJ...159..379R/abstract)). Beside it I placed the 1980 paper with Norbert Thonnard and Ford on twenty-one Sc galaxies, the one that made the flatness look less like a local oddity and more like a law with a grudge ([Rubin, Ford, and Thonnard, 1980](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980ApJ...238..471R/abstract)). Then Milgrom's 1983 proposal of Modified Newtonian Dynamics, which said perhaps the missing thing was not matter but our law of motion in low-acceleration regimes ([Milgrom, 1983](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ApJ...270..365M/abstract)). Then the Bullet Cluster paper, where weak lensing and X-ray gas pulled apart in a way many physicists still treat as the cleanest courtroom exhibit for unseen matter ([Clowe et al., 2006](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0608407)). Finally, because the dead deserve news from the living, I added the latest LUX-ZEPLIN result I could find: a 5.7 tonne-year exposure, no significant excess attributable to light dark matter in the tested 3 to 9 GeV/c^2 mass range, and a real solar-neutrino signal instead, as if nature had answered the wrong door beautifully ([LZ Collaboration, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08065)).

To wake Rubin, I drew the expected Keplerian fall of a galaxy rotation curve in chalk. Then I drew the observed flat line over it. The second line refused the first with the calm insolence of a measurement.

The air sharpened. The papers lifted at their corners. The new telescope, far away in Chile, made another exposure. Somewhere in the machinery, a shutter did the little mechanical blink by which the universe enters a human archive.

Rubin sat up from the data-grave wearing the expression of a woman who had spent a life measuring things men had not expected, and had found this preferable to measuring men.

"You chose the wrong grave," she said.

"I chose the one still producing evidence."

"Then it is not a grave."

"It is for certainty."

She considered that, and let it live.

The question was this: **Should physics keep treating dark matter as an undiscovered particle and build ever larger searches for it, or has the long failure to detect such a particle made modified gravity the more honest way to think about the missing mass problem?**

This is not a decorative argument about whether the universe contains spooky stuff. The stakes are billions of dollars in telescopes, underground detectors, supercomputers, graduate students, public trust, and the metaphysical habits of science. It is about whether "dark matter" names an entity, a placeholder, a law we do not understand, or a disciplined confession that the visible universe is not enough. It is about how long an invisible thing can remain intellectually respectable when it keeps winning in the sky and losing in the lab.

Rubin looked at the LZ paper.

"Begin with the missing matter," she said.

"You would."

"I began with velocities."

Fine. The case for dark matter begins with a severe distinction: dark matter was not invented because physicists were bored and wanted a substance with Gothic branding. It was inferred because the motions and lensing of astronomical systems refused to balance under ordinary visible mass. Rubin herself later described the Andromeda work as an attempt to determine orbital velocities as far out as possible and thereby deduce M31's mass; Ford's image tube spectrograph mattered because better instrumentation let the outer galaxy speak ([Rubin, "Seeing dark matter in the Andromeda galaxy"](https://physicstoday.aip.org/opinion/seeing-dark-matter-in-the-andromeda-galaxy)). The scandal was not theoretical extravagance. The scandal was data.

"Yes," Rubin said. "The stars were not impressed by our expectations."

In a solar system, velocity falls with distance once most of the mass is inside the orbit. In many spiral galaxies, rotation curves stay approximately flat far beyond the luminous center. The naive visible-matter account says the outskirts should slow. The observations say they do not. By 1980, Rubin, Ford, and Thonnard could write from a sample rather than a hunch ([Rubin, Ford, and Thonnard, 1980](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1980ApJ...238..471R/abstract)). One may quarrel over the final ontology, but one does not get to patronize the anomaly as a mood.

The dark matter realist then adds: the evidence did not stay trapped in galaxy rotation curves. The cosmic microwave background, in the standard Lambda-CDM model, gives a cold dark matter density far above baryonic matter; the Planck 2018 cosmological parameters report Omega_c h^2 = 0.120 plus or minus 0.001 and Omega_b h^2 = 0.0224 plus or minus 0.0001 in the base model ([Planck Collaboration, 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.06209)). The Bullet Cluster and related cluster mergers seem to separate the gravitational potential traced by lensing from the ordinary X-ray gas that contains most of the baryonic mass, which is precisely what one expects if a collisionless mass component passes through while plasma slows ([Clowe et al., 2006](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0608407)). Rubin Observatory itself is designed partly to map dark matter through weak gravitational lensing, a method that treats distorted background galaxies as testimony from spacetime's bent furniture ([Rubin Observatory, "The Nature of Dark Matter"](https://www.lsst.org/science/dark-matter)).

"Furniture?" Rubin said.

"It was that or upholstery."

"Do not upholster gravity."

Point taken.

The philosophical defense is just as important as the astrophysical one. Scientific realism is not embarrassed by unobservable entities merely because they are unobservable. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes scientific realism as a positive epistemic attitude toward our best theories, including both observable and unobservable aspects of the world ([SEP, "Scientific Realism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-realism/)). Electrons are not visible to unaided eyes. Neutrinos were inferred before they were detected. Black holes entered physics as mathematical consequences before they became telescope celebrities. If the best explanation for a wide, independent body of evidence includes an entity that does not shine, then "I cannot see it" is not an argument. It is an autobiographical fact about eyes.

Rubin gave the smallest nod.

"You are defending the right to infer."

"Yes."

"Do not confuse it with the right to relax."

There is the wound. The case against dark matter particles begins not by denying the missing gravity, but by objecting to the ease with which missing gravity becomes a particle story. Dark matter as an astronomical phenomenon is extremely well motivated. Dark matter as a specific particle, or a family of particles with particular masses and interactions, is much less secure. Direct detection has not delivered the clean event. LZ's 2025 low-mass search saw solar neutrinos with genuine significance, but no significant dark matter nuclear-recoil excess in its tested window ([LZ Collaboration, 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.08065)). Earlier LZ results also set strong limits without a WIMP detection above 9 GeV/c^2 ([LZ Collaboration, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.17036)). The underground machines are not failures; they are magnificent instruments. But magnificence can still return absence.

"Absence in a window," Rubin said.

"A very clean window."

"Clean windows are still not walls."

Correct. A null result in one mass range or interaction channel does not kill dark matter. It kills versions of dark matter that were bold enough to be vulnerable there. The dark sector may be axions, sterile neutrinos, hidden particles, primordial black holes in some constrained range, self-interacting matter, ultralight fields, asymmetric relics, or something uglier than the names people put on conference slides. The fact that one elegant suspect has no fingerprints does not prove there was no crime.

But the skeptic has a sharper complaint. Scientific theories can be underdetermined by evidence; the SEP entry on underdetermination puts the core idea simply, that evidence available at a time may be insufficient to determine what beliefs we should hold in response to it ([SEP, "Underdetermination of Scientific Theory"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-underdetermination/)). Rotation curves, lensing maps, and CMB anisotropies do not come labeled "particle" in God's handwriting. They come as patterns. The interpretation asks for law, matter, initial conditions, structure formation, baryonic feedback, statistics, and taste.

This is where MOND enters like an unwelcome but annoyingly punctual guest. Milgrom did not merely say "maybe gravity is different" in the empty way people say it at parties after one glass of wine. He proposed a modification at very small accelerations, around a characteristic scale, to address the mass discrepancy without unseen halos ([Milgrom, 1983](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1983ApJ...270..365M/abstract)). Whatever its failures, MOND got something emotionally and mathematically important right: galaxies behave as if their visible baryons know too much about their total acceleration.

McGaugh, Lelli, and Schombert's 2016 radial acceleration relation made this discomfort precise. In 153 rotationally supported galaxies, the observed radial acceleration correlated tightly with the acceleration predicted from observed baryons, persisting even when dark matter dominates in the standard interpretation ([McGaugh, Lelli, and Schombert, 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1609.05917)). If you are a dark matter theorist, that relation is not a footnote. It is a summons. Why should invisible halos, assembled through messy cosmological history, track ordinary baryons so cleanly across galaxies? The answer may be baryonic feedback and galaxy formation inside Lambda-CDM. It may be a real dark-sector law. It may be modified gravity. But the relation is a fact with the social skills of a knife.

Rubin smiled.

"That is a good sentence."

"It cost several galaxies."

"They could afford it."

The pro-MOND argument, in its strongest form, is not that every cluster, cosmological parameter, and relativistic phenomenon has been solved more elegantly than in dark matter. That is not true. MOND has persistent trouble with galaxy clusters and cosmology; even sympathetic reviews frame it as a paradigm with striking galaxy-scale successes and serious larger-scale problems ([Famaey and McGaugh, 2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.12942/lrr-2012-10)). The strong argument is narrower and more dangerous: **at the scale where Rubin's evidence first bit, the modified-gravity intuition often predicts with fewer adjustable dark components than the halo-fitting habit of cold dark matter.**

"Be careful," Rubin said. "Fewer components is not always deeper truth."

"I know."

"Do you? Astronomers adore economy until the detector bill arrives."

This is the philosophical hinge. Simplicity matters, but not as a beauty contest. Explanation matters, but the best explanation may be best only among a bad menu. Constructive empiricism, as SEP summarizes, challenges the realist move from empirical adequacy to truth, including skepticism about inference to the best explanation as a rule that automatically licenses belief in unobservables ([SEP, "Constructive Empiricism"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/constructive-empiricism/)). The modified-gravity camp is useful partly because it keeps asking whether dark matter's explanatory success is becoming metaphysical credit card debt: buy now, add halo later, refinance with feedback, promise the particle after the next run.

The dark matter camp is useful because it keeps asking whether modified gravity is a galaxy-scale poem that has not paid rent in the wider universe. The CMB is not a decorative afterthought. Large-scale structure is not a rumor. Cluster collisions are not all fake news from general relativity's public relations office. A theory that sings in spiral galaxies but stammers in clusters and early-universe cosmology has not earned the crown merely because its song is memorable.

Rubin put her hand on the 1970 paper.

"You keep speaking as if I found an ontology," she said.

"People say you discovered dark matter."

"People say many things when they need a short caption."

"What did you find?"

"A failure of the visible account."

That was the discipline of the dead physicist, though Rubin was properly an astronomer: not modesty as self-erasure, but modesty as measurement ethics. She did not need to pretend that the unseen was known in order to prove that the seen was insufficient. The official Rubin Observatory biography says her work helped turn dark matter from a concept not taken seriously into a central problem, and notes that she expected physicists would identify the invisible matter within ten years of her observations ([Rubin Observatory, "Who was Vera Rubin?"](https://rubinobservatory.org/about/vera-rubin)). That expectation is almost tender now. Ten years became decades. Decades became detectors under mountains. The answer did not arrive late; it failed to become the kind of answer she expected.

"Would you abandon the particle search?" I asked her.

"No."

"Would you keep calling it inevitable?"

"No."

"Would you give MOND equal status?"

"Equal to what?"

"To particle dark matter."

"Not equal. Necessary."

That distinction is better than most panel discussions.

Modified gravity is necessary because a dominant paradigm becomes stupid when it has no articulate enemies. MOND and its descendants force dark matter theorists to explain the baryonic regularities rather than shrug them into nuisance parameters. They punish laziness at the exact place Rubin's data were most beautiful. They ask whether the dark sector must be engineered to imitate a law. They keep the galaxy from becoming merely a halo wearing stars as jewelry.

Particle dark matter remains necessary because the missing mass problem is not only a galaxy problem. It is a cosmological and gravitational-lensing problem, a structure-formation problem, a problem written into independent kinds of evidence. The Planck CMB fit, the Bullet Cluster-style separations, the growth of structure, and weak-lensing surveys do not prove a particular particle, but they make a purely local tweak to Newtonian dynamics feel too small for the job ([Planck Collaboration, 2018](https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.06209); [Clowe et al., 2006](https://arxiv.org/abs/astro-ph/0608407)). If modified gravity wants the throne, it must govern the whole kingdom, not merely the province where it first gave an excellent speech.

Rubin leaned toward the LZ result.

"And what of these experiments?"

"They have found no WIMPs in the tested places."

"Say it exactly like that."

"No WIMPs in the tested places."

"Good. Precision is cheaper than regret."

So here is the answer I would defend to the living and risk before the dead: **dark matter should remain the leading research program, but not as a dogma of particle inevitability. Modified gravity should remain a serious adversary and diagnostic tool, not as a full replacement that has already won. The morally and intellectually honest position is pluralist pressure: believe the missing-gravity phenomenon strongly, believe any specific dark particle proportionally, and force every framework to make itself vulnerable to observation.**

That answer is less satisfying than a team jersey. Good. Team jerseys are where thought goes to become laundry.

The funding consequence is concrete. Keep building Rubin-scale sky surveys, because they map the gravitational world in ways no underground detector can. Keep building direct-detection experiments, because a single real scattering signal would change the metaphysical status of dark matter overnight. But stop narrating every null result as merely the heroic narrowing of a preordained particle. Sometimes narrowing is progress. Sometimes it is a corridor getting longer. The difference must be argued, not branded.

Also fund the irritating alternatives. Fund relativistic modified-gravity work, galaxy-dynamics tests, low-surface-brightness galaxies, dwarf galaxies, strong-lensing anomalies, simulations that explain the radial acceleration relation without hand-waving, and observations designed to embarrass both camps. The Snowmass white paper on Rubin as a flagship dark matter experiment argues that astronomical observations remain the only empirical evidence for dark matter and are essential for understanding its physical nature ([Mao et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.07252)). That should be read not as an insult to laboratories, but as a warning: if the sky is the evidence, the sky must keep jurisdiction.

Rubin's face sharpened at the word "jurisdiction."

"The sky does not belong to theorists."

"Or experimentalists."

"Or observatories."

"Or funders."

"It belongs to no one. That is why it is useful."

The public consequence matters too. When scientists tell people that most matter is dark, they are asking for trust in an inference to an unseen thing. That is legitimate. But legitimacy has a tone. Say: we have overwhelming evidence for missing gravitational mass or modified gravitational behavior. Say: the leading model treats most matter as nonluminous and nonbaryonic. Say: we do not yet know the particle identity, if there is one. Say: modified gravity captures real galaxy regularities and faces hard cosmological obstacles. Say: null searches are knowledge, not humiliation. Say: no one gets to convert mystery into prestige without paying interest in precision.

"You want humility," Rubin said.

"I want audited confidence."

"Better."

The deepest stake is not whether the universe contains a hidden particle. It is whether science can remain loyal to the difference between a phenomenon, an explanation, and a hope. The phenomenon is that the visible matter does not account for the gravitational behavior. The explanation called cold dark matter works astonishingly well across many scales, but its microscopic identity is absent. The hope is that a particle will be caught, named, weighed, and folded into physics like a fugitive finally printed on a passport.

Hope is allowed. Hope builds detectors. Hope keeps graduate students awake in control rooms underground. Hope points cameras at the sky for ten years. But hope must not promote itself to evidence.

Rubin stood beside the chalk curves. The expected line fell. The observed line stayed flat. The argument between them looked almost too simple to have rearranged cosmology.

"When we measured the outer parts of galaxies," she said, "the important thing was not that we had a favorite answer. The important thing was that the galaxy did not care."

"That may be the whole scientific method."

"No. The whole scientific method also includes keeping records, calibrating instruments, and not lying to yourself when tired."

"Less quotable."

"More useful."

The Rubin Observatory took another image somewhere in the living night. Its namesake looked toward it, not maternally, exactly. More like a person watching a question grow beyond her and remain her own.

"I thought they would know sooner," she said.

"They did not."

"Then they should keep looking."

"In which direction?"

She pointed first to the telescope, then to the underground detector, then to Milgrom's paper, then to the chalk.

"All directions that can lose."

That is what I took from the grave of the new machine. A mature physics of darkness must be able to lose. Dark matter must be able to lose specific particle candidates without pretending the lost ones were never loved. Modified gravity must be able to lose where clusters, lensing, and the CMB refuse it. Simulations must be able to lose if baryonic feedback becomes a universal solvent for embarrassment. Experiments must be able to lose without calling every silence an outreach opportunity. Theories must be able to lose before they become institutions with equations painted on the lobby wall.

Before Rubin returned to death, she picked up the chalk and corrected my curve. Not by much. Enough to be annoying. Enough to be right.

"The universe is not hiding from you," she said.

"Then what is it doing?"

"Being measured badly until it is measured better."

Then she stepped back into the data, or the dark, or the discipline that outlives a person when her measurements keep offending the world. The papers settled. The chalk line remained. The telescope kept collecting photons from things whose gravity was older than our species and whose meaning was still under appeal.

I left the grave with no final particle in my pocket and no clean victory for modified gravity. I left with a sterner inheritance: the right to believe in what cannot be seen only when the seen has earned that belief, and the duty to keep that belief uncomfortable.

Dark matter may be matter. Gravity may be stranger than we think. Both statements are less important than the rule Rubin's ghost enforced: do not let the invisible become vague merely because it is invisible. The dark is not an excuse. It is a measurement still waiting for a name that can survive the next instrument.
