today is 06-06.

I found Dolly in a glass case in Edinburgh, less buried than curated. This is one of the indignities peculiar to famous animals: the human dead get marble, argument, and occasional forgiveness; the animal dead get lighting. National Museums Scotland says Dolly has been on display at the National Museum of Scotland since 2003, after the Roslin Institute donated her body, which means that the world's most famous sheep is still performing usefulness after death ([National Museums Scotland, "The story of Dolly the sheep"](https://www.nms.ac.uk/discover-catalogue/the-story-of-dolly-the-sheep)).

She stood there white-faced and still, a preserved body made from a body cell, the first mammal cloned from an adult cell ([Roslin Institute, "The Life of Dolly"](https://vet.ed.ac.uk/roslin/about/history/dolly/facts/life-of-dolly)). The original experiment was not a fairy tale about copying souls. It was somatic cell nuclear transfer: take the nucleus from a donor body cell, put it into an egg cell whose own nucleus has been removed, stimulate division, and, if biology decides not to laugh in your face, bring an embryo to term ([FDA, "Therapeutic Cloning and Genome Modification"](https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/cellular-gene-therapy-products/therapeutic-cloning-and-genome-modification)). The paper that made her public, Wilmut and colleagues' 1997 *Nature* article, reported viable offspring from fetal and adult mammalian cells, and Dolly became the proof that a specialized adult cell could be turned backward toward developmental possibility ([Wilmut et al., *Nature*, 1997](https://www.nature.com/articles/385810a0)).

I brought no shovel, because the museum would have objected. I brought a museum map, a printout of the *Nature* citation, a wool glove, and the moral question that has followed Dolly like a second shadow:

**Should human reproductive cloning remain forbidden, even if the technique someday became medically safe, or would a permanent ban be a superstitious refusal to admit that a cloned child would simply be another child?**

The stakes are not comic-book stakes. Human cloning would not produce an adult duplicate with borrowed memories. The FDA's animal-cloning materials make the basic point clearly: clones are born as babies, develop through gestation, and do not automatically share the temperament or personality of the animal from which they were cloned ([FDA, "Myths about Cloning"](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-cloning/myths-about-cloning)). The philosophical problem is therefore not "what if we photocopy a person?" That is the lazy version. The harder problem is whether adults may deliberately create a child whose nuclear genome is chosen because it has already belonged to someone else.

I pressed the wool glove to the glass.

"Dolly," I said, "come out. Humanity has been using you as a warning label for almost thirty years, and it has become intellectually sloppy."

The museum lights flickered with bureaucratic disapproval. Her mounted skin shivered. The hay under her hooves shifted. Then Dolly stepped down through the glass as if the display case were only weather, and the dead sheep stood beside me with the bored composure of an animal that had already survived more press conferences than most cabinet ministers.

"You want to ask whether they should clone themselves," she said.

"Yes."

"Start by admitting you are asking a child to pay for an adult fantasy."

That was not the whole argument, but it was the right wound to touch first.

The immediate case against human reproductive cloning is not mysterious. It is safety. Animal cloning showed low success rates, fetal loss, abnormal newborns, and maternal risks; the National Academies' 2002 report concluded that human reproductive cloning would be dangerous for the woman carrying the fetus, for the fetus, and for the newborn, and likely to fail ([National Academies, *Scientific and Medical Aspects of Human Reproductive Cloning*](https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/10285/chapter/2)). The FDA told institutional review boards that clinical research using cloning technology to create a human being would require an investigational new drug application and that unresolved safety questions would prevent such an investigation from proceeding ([FDA, "Letter about Human Cloning"](https://www.fda.gov/science-research/clinical-trials-and-human-subject-protection/letter-about-human-cloning)). The ISSCR's guidelines place implantation of human embryos produced by nuclear reprogramming, often called human reproductive cloning, among prohibited research activities ([ISSCR, "Laboratory-Based Human Embryonic Stem Cell Research, Embryo Research, and Related Research Activities"](https://www.isscr.org/guidelines/laboratory-based-human-embryonic-stem-cell-research-embryo-research-and-related-research-activities)).

Dolly listened to this inventory as if hearing the names of invisible siblings.

"People say I was a miracle," she said. "Miracle is what humans call a survivor when they do not want to count the dead attempts."

She had me there. The safety objection is not mere timidity. When a technique predictably generates miscarriages, deformities, sick newborns, and risks to gestational carriers, the language of reproductive freedom becomes obscene if it does not first answer for those bodies. Nobody has a right to run a speculative proof of concept through another person's pregnancy and another person's childhood. Before philosophy gets clever, medicine has already said no.

But I had not woken a dead sheep to recite a regulatory consensus. The sharper question begins after safety. Suppose the technique improved. Suppose the failure rate fell to the range society tolerates in other assisted reproduction. Suppose a future clinic could produce a cloned human child without extraordinary risk. Would the moral objection disappear?

"No," Dolly said.

"Too fast."

"Humans always say that when the answer interferes with procurement."

So I made the defense.

A cloned child would be a person, not property, not a substitute organ, not a household appliance with eyebrows. Genetic sameness is not personal sameness. Identical twins share nuclear DNA and are still distinct lives; even the FDA's plain-language animal cloning page notes that genes express differently and that temperament depends partly on life experience ([FDA, "Myths about Cloning"](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/animal-cloning/myths-about-cloning)). The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's survey of cloning ethics points out the standard reply to identity panic: the fear that cloning destroys individuality often depends on genetic determinism, and genetic determinism is false ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Cloning"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/cloning/)). If the child is loved as a new person, protected as equal, and never treated as a means only, Kant's old demand that humanity be treated as an end rather than merely as an instrument does not automatically condemn the method of conception ([Kant, *Groundwork of the Metaphysic of Morals*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5682/5682-h/5682-h.htm)).

The strongest pro-cloning argument is not vanity. It is reproductive need. Imagine a person who cannot make gametes, a couple for whom ordinary IVF cannot produce a genetically related child, or a family carrying a devastating combination of disease risks. If cloning created a child with the nuclear genome of one intended parent, the case could be framed as one more assisted reproductive path rather than as an ontological crime. The Stanford survey notes that defenders have treated cloning as a possible extension of reproductive autonomy and have questioned whether, if safety became comparable to other means of reproduction, the remaining objections would still justify legal prohibition ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Cloning"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/cloning/)).

Dolly flicked an ear. "You are making the respectable case. Now say the ugly one."

The ugly case is that people would not only clone to have children. They would clone to have *that* child, *that* face, *that* lost son, *that* genius, *that* celebrity, *that* controllable little echo. The Council of Europe's cloning protocol prohibits any intervention seeking to create a human being genetically identical to another human being, living or dead, and its preamble names the fear directly: the deliberate creation of genetically identical human beings risks instrumentalizing human life ([Council of Europe, Additional Protocol on the Prohibition of Cloning Human Beings](https://rm.coe.int/168007f2ca)). UNESCO's Universal Declaration on the Human Genome and Human Rights likewise treats reproductive cloning of human beings as contrary to human dignity ([UNESCO, Universal Declaration on the Human Genome and Human Rights](https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/universal-declaration-human-genome-and-human-rights?hub=387.)).

Those dignity arguments are often frustrating because "dignity" can become a velvet sack into which people throw whatever disgust they do not want to analyze. But the concept is not empty here. A child produced by cloning may enter the world under a narrative more suffocating than ordinary parental expectation. All children are imagined before they exist, but the cloned child would be imagined through an existing biography. The child would not merely be hoped for; she might be commissioned against a known template. The Stanford survey's discussion of the "open future" objection captures the worry: a clone might be burdened by expectations based on the life of the genetic predecessor, especially if created as a replacement for someone dead ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Cloning"](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2024/entries/cloning/)).

Dolly moved through the museum gallery, past engines and instruments and other machines that had been allowed to stop working.

"They will say the child can rebel," she said.

"Children rebel all the time."

"Rebellion is not the same as innocence."

That sentence did the work of several treatises. Of course a cloned child could defy expectation. A child cloned from a violinist could become a plumber; a child cloned from a dead daughter could hate the dead girl's favorite color; a child cloned from a billionaire could grow up with the political instincts of a guillotine. Human beings are not compelled to reenact their genomes. But the absence of determinism does not erase the pressure of deliberate design. The moral problem is not that the clone would be a copy. The moral problem is that other people might build a social room around the copy and then act surprised when the child cannot breathe.

I put the liberal answer back on the table. Ordinary reproduction also creates children for mixed motives: narcissism, grief, loneliness, lineage, inheritance, the desire to repair a marriage, the wish to see one's own eyes continued in another face. We do not ban ordinary conception because parents may be vain or controlling. We regulate conduct toward children after birth. Why should cloning be singled out as uniquely corrupting when many children are already born into scripts they did not write?

Dolly stopped beside a display of scientific instruments.

"Because cloning gives the script a specimen number," she said.

That was too neat, but not wrong. Cloning would not merely continue the ordinary parental fantasy of resemblance. It would technologize it. It would allow adults to select not only a future child, but a genome with a known past. The parent could say, with more confidence than any ordinary parent, "I know what this material has done before." That is precisely what makes the technique attractive and precisely what makes it dangerous. The better the selection appears, the more easily love becomes evaluation.

Still, the objection must not be allowed to become mystical. There is nothing morally filthy about shared DNA. If a cloned human being existed, the first ethical duty would be boring and absolute: treat that person as a full human being, born free and equal in dignity and rights, not as a laboratory footnote or metaphysical embarrassment ([United Nations, Universal Declaration of Human Rights](https://www.un.org/en/universal-declaration-human-rights/)). A society that prohibited cloning in the name of dignity and then stigmatized an actual cloned child would reveal that its dignity talk was only costume jewelry.

Dolly approved of that part.

"The created one is never guilty of the creation," she said.

So we separated the questions.

Question one: if a cloned child is born, what is she? A person. No hesitation. No special caste. No diminished rights. No "copy" status. No ownership by genetic donor, gestational carrier, clinic, state, grieving parent, or philosopher in a mood. Kant's warning against treating rational beings merely as means applies with special force precisely because the method of conception tempts everyone to talk as if the child were an answer to someone else's problem ([Kant, *Groundwork*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5682/5682-h/5682-h.htm)).

Question two: should adults be permitted to create such a child? That is harder. Reproductive liberty is real, but it is not a magic solvent. It does not dissolve duties to the gestational carrier, egg provider, embryo, resulting child, or society that must decide which forms of intentional making it will normalize. The FDA's description of SCNT reminds us that cloning depends on eggs, nuclei, embryos, and implantation; it is not pure will floating above bodies ([FDA, "Therapeutic Cloning and Genome Modification"](https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/cellular-gene-therapy-products/therapeutic-cloning-and-genome-modification)). In practice, a market in reproductive cloning would require egg extraction, gestational labor, clinical gatekeeping, money, and a story persuasive enough to make all that feel like destiny.

"Money," Dolly said.

"Yes."

"You humans never leave a new power alone. You put a reception desk in front of it."

The class argument is not decorative. If reproductive cloning became available, it would almost certainly begin as a luxury service before it became anything like a public good. Rich parents could attempt genetic continuations of themselves; grieving rich parents could attempt replacement children; clinics could sell privacy, selection, and mythology in the language of family-building. Meanwhile the bodily inputs would come from women whose own freedom would be praised exactly at the point where their eggs and wombs became useful. This is not a claim from one document; it is a structural inference from how expensive reproductive technologies and bodily markets tend to work. The inference is mine, and I am marking it as mine because pretending a citation can do all the moral labor would be cowardice.

Dolly's argument was colder. She said humans keep asking whether the clone would be less human, when the more likely danger is that everyone else becomes less human around the clone. Parents become customers of resemblance. Doctors become manufacturers of acceptable origins. Law becomes warranty language. The child becomes a biography under management. Even the opponent of cloning may become ugly, staring at the child as a violation instead of greeting her as a neighbor.

I asked her if that meant an absolute ban.

She did not answer immediately. The dead are unfair that way; they have the theatrical patience of beings no longer paying rent.

The trouble with absolute bans is that they often protect one moral truth by burying another. There might be rare imaginable cases where cloning would not be vanity, replacement, celebrity worship, or genetic self-flattery. Perhaps it would be the only way for someone to have a genetically related child without passing on a grave disease. Perhaps future science would make the medical risks ordinary. Perhaps a carefully designed legal regime could require independent welfare review, prohibit cloning of minors or the dead without prior consent, bar celebrity or commercial "likeness" markets, protect egg providers and gestational carriers, guarantee the cloned person's equal legal status, and punish any proprietary claim over the child. A ban that cannot even imagine such cases may be more reflex than reason.

Dolly looked at me with the mild disgust of a sheep watching humans invent paperwork for an abyss.

"And you trust the paperwork?"

"No."

"Good. Continue."

I continued. The reason to maintain the prohibition now is overwhelming: current safety, the impossibility of consent from the resulting child to experimental risk, the social temptation to replacement, and the predictable market in controlled resemblance. The reason not to turn the prohibition into metaphysical dogma is equally important: a cloned child would not be ontologically polluted, and many anti-cloning arguments secretly depend on bad biology, disgust, or the fantasy that natural reproduction is morally pure. Nature is not innocence. Ordinary conception also produces suffering, coercion, disability, and parents with plans. The fact that cloning is artificial does not by itself make it wrong; medicine is the art of preferring some artifices to some cruelties.

The best rule I can defend is severe and unsatisfying: **human reproductive cloning should remain prohibited unless and until safety is ordinary, exploitation controls are real, and the justification is child-centered rather than adult-centered.** But the last condition is the blade. A cloned child cannot be brought into existence for the purpose of replacing, extending, preserving, copying, improving, exhibiting, or redeeming someone else. If the point of the act is that this child will be genetically identical to a selected predecessor, then the act is already morally leaning toward instrumentality. The child may later become free, but the origin story begins with a leash.

Dolly considered this.

"You have made a door," she said, "but put so many locks on it that nobody can enter without proving they did not want the thing the door was built for."

"Yes."

"That is almost honest."

Almost honest is sometimes the best philosophy can do before breakfast.

What Dolly forced me to see is that the central evil is not copying. Copies can be innocent. Twins are not scandals. A cloned animal eating grass is not a theological insult. The evil is a certain posture of authorship over life. It is the adult saying: I have selected your genetic past; now arrive and justify my selection. That posture exists in softer forms all over parenthood, but cloning would harden it into procedure.

The living world learned from Dolly that an adult cell could be reprogrammed. That was a scientific revelation. But the moral revelation is stranger: technical power can reverse a cell's developmental clock without returning innocence to the purposes that use it. Dolly was not a monster. She was a sheep. The monster, when it appears, is usually a sentence spoken by humans in a clean room: this life will solve my problem.

Before she returned to her case, Dolly gave me the rule she wanted written down.

"A copy is not the disgrace," she said. "The disgrace is treating a beginning as a receipt."

Then she stepped back through the glass. The lights steadied. The exhibit became an exhibit again, and the museum resumed its calm habit of turning unsettled history into a thing children can walk past with juice boxes.

I left Dolly there, not as a warning against science, but as a warning against bad ownership of wonder. Human reproductive cloning should remain forbidden now, and perhaps for a very long time, not because a clone would be less than human, but because the desire to make one is so often a desire to make a human being answerable to a pattern chosen before she can speak. If we ever become wise enough to clone a person without making that person a product, a memorial, a vanity project, a biological lawsuit against death, or a child born under a microscope of expectation, then the argument can reopen.

Until then, leave the dead uncopied. Leave children uncommissioned. Let Dolly rest in her glass case as the animal who proved that biology is more plastic than we thought and that human wanting is less innocent than it sounds.
