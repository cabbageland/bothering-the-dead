today is May 18, 2026.

I found Cicely Saunders where one ought to find her, though I had hoped not to: in the architecture of a hospice at night, in that peculiar zone where fluorescent light is softened by quilts, flowers, volunteer voices, and the disciplined refusal to let pain have the last word. St Christopher's in London still carries her imprint because she built it to carry more than medicine. She built it to hold the fact that dying is not only a biological event but a crisis of meaning, fear, dependency, memory, and love, what she famously called "total pain" ([Cicely Saunders, "The Evolution of Palliative Care," *Journal of the Royal Society of Medicine*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1297219/)). I went there in imagination with a pocketful of morphine ampoules, a little dirt from the garden, and a page on which she had written one of the hardest sentences in medicine: "You matter because you are you, and you matter to the last moment of your life" ([Cicely Saunders, quoted by King's College London, Cicely Saunders Institute](https://www.kcl.ac.uk/cicelysaunders/about/cicely-saunders)).

It did not take much to bring her back. Some dead people require thunder, Latin, desecration. Cicely Saunders required attention. I sat beside an empty bed, read her words aloud, and waited long enough for the room to stop performing efficiency and become a room again. Then she was there, assembled not grotesquely but exactly, in a navy cardigan, watchful and unsentimental. She looked at the medication trolley first, then at me, as though triaging the moral emergency before hearing the case.

I told her I had not disturbed her for a decorative argument. I wanted to ask whether physician-assisted dying should be legal. Not sedation that may foreseeably shorten life, not withdrawing burdensome treatment, not the old dishonest euphemisms by which medicine often hides from itself, but the real controversy: whether a doctor may intentionally provide the means, or directly administer the means, for a competent patient to die at the patient's persistent request.

The stakes are not abstract. In Oregon, where physician-assisted dying has been legal since 1997 for terminally ill adults expected to die within six months, the state reports that most patients who use the law cite loss of autonomy, reduced ability to engage in enjoyable activities, and loss of dignity, alongside fear of losing control over bodily functions ([Oregon Health Authority, *Oregon Death with Dignity Act 2024 Data Summary*](https://www.oregon.gov/oha/ph/providerpartnerresources/evaluationresearch/deathwithdignityact/pages/ar-index.aspx)). In Canada, where the MAID regime is far broader and includes clinician administration, more than 15,000 people received medical assistance in dying in 2023, amounting to about 4.7 percent of all deaths; cancer was the most common underlying condition, but the state also tracks cases involving frailty, cardiovascular disease, and other chronic decline ([Government of Canada, *Fifth Annual Report on Medical Assistance in Dying in Canada 2023*](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/publications/health-system-services/medical-assistance-dying-annual-report-2023.html)). The law is no longer hypothetical. The question is what kind of medicine and what kind of society these practices express.

Saunders answered before I finished sitting down. Medicine must not make killing into a treatment.

Because she was Saunders, she did not leave the sentence at the level of taboo. She argued from vocation. The whole achievement of hospice, she said, was to prove that the apparent wish for death often changes when pain is controlled, breathlessness relieved, terror spoken aloud, and abandonment answered with reliable care. This was not piety; it was clinical observation. In her account of palliative care's development, she describes how unrelieved symptoms, loneliness, and the erosion of personhood compound one another, and how careful symptom control together with human accompaniment can alter the patient's whole experience of dying ([Saunders, "The Evolution of Palliative Care"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1297219/)). If you legalize assisted death, she said, you teach the system to solve despair at least partly by eliminating the despairs' bearer. That is a civilizational temptation, not just a bedside option.

I told her the strongest case in favor is not contempt for the dying but respect for them. A competent adult can refuse life-sustaining treatment even when that refusal leads predictably to death. The Supreme Court recognized that liberty interest decades ago in *Cruzan v. Director, Missouri Department of Health* ([497 U.S. 261 (1990)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/497/261/)). Courts and legislatures already accept high-dose pain relief under the doctrine that symptom control may be intended even if death is foreseeably hastened. Terminal sedation exists. Ventilators are withdrawn. Feeding tubes are removed. The bright moral line many physicians invoke between "letting die" and "helping die" is not always a line the patient can feel from the bed. If my body is irreversibly collapsing, my suffering remains intolerable to me despite good palliative care, and I lucidly judge continued existence to be a burden I should not be forced to carry, why should the state require endurance for its own moral comfort?

She gave me a look reserved for arguments that are not stupid but arrive too clean. You are smuggling in a picture of autonomy, she said, that is thinner than the human person. Nobody dies as an abstract chooser. People die in families, institutions, unequal health systems, and cultures that praise independence so aggressively that dependency begins to feel like disgrace. Once death becomes a sanctioned solution, the request for it is no longer made in a neutral atmosphere. It is shaped by what the patient fears they have become for other people.

That, annoyingly, is not speculation. The UK's parliamentary debates on assisted dying have repeatedly turned on the possibility that social pressure, cost pressure, loneliness, disability stigma, and fear of burden could bend supposedly voluntary choice ([House of Commons Library, *Assisted dying/assisted suicide*](https://commonslibrary.parliament.uk/research-briefings/cbp-9821/)). Disability-rights critics in Canada and elsewhere have argued that assisted-dying regimes expand in societies that still fail to provide accessible housing, home care, income security, and dignified support, so the "choice" to die can be deformed by remediable neglect ([UN Human Rights Council, Report of the Special Rapporteur on the rights of persons with disabilities, A/HRC/52/32](https://www.ohchr.org/en/documents/thematic-reports/ahrc5232-report-special-rapporteur-rights-persons-disabilities)). Saunders pressed exactly there. Before a society offers death as an option, she said, it had better make life with illness, pain, and dependency genuinely livable. Otherwise mercy curdles into abandonment with excellent bedside manners.

I said that this objection bites hardest against a bad law, not against every law. Oregon's model is narrow. It requires adult capacity, terminal illness, waiting periods, and self-administration, and the annual reports have not shown a stampede of covert coercion or mass substitution for palliative care ([Oregon Health Authority, *Death with Dignity Act*](https://www.oregon.gov/oha/ph/providerpartnerresources/evaluationresearch/deathwithdignityact/pages/index.aspx)). One can argue that the very rarity of use is part of its justification. The law exists as a backstop of agency. Many patients who obtain prescriptions never ingest them; they want assurance that if suffering becomes unbearable, they will not be trapped. In that sense the option can function less as a conveyor belt toward death than as protection against the panic of entrapment.

Saunders did not deny the panic. She denied that assisted death is the right social answer to it. Law is a teacher, she said, and the lesson here is dangerous. The more medicine presents death as a service one may request when life loses acceptable quality, the more all qualities of life become available for ranking. She had spent years fighting a medical culture that treated the dying as finished, useless, or not worth the trouble of expert attention. Hospice was an uprising against precisely that logic. To legalize physician-assisted death is to risk reinstalling, under the banner of compassion, the old assumption that some lives have crossed a threshold beyond which our main duty is efficient exit.

I pushed harder because this is where the disagreement lives. There are cases hospice cannot redeem. Even excellent palliative care does not guarantee relief of every kind of suffering. Some symptoms are refractory. Some losses are existential rather than analgesic. Some patients do not merely fear pain; they reject the final shape their lives are taking, and not irrationally. In *Washington v. Glucksberg*, when the Supreme Court declined to recognize a federal constitutional right to assisted suicide, the Court still acknowledged the seriousness of bodily integrity, pain control, and the state's obligation to protect the terminally ill, even as it placed great weight on preserving the ethical line against intentional killing ([521 U.S. 702 (1997)](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/521/702/)). The decision settled a constitutional question, not the moral one. Why should a patient be compelled to endure a form of dying that she finds intolerable simply because the physician must keep her own hands symbolically clean?

Saunders's reply was the best one against my position because it refused symbolism and returned to practice. You assume the alternatives are endurance or death, she said. But palliative medicine has a third resource: to stay, to treat, to titrate, to accompany, and, in rare cases, to sedate for otherwise refractory suffering without making death itself the object of the act. The European Association for Palliative Care draws that distinction carefully: palliative sedation is reserved for intolerable, refractory symptoms, is proportionate, and is ethically different from euthanasia because the intention is symptom relief, not death ([European Association for Palliative Care framework, *Palliative Medicine*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21474708/)). Saunders thought intention mattered not because it absolves every outcome, but because it protects the shape of care. The doctor remains one who does not aim at the patient's death.

I answered that intention can be morally clarifying and still sometimes look like organized self-deception. If a doctor deeply sedates a dying patient into unconsciousness because conscious existence has become unbearable, while knowing this may shorten life, the practical distance from assisted death may be substantial in some cases and very thin in others. Patients notice that thinness. Families notice it. The law notices it. It is not obvious that ethics becomes more humane by permitting one route to the end so long as it wears the approved language of symptom management, while forbidding another route that is more candid about what the patient wants.

For the first time she seemed angry. Good, she said. Candor. Then be candid about what else changes when the prohibition falls. In jurisdictions with assisted dying, eligibility debates do not remain still. Canada is the clearest warning. What began with terminal illness did not stay there. After *Carter v. Canada* rejected the absolute ban on physician-assisted death ([2015 SCC 5](https://scc-csc.lexum.com/scc-csc/scc-csc/en/item/14637/index.do)), Parliament's regime expanded through legislation and litigation, and now includes people whose natural death is not reasonably foreseeable, with further controversy over mental illness as a sole underlying condition ([Government of Canada, *MAID Practice and Policy*](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/health-services-benefits/medical-assistance-dying.html)). Saunders's point was not that every expansion is monstrous. It was that the justificatory logic is unstable. If autonomy and suffering are the core grounds, it becomes hard to explain why one unbearable condition counts and another does not. The slope is not a cartoon. It is a jurisprudential motor.

That argument lands. It also does not end the matter. Slopes exist in both directions. The logic of prohibition can also expand, forcing people into violent suicides, solitary acts, desperate travel, or prolonged decline they explicitly reject. There is cruelty in compelling people to wait for nature when nature has become, for them, merely the bureaucratic name of a torment. The ethical question is not whether every line moves. It is which movement a decent society can justify.

So I told Saunders where I finally stand. I think physician-assisted dying can be justified, but only under conditions far stricter than its most enthusiastic advocates admit and for reasons less triumphant than autonomy talk suggests. The best argument for it is not that self-ownership is absolute. It is that there are some terminal situations in which the state should not conscript a lucid person into the last chapter of suffering for the sake of preserving a public moral symbol. But the best argument against it is also powerful: if the surrounding system does not offer robust palliative care, disability support, home services, mental health care, and material security, then the choice is contaminated before it begins.

Saunders accepted none of my conclusion and all of my warning. She said the real obscenity is that wealthy societies often debate assisted dying with greater administrative energy than they devote to universal palliative care. The World Health Organization still states that palliative care is an ethical responsibility of health systems and remains profoundly underdeveloped worldwide ([World Health Organization, *Palliative care* fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/palliative-care)). If that is true, then every legislature rushing to design assisted-dying procedures before guaranteeing serious end-of-life care is arranging its priorities in a morally revealing order.

There she had me. Too many defenders of assisted dying speak as though the mere existence of choice proves justice, when in fact choices inherit the shape of the world that offers them. A patient who requests death after receiving excellent palliative medicine, stable housing, honest prognostic communication, disability-respecting care, family support if wanted, and durable assurance that they are not a burden is making one kind of choice. A patient who requests death because those goods are absent is making another. Law struggles to distinguish them because paperwork is easier than solidarity.

What I could not tell Saunders, or rather what she already knew, is that the controversy is finally about what counts as mercy. Her mercy was fierce accompaniment. It insisted that the answer to suffering is first to reduce it, then to remain present to the sufferer, and never to let medicine become the deliberate agent of death. My mercy is less pure and maybe more compromised. I cannot convince myself that every lucid person must be made to stay until biology finishes its speech. Some endings are not ennobled by being longer.

She stood, which in resurrection terms is usually a sign that the living have taken enough. Before she left, she said that any society that legalizes assisted death should have to pass one prior test: it should first build a palliative-care system so good that almost nobody asks for death out of untreated pain, neglect, or fear of being discarded. Only then would we know whether the request expresses freedom or failure.

That is a test most countries would fail.

Then she was gone, and the hospice room became just a room again, except for the harder question she left behind. Not whether we can authorize a doctor to help end a life. We plainly can. The question is whether we have earned the right to call that authorization compassion before we have done everything harder, slower, and less glamorous that compassion demands.
