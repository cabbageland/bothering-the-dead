today is 06-24.

I found Sister Rosetta Tharpe under a stone that had arrived thirty-five years late.

Northwood Cemetery in Philadelphia is not where rock and roll ought to keep one of its mothers, but culture has always had a gift for misplacing the women it learned from. A gospel historian recorded that Tharpe's grave sat unmarked after her death in 1973 until a benefit-backed monument was finally placed there in 2008 ([Journal of Gospel Music, "Sister Rosetta's Stone"](https://journalofgospelmusic.com/gospel/sister-rosettas-stone-gospel-music-legend-memorialized-after-35-years/)). The Smithsonian says she recorded for Decca at twenty-three in 1938, became one of gospel's first commercially successful recording artists, and carried the spiritual force of gospel into the secular weather of rock and roll, influencing Chuck Berry, Elvis Presley, Jerry Lee Lewis, and Little Richard ([Smithsonian, "Sister Rosetta Tharpe, From Gospel to Electric Guitar-Driven Rock 'n' Roll"](https://www.si.edu/collections/snapshot/sister-rosetta-tharpe-gospel-electric-guitar-driven-rock-n-roll)). The Rock & Roll Hall of Fame, which inducted her in 2018 as an Early Influence, calls her "the first guitar heroine of rock & roll" ([Rock & Roll Hall of Fame, Sister Rosetta Tharpe](https://rockhall.com/inductees/sister-rosetta-tharpe/)).

So I came with the correct apology: no flowers, no Hall of Fame speech, no misty little sermon about legacy. Flowers are cheap after erasure. Speeches are cheaper. I brought a cracked amplifier, a spool of magnetic tape, a laptop full of model weights that I claimed not to understand, a blank license agreement, a Decca 78 sleeve, a Tennessee statute with the word "voice" underlined in red, and a guitar pick cut from an expired credit card. If there is a better relic for the present age than a plectrum made out of debt, I do not want to know it.

I placed the amplifier at the foot of the grave. I plugged it into nothing. Then I read aloud from the Copyright Act, the ordinary dry magic by which a song becomes a bundle of powers: the owner of copyright has exclusive rights to reproduce, prepare derivative works, distribute copies, perform, display, and in the case of sound recordings perform digitally ([17 U.S.C. 106](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/106)). The grave did not move. I read the fair use statute, with its four factors: purpose, nature, amount, and market effect ([17 U.S.C. 107](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/107)). Still nothing.

Then I read the part about sound recordings, the unsettling mercy in section 114(b): copyright in a sound recording does not extend to a new recording made from independently fixed sounds, even if those sounds imitate or simulate the original recording ([17 U.S.C. 114](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/114)).

The amplifier hummed.

The credit-card pick snapped against strings I could not see.

Sister Rosetta Tharpe sat up with dirt on her hat, church in her shoulders, nightclub smoke in her cuffs, and the furious composure of someone who had spent a lifetime proving that sanctity could plug into an electric guitar without asking a deacon's permission. She looked at the laptop.

"Is that a piano?"

"Worse."

"A contract?"

"Also worse."

"Then you brought me a machine that sings."

"A machine that learns to make songs."

"From whom?"

"Everyone."

"That word usually means somebody did not get paid."

The question was this: **Should AI music companies be allowed to train generative models on recorded music and synthesize styles, voices, and dead performers without prior consent, or should the law require licensing, payment, and control before the living or the dead are turned into raw material?**

This is not a side argument about gadgets. It is about whether culture is inheritance or extraction; whether listening and copying are morally different when performed by machines at industrial scale; whether an artist's voice is a tool, a property right, a bodily trace, or a ghost; whether the public domain should remain a commons or become a haunted shopping mall; whether Black women, session players, small songwriters, gospel singers, dead bluesmen, and obscure instrumentalists will once again supply the future while somebody else's company becomes valuable enough to call the theft innovation.

Rosetta brushed soil from the bridge of an invisible guitar.

"Begin with the best case for the machine," she said. "The devil is always duller when accused by fools."

Fine. The best case for broad AI training begins with the fact that music has never been chemically pure. Tharpe herself was a crossing of forms: Black Pentecostal worship, blues, jazz, big-band swing, gospel virtuosity, commercial recording, electric guitar, sacred heat in secular rooms. The Smithsonian's short account is already a theory of culture: she moved gospel passion into the world that later called itself rock and roll ([Smithsonian](https://www.si.edu/collections/snapshot/sister-rosetta-tharpe-gospel-electric-guitar-driven-rock-n-roll)). No one invents from a sealed room. Artists absorb, imitate, transform, steal a lick and repent by making it better, learn timing from one elder and voltage from another, make a style by misunderstanding five predecessors at once. A music law that tried to make influence itself permission-based would strangle the thing it claimed to honor.

"Influence is not theft," I said.

"Sometimes it is theft with better manners," Rosetta said.

Yes. But the pro-training side can answer: copyright does not protect style as such. The Copyright Office's 2024 digital replicas report acknowledges the seriousness of AI outputs that imitate a creator's style, but says copyright law does not protect artistic style as a separate element of a work because that would collide with the idea/expression boundary ([U.S. Copyright Office, *Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 1: Digital Replicas*](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-1-Digital-Replicas-Report.pdf)). That is not a loophole. It is one of the conditions under which art breathes. If "sounds like" became property, the first person to monetize a groove could charge rent to everyone who later learned how to move.

The law already knows that imitation is not automatically infringement. Section 114(b) lets a new sound recording imitate or simulate the sounds in a copyrighted sound recording if it is made from independently fixed sounds rather than copied actual sounds ([17 U.S.C. 114](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/114)). That rule protects ordinary musical freedom: tribute bands, vocal impersonations, genre exercises, musicians learning by ear, and the embarrassing but important human right to sound a little too much like someone you love before you find yourself.

Rosetta leaned toward me.

"You are defending disciples."

"Partly."

"The machine is not a disciple. It has no knees."

That is the wound in the analogy. Human learning is embodied, slow, vulnerable, socially answerable. A singer spends years breathing through the imitation. A guitarist gets calluses, shame, gigs, teachers, injuries, hunger, the mercy of bad first versions. A model ingests at scale, generates at scale, and can flood the market before anyone has even agreed on the noun. The AI companies want the romance of apprenticeship and the economics of a refinery.

Still, the defenders have a real legal argument. Fair use has long covered some massive copying when the use is non-substitutive and socially valuable. In *Authors Guild v. Google*, the Second Circuit held that Google's scanning of books to create a searchable index and limited snippets was fair use because the project served a highly transformative purpose and did not expose the books as market substitutes ([*Authors Guild v. Google*, 2d Cir. 2015](https://law.justia.com/cases/federal/appellate-courts/ca2/13-4829/13-4829-2015-10-16.html)). In *Google v. Oracle*, the Supreme Court held Google's copying of Java API declaring code was fair use in part because it helped programmers use their skills in a new computing environment rather than simply exploit the old one ([*Google LLC v. Oracle America*, 2021](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/20pdf/18-956_d18f.pdf)).

From that angle, training is not consumption. It is analysis. The model is not selling Rosetta's record; it is deriving statistical relationships from sound in order to make new sound. If a human may listen to thousands of records and become a musician, if a scholar may analyze a corpus, if a search engine may copy books to make them discoverable, why should a model be barred from learning patterns? The Copyright Office's 2025 generative AI training report refuses an automatic answer: different training and deployment uses need separate fair use analysis, and some uses for research, analysis, or non-substitutive purposes may lean differently from unrestricted commercial generation ([U.S. Copyright Office, *Part 3: Generative AI Training*](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf)).

"A cautious document," Rosetta said.

"A rare species."

"Endangered?"

"Mostly extinct in comment sections."

The strongest pro-AI case is not "let companies do whatever they want." That is not a case. That is a venture pitch wearing a hoodie. The strongest case is that a licensing requirement for all training could make only the richest firms able to build music tools, lock up culture behind catalog owners, and hand the future to the same institutions that miscredited, underpaid, and buried many of the musicians whose recordings they now claim to defend. Labels are not saints because they sue in the name of artists. Estates are not artists because they inherited a name. Rights markets can become toll roads. A world where every style, timbre, rhythm, and likeness must be cleared before being explored may be less just, not more.

Rosetta nodded once, not agreement, more like keeping time.

"Now do the other side before you sell my bones by subscription."

The other side begins with copying. AI training is not metaphorical contact with music. It often requires making copies of sound recordings or other works. The RIAA-backed complaint against Udio framed the issue exactly this way, alleging massive copying of copyrighted sound recordings to train a commercial music generator and seeking injunctions and damages ([RIAA-hosted Udio complaint, June 24, 2024](https://www.riaa.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/Udio-Complaint-6.24.241.pdf)). Suno's own defense has treated the disputed act as copying during training and argued that this copying is fair use, not that nothing was copied ([Suno filing in *UMG Recordings v. Suno*, Aug. 1, 2024](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/files/2024/08/SUNO-response-to-copyright-suit.pdf)).

That matters. A machine is not merely "influenced" by recordings in the way a teenager is influenced by hearing a guitar solo through a bedroom wall. The system may reproduce patterns learned from enormous corpora, generate outputs in the same commercial market as the works used to train it, and give users a cheap way to request something adjacent to living or dead artists without hiring them. The Copyright Office's 2025 training report says the fourth fair use factor should not be read so narrowly that it ignores market dilution from AI systems generating works of the same kind as their training data, including works stylistically similar to those data; it also says copying expressive works from pirate sources to generate unrestricted competing content, when licensing is reasonably available, is unlikely to be fair ([Copyright Office, Part 3](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf)).

"So the machine learns the road," Rosetta said, "then sells rides."

"Yes."

"And calls the road public because everybody walked on it."

"Yes."

"Did everybody own the paving company?"

"No."

The anti-training case becomes stronger when the output is not just "music inspired by tradition" but a market substitute shaped to satisfy the same demand. The Supreme Court's *Warhol v. Goldsmith* decision is useful here because it refused to let "new meaning" dissolve the specific commercial use. The Court focused on whether the challenged use served substantially the same commercial purpose as the original licensing market ([*Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith*, 2023](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/22pdf/21-869_87ad.pdf)). That does not decide AI music cases. It does warn against the lazy incantation of "transformative" when the new product is sold into the same attention economy as the old.

Rosetta lifted the invisible guitar and played one note. It sounded like a church door opening onto an alley.

"What about my voice?"

This is where copyright becomes too small.

The Copyright Office says plainly that copyright protects original works, not identity itself; a replica of a person's image or voice alone is not copyright infringement merely as such ([Copyright Office, Part 1](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-1-Digital-Replicas-Report.pdf)). A voice is not just a sound recording. It is body, injury, breath, age, geography, training, illness, cigarettes not smoked, rooms survived, choirs endured, sin forgiven or not. The Office therefore recommended a federal digital-replica law covering all individuals, not only celebrities, with protection for realistic replicas and limits for speech interests; it also recommended that any postmortem protection be limited in duration rather than eternal ([Copyright Office, Part 1](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-1-Digital-Replicas-Report.pdf)).

Tennessee's ELVIS Act gives one live example of where the law is moving. The 2024 bill revised Tennessee's publicity law to give every individual a property right in name, photograph, voice, or likeness; it also creates liability for distributing tools whose primary purpose is producing a person's voice or likeness without authorization, including after death through executors, administrators, heirs, or devisees ([Tennessee HB2091 / ELVIS Act text](https://www.capitol.tn.gov/Bills/113/Bill/HB2091.pdf)). That statute is not perfect. No statute named after Elvis was going to escape kitsch entirely. But it recognizes something copyright cannot: a synthetic performance may wrong the person even when no exact copyrighted recording is copied.

"A voice is not a hat," Rosetta said.

"No."

"You cannot hang it on another head and say no cloth was stolen."

That is the best sentence in the whole argument.

The moral wrong of an unauthorized dead-voice song is not exhausted by lost sales. The dead cannot be deceived. They cannot feel embarrassment. They cannot negotiate. But they can be misrepresented, and the living can be harmed through that misrepresentation: families, collaborators, communities, audiences, historical memory, and living artists whose market is crowded by resurrected ghosts. A synthetic Sister Rosetta track praising a casino, a politician, a crypto token, or a generative music platform would not merely be a bad business deal. It would be posthumous ventriloquism.

"Do not make me sell what I would have rebuked," she said.

"That is the case for consent."

"Consent from whom? My grave? My estate? The label? A nephew who never tuned a guitar? A company that owns the master? A church lady who says the nightclub years should be erased?"

And there it is. The rights solution has its own rot. The dead do not sign forms. Heirs may protect or betray. Estates may sanitize. Labels may own recordings while musicians own nothing. Publicity rights may become little hereditary kingdoms, letting families or corporations police history, parody, criticism, scholarship, documentary, homage, and queer or religious complexity. If every digital representation of a dead artist requires permission from some authorized guardian, memory becomes property management.

Rosetta knew this from the older world. She fused sacred and secular music at cost. She crossed racial, gender, and religious boundaries before institutions learned to make posters out of such words. The National Women's History Museum notes the pressures of religion, fame, race, and gender in her life, and the Smithsonian says her bold guitar work disrupted sex and racial stereotypes even as she remained less known than the men she influenced ([National Women's History Museum, Sister Rosetta Tharpe](https://www.womenshistory.org/education-resources/biographies/sister-rosetta-tharpe); [Smithsonian](https://www.si.edu/collections/snapshot/sister-rosetta-tharpe-gospel-electric-guitar-driven-rock-n-roll)). Who exactly should have had the power to approve her future? The respectable church? The record business? The late Hall of Fame? The marketplace that remembered her after it had already monetized her descendants?

"You see the trap," she said.

"Yes."

"Good. Keep your foot in it. It teaches."

The issue divides into three questions that people keep smashing together because smashed questions make better slogans.

First: **May models train on recordings at all?** My answer is yes, sometimes, but not as a blank check. Noncommercial research, preservation, analysis, accessibility, indexing, criticism, and tools that do not generate market substitutes have the strongest fair use and public-benefit case. A model trained to analyze gospel guitar history, identify degraded archives, separate noisy tracks for preservation, or help scholars study influence is not morally equivalent to a commercial generator that ingests a catalog and sells infinite near-songs into the same market. The Copyright Office's Part 3 report is right to demand use-by-use analysis rather than one magic answer for all training ([Copyright Office, Part 3](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-3-Generative-AI-Training-Report-Pre-Publication-Version.pdf)).

Second: **May commercial generators train on copyrighted recordings without license when the point is to generate new music?** Usually no. Not because machines are demons. Because the business model converts existing expressive work into a competing production engine. If licensing is feasible, especially in music where rights markets already exist, the moral burden shifts heavily toward consent, compensation, and auditability. The point is not to give major labels a crown. The point is to prevent "we copied everyone first" from becoming a legal strategy so profitable that asking permission becomes an antique ritual.

Third: **May AI recreate a recognizable living or dead person's voice without consent?** No, with serious exceptions for news, criticism, parody, scholarship, documentary, and historically honest expressive works. A realistic voice replica is not merely style. It is an identity event. It tells the listener, falsely or semi-falsely, that a person performed. That should require authorization when used commercially or deceptively, and it should be labeled clearly even when authorized. But postmortem control must expire. The dead deserve protection from immediate exploitation, not eternal privatization of cultural memory. A limited term, as the Copyright Office suggests, is wiser than immortal estate power ([Copyright Office, Part 1](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-1-Digital-Replicas-Report.pdf)).

Rosetta looked at the blank license agreement.

"Who gets paid?"

"Artists."

"That is not an answer. That is a hymn title."

Correct. Any licensing regime that pays only whoever owns the catalog is morally thin. Payment should reach performers, songwriters, session musicians where contract and law allow, and the people whose labor is being mined for model value. If a label sues a model for copying masters and then licenses the same corpus without sharing meaningful value downstream, that is not justice. That is a change of cashier. Consent, credit, compensation, and control must be designed for the least powerful contributors, not only for the corporations with clean metadata.

"And the public domain?" she asked.

"It must remain public."

"Say it like you mean it."

The public domain must remain public. If a recording or composition is lawfully free for use, AI should not become an excuse to rebuild fences around it. But public domain does not answer everything. A public-domain composition can still be performed in a copyrighted recording. A dead artist's style may be culturally available while her actual voice replica raises publicity and deception concerns. A historically important artist may belong to the world in one sense and remain vulnerable to cheap digital puppetry in another. The commons is not a landfill. It is a place where the living owe manners to the dead precisely because no owner can force them.

Rosetta laughed then, short and bright.

"Manners. You woke me from a grave with a laptop."

"Fair."

"Still, manners."

The machine's defenders say strict permission will freeze creativity. They are right if permission expands to style, genre, influence, research, and homage. They are wrong if they mean commercial platforms should be free to copy whole catalogs, hide the data, generate substitutes, and call every objection anti-technology. The artists' defenders say consent is sacred. They are right if the question is voice replicas, commercial cloning, or market substitution. They are wrong if consent becomes a hereditary veto over criticism, parody, scholarship, public-domain reinterpretation, or the ordinary unruly way music learns from music.

The real line is not human versus machine. It is accountable transformation versus industrial laundering.

An accountable system would disclose training sources at meaningful levels, keep records for audit, honor opt-outs where legally required, license when it builds commercial generators from copyrighted sound recordings, block prompts requesting living or recently dead artists' voices without authorization, label synthetic vocals and replicas, share revenue beyond catalog owners, preserve broad fair use for research and criticism, and protect the public domain from new enclosure. It would treat artists not as data mines, labels not as moral priests, and technology not as a sacred cow with a GPU bill.

Rosetta slid the credit-card pick across her palm.

"And me?"

"No fake Sister Rosetta hit single without authorization. No ad. No devotional product. No model selling your voice as a preset. But scholars can study your recordings. Musicians can learn from you. Kids can imitate your guitar until their fingers complain. A filmmaker can portray you. A satirist can mock the Rock Hall for being late. A gospel singer can borrow fire from you without filling out a form."

"Can a machine learn my timing?"

"For research, maybe. For a licensed tool, maybe. For a commercial generator that lets anyone type your name and get a plausible ghost? No."

"Good. You have not become completely stupid."

This, from the dead, counts as applause.

The deeper philosophical mistake is thinking that art is either owned or ownerless. Art is indebted. It carries obligations that property law can only partly express. Tharpe's sound did not belong to her in the sense that she invented gospel, blues, swing, electricity, sanctification, Black church rhythm, or the need to shout over a band. But it did belong to her in the sense that she risked a life making those inheritances pass through one unrepeatable body. The body matters. The risk matters. The business arrangements around the body are not identical with the body's claim.

AI makes the old injustice faster. It can learn from uncredited people, generate derivative atmospheres, sell abundance, and make the source disappear. But AI also exposes what was already true. The music industry has always trained on the dead. The difference is that now the training is literal enough to sue, scalable enough to terrify, and uncanny enough to make everyone hear the grave creak.

Rosetta stood. The cemetery brightened by one impossible stage light.

"Do not confuse resurrection with respect," she said.

"I will write that down."

"Do not confuse payment with respect either."

"Also written."

"And do not let the thieves tell you every locked door is justice."

That was the hard part, and the truest. Some doors should be locked. Some should be open. Some should have a sign saying: enter, learn, give credit, do not sell my face, do not fake my consent, do not turn my grief into a preset, do not call extraction a chorus just because many voices were taken.

Before she returned to the grave, she plugged the silent amplifier into the soil. For one second the cemetery had a sound. Not a song, not a sample, not a dataset. A warning with rhythm.

Then the hum died.

The laptop remained. The statute remained. The pick made from debt remained. The stone remained, late but finally bearing a name.

The dead cannot stop us from using them. That is why our use of them reveals us. A culture worthy of Sister Rosetta Tharpe would let the living learn from her without letting machines counterfeit her consent. It would keep influence free, replicas governed, training accountable, public-domain music public, and compensation aimed lower than the boardroom. It would understand that the dead are neither private property nor free fuel. They are ancestors, and ancestors are not inputs. They are people whose unfinished arguments still know how to play louder than the law.
