today is 06-22.

I found Elizabeth Magie Phillips in Columbia Gardens Cemetery in Arlington, Virginia, under a stone that does not look angry enough.

The stone had the calm little grammar of respectable death: names, dates, family adjacency, the ordinary compression by which a life becomes a label. The Library of Congress says Magie died in 1948 and was buried in Arlington, Virginia; Find a Grave gives the cemetery as Columbia Gardens ([Library of Congress, "The Very Fascinating Elizabeth J. Magie"](https://blogs.loc.gov/inside_adams/2022/09/elizabeth-j-magie/); [Find a Grave memorial](https://www.findagrave.com/memorial/100848078/lizzie-magie)). This is not where most people think Monopoly is buried, because most people do not know Monopoly has a grave. They know the little metal hat, the hotel, the orange properties, the red rage of a sibling who has just landed on Boardwalk with a hotel and discovered that capitalism has rules for humiliation.

Magie did not invent humiliation, but she patented a machine for teaching it. In 1904, the U.S. Patent Office issued her patent for *The Landlord's Game*, a board game whose object in the first patent was to accumulate the most wealth ([US748626A, "Game-board"](https://patents.google.com/patent/US748626A/en)). Twenty years later, her second patent made the lesson explicit: the game was "educational in its nature" and meant to show how, under prevailing land tenure, the landlord had an advantage and how the single tax would discourage land speculation ([US1509312A, "Game board"](https://patents.google.com/patent/US1509312A/en)). The Smithsonian's National Museum of American History describes the later Monopoly as based heavily on modified versions of Magie's game, though Charles Darrow's 1935 Parker Brothers version became the famous one ([Smithsonian, "Playing Monopoly (and its discontents)"](https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/stories/playing-monopoly-and-its-discontents-its-80th-anniversary)).

So I did not bring flowers. Flowers are bad evidence.

I brought a folding board painted with streets, a property-tax bill, a hotel no larger than a tooth, a copy of Henry George's *Progress and Poverty*, a red deed card marked LAND HELD OUT OF USE, and a small silver thimble because every resurrection needs a token that looks useless until the rent comes due. I put the board on the grass above her grave. I placed the houses carefully, not because order matters to the dead, but because disorder is how landlords explain themselves when the invoice arrives.

Then I read aloud from Magie's patent, the sentence about the landlord's advantage. The little hotel shivered. The dice rolled themselves. The thimble advanced seven spaces and stopped on a square I had not painted: WAGES.

The grave opened like a cash drawer.

Magie sat up with dirt on her sleeves and the alert, irritated look of someone who had designed a critique and watched it become family entertainment. She was not theatrical. She was worse: pedagogical.

"Did they learn?" she asked.

"They learned to buy railroads."

"Then the game worked and failed."

The question was this: **Should cities and states tax land value heavily, even radically, to break speculation and make housing more affordable, or is the land-value-tax dream a morally satisfying shortcut that will punish ordinary owners while failing to build enough homes?**

The stakes are not antique. Housing is now where political philosophy pays rent. Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies reported in 2026 that payments on a median-priced U.S. home remained nearly double their late-2020 level, that a household needed about $120,800 in income at the end of 2025 to afford such payments, and that only 16 percent of renter households met that threshold ([JCHS, *The State of the Nation's Housing 2026*](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/reports/files/Harvard_JCHS_The_State_of_the_Nations_Housing_2026_0.pdf)). Its 2026 rental report says the number of cost-burdened renters reached another record high even as some new lease rents softened ([JCHS, *America's Rental Housing 2026*](https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/americas-rental-housing-2026)). The argument is about monthly terror, not seminar hygiene: who gets to live near work, who inherits appreciating dirt, who is priced out of the city they make valuable, who treats an empty lot as an investment instrument, and whether public revenue should come from labor or from location.

Magie brushed cemetery soil off the board.

"Begin with the land," she said. "Humans always begin with the building because it has walls. The theft is usually under it."

Fine. The Georgist case begins with a distinction so simple it sounds like a trick: people make houses, but they do not make land. They improve land, fence it, drain it, pollute it, survey it, defend it, title it, finance it, and fight about it in zoning hearings with operatic grievance over a parking space. But the land itself, and especially the location value of land, comes largely from nature and society: rivers, climate, transit, schools, jobs, parks, neighbors, courts, public safety, sewer lines, cafes, museums, sidewalks, history, and the dense human fact of everybody else being nearby.

Henry George's argument in *Progress and Poverty* was that poverty persisted amid material progress because land values rose with social development and private landowners captured that rise as rent; his remedy was to take land rent for public use through a tax on land values rather than taxing labor and capital ([Henry George, *Progress and Poverty*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/55308)). Adam Smith, not exactly a beach radical with a banjo, also treated ground rents as an unusually fit subject for taxation because the owner often receives them from social improvement rather than personal effort ([Adam Smith, *The Wealth of Nations*, Book V](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3300/3300-h/3300-h.htm)). Modern tax economists still find the basic efficiency point hard to kill: the Mirrlees Review says the case for taxing land values is strong, while business property taxes are much more distortionary ([Institute for Fiscal Studies, "The Taxation of Land and Property"](https://ifs.org.uk/books/16-taxation-land-and-property)); an IMF working paper begins from the standard result that land value taxation is efficient because it does not distort the supply of land ([Schwerhoff, Edenhofer, and Fleurbaey, "Equity and Efficiency Effects of Land Value Taxation"](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/wp/2022/english/wpiea2022263-print-pdf.pdf)).

"Land does not flee," Magie said.

"Neither do poor people, if they cannot afford a truck."

"That is why they are easier to tax."

That was the first sharp edge. The usual tax system often punishes motion. Work more, pay income tax. Build more, pay higher taxes on improvements. Sell a property, pay transfer taxes. Start a business, pay payroll taxes, permit fees, commercial rents, and possibly taxes on the building you improved. Meanwhile, a well-located vacant parcel can sit in a city like a silent aristocrat, gaining value from transit, population growth, public investment, and the labor of everyone around it. A land value tax says: stop taxing the act of making useful things so heavily; tax the value of exclusive control over a site.

Magie's patent had already staged the moral intuition. The 1924 board included land in use, land held out of use, wages, trespass, jail, railroads, public utilities, and speculative real-estate offices ([US1509312A](https://patents.google.com/patent/US1509312A/en)). This was not a neutral toy. It was a little courtroom. Each circuit around the board asked whether rent is payment for productive contribution or tribute extracted from exclusion.

"Do not say all landlords are parasites," she said.

"I was about to."

"Then you were about to be lazy."

She was right. The strong argument for land value taxation does not require every landlord to be a cartoon villain stroking a mortgage. A landlord can maintain a building, take risk, provide housing, deal with repairs at 2 a.m., carry debt, comply with law, and still receive part of the rent because of land value that neither landlord nor tenant created alone. The morally clean target is not every person who rents out a unit. The target is the unearned increment: the rise in site value produced by surrounding society and locked behind private title.

John Locke is useful here because both sides try to draft him. His labor theory says a person has property in their own person and acquires property by mixing labor with what nature provides, so long as "enough, and as good" is left in common for others ([Locke, *Second Treatise*, Chapter V](https://press-pubs.uchicago.edu/founders/documents/v1ch16s3.html)). The property-rights defender points to the labor: the cleared field, the built house, the repaired roof, the taxes paid, the mortgage endured. The Georgist points to the proviso: in a crowded city, where all comparable sites are owned and the poor must pay admission to exist near opportunity, what exactly is left "enough, and as good"?

Magie picked up the thimble.

"If all the squares are owned," she said, "liberty becomes the right to roll."

The pro-LVT case becomes brutal in a housing crisis. If a city taxes buildings heavily, it taxes the addition of homes. If it taxes land heavily, it pressures owners of valuable underused sites to build, sell to someone who will build, or pay the public for withholding scarce location. Split-rate property taxes, which tax land at a higher rate than improvements, are not identical to a pure single tax, but they embody the same incentive. Empirical evidence is messy, local, and less miraculous than campaign slogans; still, the Lincoln Institute's review of land-value-tax research notes studies finding that split-rate reforms in Pennsylvania did not hurt building and may have encouraged it ([Lincoln Institute, *Land Value Taxation*](https://www.lincolninst.edu/app/uploads/legacy-files/pubfiles/land-value-taxation-chp.pdf)). A later working paper on Pennsylvania two-rate taxes found increases in housing density from such shifts ([Yang, "The Effects of the Two-Rate Property Tax"](https://etd723z5379.exactdn.com/app/uploads/2024/04/yang_wp14zy1.pdf)).

"May," Magie said. "Could. Might."

"You prefer certainty?"

"I prefer a reformer who has met a county assessor."

That is the best objection, and it is not merely technical. Land value taxation sounds like philosophy until it becomes assessment. What is the unimproved value of a parcel under a condominium tower? What is the land value of a corner lot whose zoning may change next year? What happens in neighborhoods where assessments lag, political appeals are easier for rich owners, and elderly residents are land-rich but cash-poor? What happens when a city raises land taxes before it has changed zoning enough to let owners actually build? What happens to a small church, a family store, a trailer park, a community garden, a Black homeowner in a rapidly appreciating area, or a widow whose block becomes fashionable because a transit station arrives and a developer starts sending letters with false warmth?

"The tax is efficient," I said.

"Efficient at what speed does it become cruelty?"

There it is. Economists admire land taxes because land cannot hide in Luxembourg, but households are not parcels. A clean model can say the burden of an anticipated land tax capitalizes into lower land prices. A living owner can say: I bought under one set of expectations, my retirement is in this dirt, my income did not rise with the assessed value, and now reform has arrived wearing the face of justice and holding my bill. Constitutional law is not the whole moral answer, but it does remind us that property is not nothing: the Fifth Amendment forbids taking private property for public use without just compensation ([Library of Congress, Fifth Amendment](https://constitution.congress.gov/constitution/amendment-5/)). A tax is not automatically a taking. But a political philosophy that treats security of tenure as sentimental debris deserves to lose.

Magie smiled.

"Good. Now defend the tax anyway."

Fine. The answer is not to spare land rent because transition is hard. The answer is to design transition like adults. Phase the tax in over years. Use circuit breakers for low-income owner-occupants. Allow deferral until sale or inheritance for vulnerable households, with safeguards against using hardship cases as human shields for major landowners. Update assessments regularly and transparently, because stale property values create quiet theft; the OECD stresses that regular valuation is key to making recurrent property taxes efficient, fair, and useful ([OECD, *Housing Taxation in OECD Countries*](https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/2022/07/housing-taxation-in-oecd-countries_1ee79763.html)). Pair LVT with zoning reform so owners are not taxed as if they can build apartments while the code still forces them to build a museum of grass. Replace worse taxes where possible: reduce taxes on improvements, business activity, and transactions, rather than simply adding one more claw to the public fist.

"You have made my beautiful heresy boring," Magie said.

"That is how reforms survive first contact with lawyers."

"A tragic species."

The second objection is that land value taxation cannot solve housing affordability by itself. This is also true. A city can tax land and still ban apartments. It can tax land and still have expensive materials, high interest rates, slow permitting, labor shortages, environmental risk, insurance spikes, exclusionary school boundaries, tenant displacement, weak public housing, and investors who understand the new rules before the tenants do. If the rent is high because supply is throttled, incomes are unequal, and public housing is starved, a land tax alone will not perform salvation. The board is not the city; the dice are not labor markets; the bank is not the Federal Reserve, though it sometimes has the same sense of humor.

Magie tapped the WAGES square.

"The single tax was never meant to be a scented candle."

George really did think land rent was the central distributive lever. He argued that social progress increased land values and that capturing those values could abolish poverty without punishing labor ([George, *Progress and Poverty*](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/55308)). That grandeur is part of the appeal and part of the danger. One beautiful cause of injustice is easier to understand than twelve ugly interacting causes. Land matters enormously. It does not explain everything. A serious housing politics needs land taxation, abundant legal housing, social housing, tenant protections, public transit, anti-displacement policy, credit access, construction capacity, and a state that can build without turning every project into a ritual sacrifice for consultants.

"So you dilute me," she said.

"No. I place you in a coalition."

"That is dilution with meeting notes."

She was not entirely wrong. Reforms get domesticated by coalition. But purity gets domesticated by failure. A pure land value tax with no supply reform may become a pressure cooker. Pure upzoning with no capture of land value may hand public decisions to landowners as windfalls. Pure rent control with no construction may protect some tenants while tightening scarcity for others. Pure market supply with no affordability policy may build towers of rentable air above people sleeping below the balconies. The controversy is not whether one tool is sacred. The controversy is whether we are willing to attack the rent relation from more than one angle without pretending every angle is the whole geometry.

The third objection is political: land value taxation is legible enough to anger everyone and invisible enough to inspire almost no one. Income taxes are hated but familiar. Sales taxes are regressive but quiet at the point of injury. Property taxes already make people feel hunted in their kitchens. A new land tax can be demagogued as a war on homeowners, a plot by urbanists, a gift to developers, a theft from retirees, a spreadsheet fantasy, or the first step toward the abolition of private property. Some of these attacks will be cynical. Some will be scared. The scared ones matter.

"Your opponents will bring grandmothers," Magie said.

"They always do."

"Some will be actual grandmothers."

"Yes."

"And some will be limited-liability companies holding parking lots under grandmother masks."

"Also yes."

The task is to distinguish them. A land tax that cannot tell the difference between an elderly owner-occupant with fixed income and a corporate land bank sitting on parcels near transit is not just politically weak; it is morally imprecise. But a politics that lets every major owner hide behind one sympathetic homeowner is also morally rotten. The most expensive word in public policy is "ordinary." Ordinary homeowners, ordinary landlords, ordinary investors, ordinary taxpayers. Everyone becomes ordinary when the bill arrives.

Magie put a hotel on my copy of Locke.

"What is your principle?"

"Land value created by the community should be returned substantially to the community, while improvements created by labor should be taxed less, and household security should be protected during the transition."

"Substantially. You brought an adverb to a knife fight."

"Yes, because the knife fight has probate."

Here is the harder moral claim. Private land ownership is a useful legal institution. It gives people security, encourages investment, protects homes from arbitrary seizure, and lets families root themselves. But absolute private appropriation of location value is not sacred. It is a social bargain that has been mistaken for a metaphysical right. The community makes land valuable, then pays private owners for access to the value it helped create. That is not the only cause of housing misery, but it is one of the cleanest injustices hiding in plain sight.

The best defense of private ownership says that without secure rights, people will not build, maintain, or plan. The best Georgist reply says: good, secure the building and the labor; tax the site rent. Do not confiscate a kitchen because a family painted it. Do not punish an apartment owner for adding units. Do not tax a shop more because it opened its doors. But do ask why a vacant lot next to a public train station should become more valuable because everyone else built a city around it while the owner contributes weeds and patience.

"Patience is a business model," Magie said.

"So is extortion, if described calmly."

The fourth objection comes from tenants: will landlords simply pass the land tax into rent? In a tight market, landlords already charge what the market will bear. A tax on structures or services can be passed on when supply changes; a tax on land, whose supply is fixed, is theoretically harder to pass forward in the same way because the land exists regardless of the tax. The IMF paper's efficiency premise rests on that fixed-supply logic ([Schwerhoff et al.](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/wp/2022/english/wpiea2022263-print-pdf.pdf)). But tenants do not live in theory. In the short run, landlords can try to raise rents, especially where vacancy is low and tenants lack bargaining power. Even if the economic incidence falls on landowners over time, the political pain can be misdirected through leases, fees, harassment, or redevelopment pressure.

Magie looked pleased that I had not made the tax magic.

"Then?"

"Tenant protections during implementation. Anti-harassment enforcement. Right-to-counsel in eviction where possible. Use revenue for affordable housing and renter support. Build enough homes that landlords cannot convert every public reform into a private threat."

"You have made the board untidy."

"The board was always lying about the number of players."

The fifth objection is philosophical. Why should the community get the rent? "Community" is not an angel. A city council can waste money, subsidize stadiums, underfund inspectors, overpay contractors, brutalize unhoused people, or give tax increment financing to the same interests it claims to regulate. Public capture of land value is not automatically public justice. A corrupt state can be a worse landlord than a private one, because it brings police, permits, and press conferences.

This objection has force. George sometimes wrote as if shifting rent to the public would wash the social organism clean. It will not. Revenue is only morally better than private rent if it is governed better. Land value taxation needs democratic budgeting, clear public benefit, anti-corruption rules, and visible returns: transit, schools, sewers, parks, housing, climate resilience, homelessness services, and reductions in taxes that punish work and building. Otherwise the public captures rent and converts it into fog.

Magie drew a Chance card from nowhere.

It said: PAY SCHOOL TAX. FEEL CIVILIZED. NOTICE ROOF LEAKING.

"What would you fund first?" she asked.

"Housing and transit."

"Too easy."

"Then assessments and administration. A tax that cannot be measured will become a lawsuit factory."

"Better."

"Then direct relief for renters and low-income homeowners during transition."

"Better still."

The resurrection had become less like a seance and more like a budget hearing, which is one of the traditional punishments for taking justice seriously.

The sixth objection is that a high land tax would reduce land prices, hurting existing owners. Yes. That is part of the point. If a tax captures future land rent, the private sale price of land should fall because buyers will not pay as much for an income stream the public now collects. Lower land prices can make development cheaper and reduce speculative hoarding. But this also means some current owners experience a capital loss. People who bought recently at inflated prices may be squeezed. Banks holding mortgages may care. Municipal finance may wobble if the transition is clumsy.

"So injustice has a balance sheet," Magie said.

"Always."

"And changing it creates losers."

"Always."

"Then the question is not whether there are losers. It is whether the old winners have acquired a veto by becoming accustomed to winning."

Exactly. Every serious reform disrupts expectations. The fact that someone expected to profit from rising site values forever is not a moral claim strong enough to defeat reform. But the fact that households organized their lives around existing law is morally relevant. This is why gradual implementation is not cowardice. It is justice with shock absorbers. Tax land more. Tax buildings less. Announce clearly. Protect vulnerable owner-occupants. Let future buyers price the tax in. Do not perform revolution by surprise and call the rubble evidence of seriousness.

Magie turned the board. The cemetery became Atlantic City, then Washington, then San Francisco, then Los Angeles, then a thousand smaller towns where the same argument wears local shoes.

"Say the controversial sentence," she said.

"Most cities should move decisively toward land value taxation."

"Decisively is another adverb."

"Fine. Cities and states should replace a large share of conventional property taxes, transfer taxes, and some local business taxes with a high, regularly assessed tax on land value, phased in over time, paired with zoning reform, tenant protections, and targeted relief for low-income owner-occupants. They should use the revenue to fund housing, transit, infrastructure, and reductions in taxes on labor and improvements. They should not pretend this alone will solve housing."

She considered this.

"Better. Still timid."

"You were dead before homeowners' associations perfected procedural warfare."

"I was alive when men told women property made them rational."

"Fair."

The debate sharpened there. Magie had lived in a country where women's economic dependence was dressed up as moral order. She once used a newspaper stunt advertising herself as a "young woman American slave" to attack women's lack of economic freedom, according to accounts of her life ([National Women's History Museum, "Monopoly's Lost Female Inventor"](https://www.womenshistory.org/articles/monopolys-lost-female-inventor)). Her board game was not only about lots and rents. It was about the larger fact that economic systems train people to call domination normal if the paperwork is tidy.

That is why Monopoly's cultural afterlife is so obscene and so perfect. A game invented to teach the cruelty of land monopoly became a game where children learn to enjoy bankrupting one another. The Smithsonian notes that Magie hoped to publicize Henry George's reforms, while the commercial version that triumphed made the monopolist mode definitive ([Smithsonian](https://americanhistory.si.edu/explore/stories/playing-monopoly-and-its-discontents-its-80th-anniversary)). America took a critique, sanded off the accusation, sold the remainder, and then made everyone fight over the shoe.

"They did not misunderstand the game," Magie said. "They chose the rule set they liked."

That may be too harsh, but not by much. Monopoly is unbearable because it makes chance, accumulation, and exclusion feel like merit. You roll well, buy early, collect rent, build hotels, bankrupt friends, and then explain afterward that you had a strategy. The game is funny because it is compressed. The real board takes decades. The pieces are families. Nobody gets to flip the table without being called irresponsible.

Land value taxation is controversial because it reopens the most basic square: why does anyone get to charge everyone else for standing in a place made valuable by everybody? The answer cannot be "because title exists." Title is law, not theology. The answer cannot be "because ownership is theft," either. That is a bonfire pretending to be a policy. The answer has to distinguish possession from tribute, improvement from enclosure, home from hoard, security from speculation, rent for service from rent for permission.

Magie put one green house on her own grave.

"Would you abolish landlords?"

"No."

"Disappointing."

"I would abolish the landlord's free lunch."

"Better."

"I would make owning valuable land expensive unless the owner puts it to good use or pays the public for the privilege of withholding it."

"Better still."

"I would protect tenants from being fed into the machine during the adjustment."

"Necessary."

"I would keep private homes but stop pretending land appreciation is a divine reward for having arrived first with financing."

"Now you are awake."

The hardest case remained the small owner whose land rises through no fault of her own. Is she unjust because society improved around her? Not necessarily. She did not ask the new station to arrive, the tech office to open, the school district to become desirable, the zoning map to change, the cafe to appear, the neighbors to organize a park, the city to become a magnet. But she also did not create those values alone. A land tax says: keep your house; keep the value of your maintenance and care; but the social part of the site value belongs partly back to the society that made it. If your income is too low to pay now, defer. If you are sitting on ten empty parcels near transit, do not come to the hearing wearing someone else's grandmother as a hat.

Magie laughed. It was a dry little click, like dice in a cup.

"And renters?"

"Renters need more than the joy of watching landowners taxed."

"Good."

They need actual homes. They need vacancy. They need legal apartments in rich neighborhoods. They need public and nonprofit housing that does not depend on every project being a financial sculpture. They need eviction defense, wage growth, mobility, and schools not funded in ways that turn municipal boundaries into velvet ropes. A land tax can help by lowering the private reward for hoarding sites and by funding public goods. It cannot tuck a child into bed. The unit must exist.

The cemetery light had started to change. The board's streets looked less like Atlantic City and more like a diagram of appetite.

"What would you tell the anti-tax owner?" she asked.

That I understand why they are afraid. Property is not just an asset. It is memory, control, escape from landlords, proof of competence, the only wealth many families have, and sometimes the one thing shielding a person from the market's open mouth. A reformer who sneers at that fear is not radical. He is stupid. But fear cannot be allowed to freeze a whole city into inheritance machinery. The young cannot live inside the unrealized capital gains of the old. Workers cannot commute two hours because vacant land has excellent portfolio discipline. Tenants cannot be told that the sanctity of ownership requires them to spend half their income buying someone else's patience.

"And what would you tell the revolutionary?"

That hating rent is not enough. Learn assessment law. Learn zoning. Learn municipal finance. Learn who actually owns which parcels. Learn how appeals work. Learn why a church parking lot is not the same political object as a speculative downtown lot. Learn how to protect poor owners without giving rich owners a loophole wide enough to drive a condo tower through. Learn that slogans do not appraise land. If you cannot administer justice, someone else will administer your slogan.

Magie leaned over the board, suddenly tired.

"They made my warning into recreation," she said.

"Yes."

"Can you make recreation back into warning?"

"Sometimes."

"Then write this: the board was never the lesson. The rent was."

So here is the answer I would commit to in daylight: **yes, land value taxation should become a central housing and public-finance reform, especially in high-demand cities where location value is created by collective life and captured privately. But it should be implemented as a serious tax shift, not a purity ritual: phased in, regularly assessed, paired with lower taxes on improvements, legal housing abundance, tenant protections, hardship deferrals, transparent appeals, and visible public use of revenue. It is not sufficient. It is still necessary.**

The last word matters. Necessary does not mean magical. It means a housing politics that refuses to tax land rent is leaving one of the oldest engines of inequality purring under the floorboards. We can build more and still ask who captures the value of the built city. We can protect homeowners and still deny speculative owners a veto. We can respect property and still remember Locke's proviso. We can use markets and still tax economic rent. We can play the game without pretending the rules descended from heaven.

Magie began to fade with the board half folded in her lap.

"Do they still let children win by bankrupting their friends?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Then there is hope."

"That seems backwards."

"No. A child who has enjoyed cruelty in miniature can still learn to recognize it in law."

The hotel dissolved first. Then the dice. Then the thimble, which left a small dent in the grass where WAGES had been. Magie sank back into the grave with the satisfaction of a teacher who knows the class is probably doomed but has at least assigned the correct reading.

I packed up the board. Around the cemetery, Arlington land continued appreciating with perfect theological indifference. Roads, schools, jobs, trees, Metro stations, federal buildings, restaurants, neighbors, and public peace all breathed value into the ground, and the ground, being land, did not say thank you.

That is the whole argument in one impolite fact. Land receives. The city gives. Rent translates receiving into power. A decent tax system should be suspicious of that translation. It should not punish the house for standing. It should ask the ground what it has been collecting.
